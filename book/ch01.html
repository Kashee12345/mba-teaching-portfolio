The Positive Lens on Organizational Behavior

Organizational behavior gives you a way to see why people at work act as they do. This chapter shows you how to look at the same workplace through two lenses, one that fixes what is broken and one that learns from what is already working, and how to hold both to the standard of good evidence.

Learning objectives

  • Explain what organizational behavior studies and why it matters to people who lead clinics, studios, teams and small businesses.
  • Distinguish the individual, group and organizational levels of analysis and diagnose which level a workplace problem mainly lives at.
  • Apply the four sources of evidence used in evidence-based management to a real decision in your own workplace.
  • Evaluate the positive organizational scholarship and positive deviance approaches, including their strengths and their honest limits.

Two physical therapy clinics sit fifteen minutes apart in the same mid-sized city. They share an owner, use the same scheduling software, pay the same wages, and treat patients with the same insurance mix. Yet one clinic has a patient no-show rate of about 6 percent, and the other runs close to 18 percent. For months the owner, whom we will call Dana, treated the higher number as a problem to be fixed. She sent reminder texts, tightened the cancellation policy, and added a fee. The rate barely moved. Then a new operations lead asked a different question: not "what is wrong at the struggling clinic?" but "what exactly are people at the other clinic doing that we have never written down?" Within two weeks of watching and listening, the answer surfaced. At the stronger clinic, the front desk staff ended every visit by booking the next appointment with the patient while the patient was still standing there, and the therapist walked the patient to the desk and said, out loud, why the next session mattered. Nobody had designed this. It was simply how that team worked. This chapter is about the two questions Dana's business asked, and why a capable leader needs both of them.

What organizational behavior is for

Every workplace is a set of people trying to get something done together. Some days it works beautifully. Other days the same people, with the same skills, get in each other's way. Organizational behavior, usually shortened to OB, is the field that studies what people think, feel and do in organizations, and how those patterns affect the results the organization cares about. Put plainly, OB tries to answer three questions: why do people at work behave the way they do, what happens because of it, and what can a leader actually change?

OB borrows from several older disciplines. Psychology contributes what we know about personality, perception, motivation and emotion. Social psychology contributes what we know about groups, influence and conformity. Sociology contributes what we know about structure, status and culture. Economics contributes ideas about incentives and trade-offs, and anthropology contributes careful ways of watching how people live inside a group. What makes OB its own field is that it pulls these threads together around one practical setting: the workplace.

The field has an interesting origin story that is worth knowing because it teaches a lesson about evidence. In the 1920s and early 1930s, researchers at the Hawthorne Works of the Western Electric Company near Chicago set out to test whether brighter lighting made factory workers more productive. Output seemed to rise whether lighting went up or down, and researchers concluded that social factors, such as supervisor attention and group norms, mattered more than physical conditions. The phrase "Hawthorne effect" came to mean people changing their behavior because they know they are being watched. Later reanalyses found the effects smaller and less consistent than the textbook version claims. Both halves of that history are useful: the studies turned attention toward the human side of work, and the reanalysis reminds us that a famous story is not the same thing as strong evidence.

Outcomes OB cares about

OB researchers usually track a small set of outcomes. The first is performance: how well people do the core tasks of their role, such as treating patients, closing sales or coaching athletes. The second is citizenship behavior: the helpful extras that no job description lists, such as covering for a colleague or training a new hire informally. The third is withdrawal: lateness, absence and, eventually, turnover. The fourth is attitudes and wellbeing: job satisfaction, commitment, stress and burnout. In health and wellness settings a fifth outcome matters enormously: the quality and safety of the service the customer or patient receives. These outcomes are linked. A physiotherapist who is burned out is more likely to leave, and the clinic that loses her pays for recruiting, onboarding and a dip in patient continuity. Seeing those links is one of the main things OB helps you do.

Why this matters if you run something small

It is tempting to think OB is for large corporations with human resources departments. In practice, the smaller the organization, the more each person's behavior shows up in the results. In a five-person yoga studio, one instructor who shows up late and unprepared can shift the whole atmosphere, and there is no layer of managers to absorb the effect. Owners and managers in small settings make OB decisions every day, often without naming them: who to hire, how to give feedback, how to split up tasks, how to respond when someone makes a mistake. The goal of this book is to make those everyday decisions more deliberate and better informed.

Three levels of analysis

When something goes wrong or right at work, the first useful question is: where does it mainly live? OB answers with three levels of analysis. Think of them as zoom settings on a camera. At the closest zoom you see individuals. Pull back and you see groups and teams. Pull back again and you see the whole organization with its structure, culture and systems. Many problems show up at one level but are caused at another, so learning to switch zoom settings is a core leadership skill.

The individual level

At the individual, or micro, level you ask about the person: their personality, abilities, values, perceptions, emotions and motivation. Chapter 2 and Chapter 3 live mostly here. If a new massage therapist is technically skilled but struggles to talk comfortably with anxious clients, that is largely an individual-level question about skills and confidence, and coaching the person directly is a sensible response.

The group level

At the group, or meso, level you look at how people interact: the roles they take, the unwritten norms they follow, how they handle disagreement and whether they trust each other enough to admit mistakes. Chapters 4 and 5 focus here. If every new hire on the evening shift of a fitness center starts arriving ten minutes late within a month, the cause is probably not a string of unrelated character flaws. It is more likely a group norm that teaches newcomers what "on time" really means on that shift.

The organizational level

At the organizational, or macro, level you examine structure, culture, strategy, policies and systems such as pay, scheduling and promotion. Chapters 7 and 8 work at this level. If nurses across every unit of a hospital report skipping breaks, the root cause may be a staffing model or a documentation system, not a lack of personal resilience. Sending those nurses to a stress-management workshop, an individual-level fix, would miss the point.

Diagnosing the right level

A common and costly error is to treat a group or system problem as if it were an individual one. Psychologists have a name for our tendency to explain other people's behavior by their personality while underestimating the situation around them; you will meet it in Chapter 2 as the fundamental attribution error. Leaders fall into it constantly. The practical habit to build is simple: before you decide who to coach or correct, ask whether several different people in the same spot would probably behave the same way. If the answer is yes, look up a level.

Evidence-based management

Managers are flooded with advice. Books, podcasts, consultants and social media posts all promise the secret to engagement, productivity or culture. Some of that advice is sound, some is harmless, and some is confidently wrong. Evidence-based management is a disciplined way of sorting it. In plain language, it means making decisions by combining the best available evidence from several sources, thinking critically about how trustworthy each source is, and then checking whether the decision actually worked.

The idea borrows directly from medicine. In the 1990s, clinicians led by David Sackett and colleagues described evidence-based medicine as the conscientious use of current best evidence, combined with clinical expertise and patient values, in decisions about individual patients. Many readers of this book work in health and wellness and will recognize the model. In 2006 Denise Rousseau, then president of the Academy of Management, asked whether there could be such a thing as evidence-based management, and in the same year Jeffrey Pfeffer and Robert Sutton published a widely read book arguing that managers routinely rely on hunches, imitation of other firms and outdated beliefs instead of facts. Later work by Eric Barends, Rousseau and Rob Briner in 2014 set out a practical framework built on four sources of evidence.

The four sources

Scientific research. Published studies, and especially summaries of many studies called meta-analyses, tell you what tends to happen across many organizations. Research is powerful because it is systematic and public, but it tells you about averages and may come from settings unlike yours.

Organizational data. Your own numbers, such as patient retention, membership cancellations, overtime hours, online reviews or exit interview themes, tell you what is happening in your specific setting. Data are only as good as how they were collected, and small samples can mislead.

Professional expertise. The accumulated, reflective judgment of experienced practitioners is real evidence. A clinic manager who has watched hundreds of patient check-ins knows things no survey will capture. Expertise is weakest when people rarely get clear feedback on whether their judgments were right.

Stakeholder values and concerns. The people affected by a decision, including staff, patients, clients, athletes and owners, have values that should shape what counts as a good outcome. A scheduling change that saves money but makes parents unable to pick up their children will not stick, however efficient it looks on paper.

Asking better questions of evidence

You do not need to be a researcher to use evidence well, only a few habits. Ask how the claim was tested: a survey from one gym tells you less than a study comparing many gyms over time. Ask whether there is another explanation: if teams that hold daily huddles have fewer errors, perhaps better-run teams are simply more likely to hold huddles. Ask how big the effect is, since a statistically significant result can be too small to matter. And ask whether the setting resembles yours.

What the evidence says

Pfeffer and Sutton's review of common management practices found that many popular beliefs, such as the idea that forced ranking of employees reliably raises performance, rest on weak or mixed evidence, while less glamorous practices, such as careful selection and clear goals, have much stronger support. The broader lesson from the evidence-based management literature is not that research has all the answers. It is that combining several imperfect sources and then measuring what happens beats relying on any single source, especially confident intuition or the example of a famous company.

Common mistake

Copying a practice because a successful company uses it. This is sometimes called "best practice" thinking, and it skips the question of why the practice worked there. A boutique fitness chain that copies a tech company's unlimited vacation policy may find that staff take less leave, not more, because nobody knows what is acceptable. Ask what problem the practice solved, what conditions it relied on, and whether those conditions exist in your setting.

Positive organizational scholarship

Most management thinking starts with problems. Absence is too high, errors are rising, a team is fighting. This is sensible: problems demand attention, and fixing them matters. But a purely problem-focused view has a blind spot. When you remove a problem, you return to normal. You do not automatically reach excellent. A clinic that eliminates its billing errors is now a clinic without billing errors, which is good, but it is not yet a clinic where patients rave about their care and staff compete to work there.

Positive organizational scholarship, often shortened to POS, is a research movement that studies the other end of the range: what enables individuals, teams and organizations to perform far above normal and to flourish. The term was introduced by Kim Cameron, Jane Dutton and Robert Quinn at the University of Michigan in a 2003 edited volume. It grew from the positive psychology movement, which Martin Seligman and Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi launched in a 2000 special issue of the American Psychologist, arguing that psychology had learned a great deal about illness and much less about what makes life go well.

A close cousin is positive organizational behavior, a term Fred Luthans introduced around 2002 for measurable, developable strengths in individual employees. His best-known concept is psychological capital, or PsyCap: hope, efficacy (confidence in one's ability to succeed), resilience and optimism. Research by Luthans and colleagues suggests PsyCap can be developed through short training and is associated with performance and satisfaction, though many studies rely on self-reports collected at one point in time.

The deviance continuum

Cameron offers a simple picture that makes the idea concrete. Imagine a line. In the middle sits "normal": healthy, effective, reliable, ethical. On the left sits negative deviance: illness, ineffectiveness, errors, unethical conduct. On the right sits positive deviance: vitality, excellence, extraordinary service, virtue. Most management tools are designed to close the deficit gap, moving from the left side to the middle. Positive organizational scholarship asks what closes the abundance gap, moving from the middle to the right side.

Key ideas from the positive lens

The heliotropic effect. Cameron borrowed a term from biology: plants turn toward the sun. He argued that people and systems tend to move toward positive energy and away from negative energy. Colleagues seek out people who leave them feeling energized. The metaphor is intuitive, but it is a metaphor, and the research it points to is mostly about specific mechanisms, such as the energizing relationships studied by Wayne Baker and colleagues, rather than a single universal law.

Broaden-and-build. Barbara Fredrickson's broaden-and-build theory, developed in the late 1990s and early 2000s, proposes that positive emotions such as interest, joy and gratitude temporarily widen the range of thoughts and actions people consider, and that over time these moments build lasting resources such as skills and relationships. Negative emotions, by contrast, narrow attention toward a specific threat, which is useful in a crisis but limiting for creative problem-solving. Laboratory studies generally support the broadening effect, though sizes vary and the longer-term "building" claims are harder to test.

Positive practices. Cameron and colleagues, in a 2011 study published in the Journal of Applied Behavioral Science, measured practices such as caring for colleagues, supporting one another, forgiving mistakes, inspiring one another, emphasizing meaning and treating one another with respect and integrity. In samples from financial services and health care, organizations and units that reported more of these practices tended to show better subsequent outcomes, including in some cases indicators of care quality. These are promising associations, and they fit with other research on climate and trust, but they are not controlled experiments. Chapter 7 returns to these practices in detail.

Case

Harbor Point Sports Medicine, a fictional twelve-clinician practice, ran a staff survey that showed middling engagement scores. The first response was a list of fixes: a new break room, a pay review, a complaint box. Useful, but the scores barely changed. The practice manager then tried a second approach. At each monthly meeting, one clinician told a short story about a recent visit that had gone unusually well and the team discussed what had made it work. Within a few months, a pattern appeared: the best visits almost always included a clinician explaining the "why" of the treatment plan in the patient's own words. The practice began coaching that habit explicitly. The fixes from the first approach still mattered. The second approach found something the first approach never would have.

Positive deviance

The phrase positive deviance has two related meanings, and it helps to keep them apart. In the broad POS sense described above, it means performance or behavior far above the norm. In a second, more specific sense, it names a practical change method: finding the people in a community or organization who already succeed against the odds, understanding what they do differently, and helping others adopt it.

The method grew out of nutrition research in the 1980s and was made famous by Jerry and Monique Sternin, who worked for Save the Children in Vietnam in the early 1990s. Child malnutrition was widespread in the villages they visited, and outside experts had long blamed poverty, sanitation and lack of education, all real problems that were too big to fix quickly. The Sternins asked villagers a different question: were there very poor families whose children were nonetheless well nourished? There were. Careful observation showed that these families did several small, unusual things. They added tiny shrimp, crabs and sweet potato greens, foods many neighbors considered unsuitable for children, to the rice. They fed children several smaller meals a day rather than two large ones. They washed children's hands before meals. Because these practices came from within the community, using resources everyone had, other families could adopt them. The program reported large reductions in malnutrition, and the approach spread to many other countries and problems. The story is told in detail in The Power of Positive Deviance by Richard Pascale, Jerry Sternin and Monique Sternin (2010).

Organizational scholars Gretchen Spreitzer and Scott Sonenshein, writing in 2004, offered a careful definition that works well for workplaces: positive deviance is intentional behavior that departs from the norms of a reference group in honorable ways. Each part of that definition matters. The behavior is intentional, not luck. It departs from the norm, which means it is genuinely unusual. And it is honorable, meaning it would be judged as good, not merely as clever or profitable.

How positive deviance works in organizations

In health care, the method has been used to tackle hospital-acquired infections. In the mid-2000s several US hospitals applied a positive deviance approach to the spread of drug-resistant staph infections (MRSA). Rather than issuing new rules from above, facilitators asked frontline staff, including cleaners, transport workers and nurses, who already had clever ways of preventing spread, and those staff shared and spread their own solutions. Participating hospitals reported meaningful declines in infection rates. Because these efforts ran alongside other infection-control measures, it is hard to say how much of the improvement came from the positive deviance process itself, and later reviews of positive deviance in health care have called for more rigorous evaluation. Still, the approach has a clear logic: solutions discovered by peers, using resources peers already have, face less resistance than solutions imposed by outsiders.

You can run a small version of this inquiry in almost any organization. Start by defining an outcome you care about and can measure, such as patient rebooking, member renewal or on-time session starts. Then look at your data for the people or teams who are doing unusually well while facing the same constraints as everyone else. The words "same constraints" matter: a trainer with the most renewals might simply have the easiest clients, and that is not positive deviance. Once you find genuine outliers, watch and ask what they do. Often the practices are small, invisible and taken for granted by the people who do them, as with Dana's front desk in the opening story. Finally, invite the rest of the team to try the practices and measure whether results change.

Try it this week

Pick one number that matters in your workplace and is recorded somewhere, such as rebooking rate, customer complaints, on-time starts or late documentation. Find the person, shift or location that does best on that number while facing similar conditions. Spend thirty minutes observing or talking with them and ask: "What do you do that you think other people might not?" Write down three specific behaviors. Resist the urge to turn them into a rule yet. Just notice how small and practical they are.

Limits and critiques of the positive lens

Any approach worth using deserves honest scrutiny, and the positive lens has attracted thoughtful critics. Knowing these criticisms will make you a better, not a more cynical, user of the ideas.

Bad is stronger than good

In a widely cited 2001 review, Roy Baumeister and colleagues concluded that across many areas of psychology, bad events, emotions and feedback have larger and more lasting effects than good ones of the same size. A single rude comment from a manager can outweigh several compliments. This does not undercut positive approaches, but it warns against ignoring the negative. Removing a toxic behavior may do more for a team than adding a new positive ritual. The wise leader closes the deficit gap and works on the abundance gap.

The positivity ratio controversy

In 2005 Barbara Fredrickson and Marcial Losada published a paper claiming that people and teams flourish when their ratio of positive to negative emotions exceeds a precise figure, roughly 2.9 to 1, derived from mathematical models of fluid dynamics. The claim spread widely in popular books and training programs. In 2013 Nicholas Brown, Alan Sokal and Harris Friedman published a critique showing that the mathematical basis for the precise ratio was invalid, and the journal issued a formal partial retraction of the modeling. Fredrickson accepted that the specific number should be set aside while maintaining that, broadly, more positive emotion relative to negative is beneficial up to a point. The lesson for managers is pointed. A precise, memorable number attached to a human behavior should make you more skeptical, not less.

Is "positive" a value judgment?

Stephen Fineman argued in a 2006 article in the Academy of Management Review that positive scholarship can drift into prescribing how people ought to feel, can overlook the value of negative emotions such as anger at injustice, and can serve management interests by encouraging employees to stay cheerful in difficult conditions. J. Richard Hackman, a leading team researcher, raised a related concern in 2009, warning that enthusiasm for positivity could lead scholars to accept weaker evidence for pleasing conclusions. Both critiques are fair. In practice, the healthiest reading of the positive lens is not "always be positive" but "study excellence as carefully as you study failure."

Toxic positivity at work

A related everyday risk is what people now call toxic positivity: pressure to look happy and grateful regardless of real conditions. A clinic that runs gratitude exercises while ignoring chronic understaffing sends a clear message that staff concerns are unwelcome. That undermines the trust and psychological safety that Chapter 4 will show are essential for learning and quality. Positive practices work when they sit on top of fair treatment and reasonable workloads, not in place of them.

Common mistake

Using positive language to shut down bad news. If a team member raises a safety concern and hears "let's stay positive," the team learns that problems should be hidden. A positive leader welcomes the concern, thanks the person for raising it and treats it as useful information. Positivity is about how you respond to problems, not about pretending they do not exist.

Selection and survivorship

Finally, studies of excellent organizations are vulnerable to survivorship bias: we study the firms that thrived and assume their practices caused it, forgetting the firms that used the same practices and failed. Positive deviance inquiry partly guards against this by comparing people under the same constraints and testing whether spreading a practice improves results.

Using both lenses: a working approach

By now you have two ways of looking at any workplace. The problem-solving lens asks what is going wrong and how to fix it. The positive lens asks what is already going well, sometimes exceptionally, and how to learn from it and spread it. Evidence-based management asks both lenses to show their evidence. Together they give you a practical approach you can use throughout this course.

A five-step habit

1. Name the outcome. Be specific about what you want to change and how you will know. "Better teamwork" is vague; "fewer handoff errors between the day and evening shift" is testable.

2. Choose the level. Ask whether the outcome mainly lives at the individual, group or organizational level, and check whether an individual-level story might really be a system-level problem.

3. Look both ways. Ask what is going wrong and what is going unusually right. Look for positive deviants: people or units that succeed despite the same constraints.

4. Gather the four kinds of evidence. What does research suggest, what do your data show, what do experienced people believe, and what do the affected stakeholders care about?

5. Act small and measure. Try a change on a small scale, measure the result against a baseline, and adjust. Treat your own management decisions as experiments.

Case

Ridgeline Climbing Gym, a fictional business with two locations and forty staff, faced rising member cancellations. The owner's first instinct was a problem-solving one: survey cancelling members, which revealed complaints about crowding at peak hours. That was useful. Using the positive lens, the manager also noticed that members who joined through the gym's beginner class series cancelled at about half the rate of other new members. Talking with the instructors who taught the series revealed that they introduced beginners to each other by name and suggested climbing partners. The gym did two things: it adjusted peak-hour route setting to spread climbers out, and it trained all front desk staff to introduce new members to at least one other member in their first week. Three months later, cancellations had fallen. The owner could not be sure which change mattered more, so the next quarter she tracked the two groups separately. That is evidence-based management in a small business: not perfection, but steady, honest learning.

Throughout the rest of this book, you will be asked again and again to look both ways, to ask for evidence and to think about the level at which a problem really lives. That is the core discipline of organizational behavior, and it is the foundation of leading with purpose.

Key takeaways

  • Organizational behavior studies what people think, feel and do at work, and how those patterns affect performance, citizenship, withdrawal, wellbeing and service quality.
  • Problems can live at the individual, group or organizational level; many leadership errors come from treating a system problem as a personal failing.
  • Evidence-based management combines scientific research, organizational data, professional expertise and stakeholder values, then checks whether the decision worked.
  • Positive organizational scholarship studies what enables excellence and flourishing, closing the abundance gap rather than only the deficit gap.
  • Positive deviance inquiry finds people who succeed under the same constraints as others, discovers their uncommon practices and helps peers adopt them.
  • Bad tends to be stronger than good, so positive practices complement problem-solving rather than replace it.
  • Be wary of precise, catchy numbers such as the retracted positivity ratio; test ideas in your own setting.
  • The strongest approach looks both ways, names the level, gathers evidence and treats decisions as small experiments.

Reflect and apply

  1. Think of a recurring frustration in your workplace. Write one sentence describing it at the individual level, one at the group level and one at the organizational level. Which description feels most accurate, and which would lead to the most useful action?
  2. Recall a recent decision your organization made, such as a new policy, tool or schedule. Which of the four sources of evidence were used, and which were missing? What would you have wanted to know?
  3. Identify one person, shift or location in your organization that does unusually well on an outcome you care about. What might they be doing that nobody has written down, and how could you find out respectfully?
  4. Where in your workplace might positive language be used, even unintentionally, to discourage people from raising concerns? What could you say differently the next time someone brings you bad news?
