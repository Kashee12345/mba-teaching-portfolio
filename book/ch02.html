Individual Differences, Strengths and Self-Awareness

People differ in personality, in how they read situations, in how they handle emotion and in what they do best. This chapter gives you evidence-based tools for understanding those differences in others, and the harder skill of seeing them clearly in yourself.

Learning objectives

  • Describe the Big Five personality traits and summarize what research says about their links to job performance and leadership.
  • Identify common perception and attribution errors, such as the fundamental attribution error and the halo effect, in everyday workplace judgments.
  • Critique the concept of emotional intelligence by comparing ability and mixed models and weighing the evidence for each.
  • Apply the VIA classification of character strengths and a feedback-based approach to build self-awareness in yourself and your team.

Marcus and Priya are both personal trainers at a busy fitness studio, and both are excellent. Marcus is loud, playful and always in motion. Clients leave his sessions laughing and a little exhausted. Priya is quiet and precise. She tracks every client's progress in a spreadsheet, notices when someone's form slips by a few degrees and sends a short message after each session. When the studio's new manager arrived, she assumed Priya was "less engaged" because she rarely spoke up in staff meetings, and she nearly gave the lead trainer role to Marcus on that basis alone. Then she looked at the retention data. Priya's clients stayed, on average, almost twice as long. The manager had made a very human mistake: she had read a difference in personality as a difference in commitment. This chapter is about seeing people, including yourself, more accurately.

Person and situation

Why does one employee thrive under pressure while another freezes? Why does the same person behave differently in two different teams? For most of the twentieth century psychologists argued about whether behavior is mainly driven by stable traits inside the person or by the situation around them. In 1968 Walter Mischel published an influential critique pointing out that personality measures often predicted behavior in specific situations only weakly. That sparked the so-called person and situation debate.

Today most researchers accept an interactionist view: behavior depends on both the person and the situation, and on how they fit together. Traits describe tendencies, not destinies. A useful refinement is the idea of situational strength. In a strong situation, such as a surgical time-out checklist or a strict safety protocol, rules and expectations are clear and most people behave similarly, so personality matters less. In a weak situation, such as an open-ended project with vague goals, people fill the space with their own tendencies, and personality matters more. Research by Reeshad Dalal, Rustin Meyer and colleagues around 2010 supported this pattern.

For leaders, this has a practical meaning. When you need consistency, such as in infection control, medication handling or safety procedures, you strengthen the situation with clear protocols. When you want initiative and creativity, you deliberately weaken the situation a little and let individual differences come through. Neither is better in general; each fits a purpose.

The Big Five personality traits

Personality is the relatively stable pattern of thinking, feeling and behaving that distinguishes one person from another. Hundreds of personality labels exist, but decades of research have converged on five broad dimensions, known as the Big Five or the five-factor model. The model emerged from two lines of work. One was the lexical approach, which assumed that important personality differences would be captured in everyday language; researchers such as Lewis Goldberg analyzed thousands of descriptive words and found they clustered into five groups. The other was questionnaire research, most prominently by Paul Costa and Robert McCrae in the 1980s and 1990s. The five dimensions are easy to remember with the acronym OCEAN.

The five traits in plain language

Openness to experience describes curiosity, imagination and comfort with new ideas. People high in openness enjoy variety and abstract thinking; people lower in openness prefer the familiar and the practical. Conscientiousness describes organization, dependability and persistence. High scorers plan, follow through and pay attention to detail. Extraversion describes sociability, assertiveness and energy drawn from social contact. Low scorers, often called introverts, are not shy by definition; they simply tend to prefer quieter settings and recharge alone. Agreeableness describes warmth, cooperation and trust. High scorers avoid conflict and put others first; low scorers are more willing to challenge and compete. Neuroticism, often reversed and called emotional stability, describes how strongly and how often a person experiences negative emotions such as anxiety and irritability.

What the research shows

What the evidence says

In a landmark 1991 meta-analysis, Murray Barrick and Michael Mount found that conscientiousness was the most consistent personality predictor of job performance across occupational groups. Later meta-analyses have generally confirmed this. A 2002 meta-analysis by Timothy Judge and colleagues found that extraversion was the trait most strongly linked to leadership emergence, meaning being seen as a leader, with conscientiousness and openness also contributing. Emotional stability is related to lower stress and to job satisfaction. These links are real but modest: correlations are typically in the range of about 0.1 to 0.3. Personality helps predict behavior across many people and situations, but it leaves much of any single person's performance unexplained.

Two further findings are worth knowing. First, personality is stable but not frozen. Longitudinal research by Brent Roberts and colleagues shows that, on average, people become more conscientious, more agreeable and more emotionally stable across adulthood, a pattern sometimes called the maturity principle. Second, more is not always better. Very high conscientiousness can shade into perfectionism and rigidity, and very high agreeableness can make it hard to deliver unwelcome news. In a physical therapy clinic, a highly agreeable therapist may struggle to tell a patient that they are not doing their home exercises; a highly conscientious manager may find it hard to delegate.

Common mistake

Using personality type labels to make hiring or promotion decisions. The Myers-Briggs Type Indicator is widely used in team workshops, but it sorts people into sixteen types based on dichotomies, even though the underlying traits are continuous and most people fall near the middle. Research has raised concerns about its test-retest reliability, since many people receive a different type when retested, and about its limited ability to predict job performance. It can be an enjoyable conversation starter. It should not be used to select or sort people. If you use any personality measure in hiring, use a validated Big Five based instrument, combine it with other evidence and check local legal requirements.

Some researchers argue for a sixth dimension. The HEXACO model, developed by Kibeom Lee and Michael Ashton, adds honesty-humility: sincerity, fairness and modesty. It has proven useful for predicting counterproductive behavior such as cheating or exploiting colleagues, which is relevant to Chapter 9 on ethics.

Perception and attribution

Before you can respond to anyone at work, you have to interpret what they did. Perception is the process of selecting, organizing and interpreting information to make sense of the world. It feels like simply seeing what is there, but it is actually an active construction shaped by attention, expectations and past experience. Two leaders can watch the same staff meeting and come away with different stories about who was engaged, who was difficult and what was decided.

Shortcuts that save time and cause errors

Because we cannot process everything, we use mental shortcuts. They are efficient and usually good enough, but they create predictable errors. Selective perception means noticing what fits our interests or expectations; a manager focused on costs may see only the expensive parts of a proposal. The halo effect, first described by Edward Thorndike in 1920, is the tendency to let one positive trait color judgments of everything else; a charming job candidate may be rated as more competent than the evidence supports. Its opposite, sometimes called the horn effect, lets one negative trait drag everything down. Stereotyping means judging someone based on the group we assign them to rather than on their own behavior. Contrast effects occur when our judgment of one person is shaped by the person evaluated just before; an average interviewee can look outstanding after a weak one.

Attribution theory

Attribution is the process of explaining why someone behaved as they did. Fritz Heider, writing in 1958, noted that people act like everyday scientists, sorting causes into two broad kinds: internal causes, which lie within the person, such as effort, ability or character, and external causes, which lie in the situation, such as workload, luck or unclear instructions. Harold Kelley, in 1967, proposed that we make these judgments by looking at three kinds of information, known as the covariation model.

Suppose a massage therapist's clients have complained about late starts three times this month. Consensus asks whether other therapists' sessions are also running late; distinctiveness asks whether this therapist runs late only on Tuesdays or everywhere; consistency asks whether it happens every week. If every therapist runs late on Tuesdays because that is the day the laundry service arrives and blocks the treatment rooms, the cause is the situation. If only this therapist runs late, on every day, the cause more likely lies with the person. Real people rarely gather all three kinds of information, which is why attribution errors are so common.

Attribution errors

The fundamental attribution error, a term coined by Lee Ross in 1977 and building on experiments by Edward Jones and Victor Harris in 1967, is the tendency to overestimate internal causes and underestimate situational causes when explaining other people's behavior. The front desk worker who is short with a patient is "rude," not exhausted after covering two absent colleagues. Research by Michael Morris and Kaiping Peng in 1994 suggested this bias is weaker in cultures that emphasize context and relationships, a reminder that even biases vary across settings.

The self-serving bias is the tendency to credit our successes to our own ability and effort while blaming our failures on circumstances. A studio owner may attribute a strong quarter to her marketing skill and a weak one to the economy. Leaders who fall into both biases at once are in a difficult position: they blame staff for failures and credit themselves for successes, which erodes trust quickly. A related idea, the actor-observer asymmetry, holds that we explain our own behavior situationally and others' dispositionally. It is intuitive, but a 2006 meta-analysis by Bertram Malle found the overall effect to be much weaker and less consistent than textbooks often suggest, a good example of a popular idea being tempered by evidence.

Case

At Lakeside Family Medicine, a fictional six-physician practice, the office manager noticed that one medical assistant, Jordan, took far longer to room patients than others. Her first explanation was internal: Jordan was slow. Before raising it, she checked the schedule. Jordan was the only assistant assigned to the physician who saw most of the practice's elderly patients with complex medication lists. When another assistant covered that schedule for a week, rooming times rose just as much. The manager changed the scheduling template to allow extra rooming time for complex visits and asked Jordan to share her medication reconciliation checklist, which turned out to be the most thorough in the practice. A situational cause had been hiding behind what looked like a personal shortcoming, and a strength had been hiding behind what looked like a weakness.

Emotions at work

For a long time management thinking treated emotions as noise: something to be controlled so that rational work could proceed. That view has changed. Emotions carry information, drive decisions and spread between people. It helps to separate emotions, which are intense, short-lived reactions to a specific event, from moods, which are longer-lasting, lower-intensity feelings that often lack a clear cause.

Affective events theory

Howard Weiss and Russell Cropanzano proposed affective events theory in 1996. In plain terms, the idea is that the small events of a workday, a compliment from a client, a rude email, a broken treadmill, a shift swap that falls through, trigger emotional reactions that accumulate and shape attitudes such as job satisfaction and behaviors such as helping or withdrawing. This matters because leaders often focus on big-ticket items like pay and promotion while overlooking the daily hassles and uplifts that shape how work actually feels. Fixing a scheduling system that causes weekly frustration may do more for morale than an annual bonus.

Emotional labor

Sociologist Arlie Hochschild introduced the term emotional labor in her 1983 book about flight attendants. It refers to managing one's emotional expression to meet the requirements of a job. A pediatric nurse stays calm and warm with a frightened child and an angry parent; a fitness instructor projects energy at a 6 a.m. class regardless of how she feels. Researchers distinguish two strategies. In surface acting, you display the required emotion without feeling it, like putting on a mask. In deep acting, you try to actually feel the required emotion, for example by reminding yourself of the patient's fear or reframing a difficult client's behavior. Studies, including work by Alicia Grandey, have generally found that heavy surface acting is linked to exhaustion and burnout, while deep acting is less harmful and sometimes linked to better customer outcomes. Organizations can help by acknowledging emotional labor openly, rotating the most demanding tasks and giving staff genuine breaks and backup.

Emotional contagion

Emotions spread. Emotional contagion, described by Elaine Hatfield and colleagues, is the process by which people catch the feelings of those around them, often by unconsciously mimicking facial expressions and tone. In a 2002 experiment, Sigal Barsade showed that a trained confederate's positive or negative mood spread through small groups and influenced how cooperatively they worked. Leaders are especially contagious because people watch them closely. A clinic director who arrives visibly stressed and snaps at the front desk may set the emotional tone for the whole morning.

Emotional intelligence and its critiques

Few ideas in management have become as popular as emotional intelligence, often shortened to EI or EQ. In plain language, it refers to how well a person can notice, understand and handle emotions, their own and other people's. The concept was introduced in academic form by Peter Salovey and John Mayer in 1990 and refined by Mayer and Salovey in 1997 into a four-branch model. Daniel Goleman's 1995 bestseller brought the idea to a mass audience and made bold claims about its importance, which is where much of the later controversy began.

Ability models and mixed models

The biggest source of confusion about EI is that the same label is used for quite different things. Ability models, like Mayer and Salovey's, treat EI as a form of intelligence: a set of skills that can be measured with performance tests that have better and worse answers, much as a reasoning test does. The best-known measure of this kind, the MSCEIT, asks people to identify emotions in faces and pictures and to judge the most effective ways of handling emotional situations. Mixed models, like those popularized by Goleman and by Reuven Bar-On, combine emotional abilities with a broad set of personality traits, motivations and social skills, such as optimism, self-confidence and adaptability, and are usually measured through self-report questionnaires or ratings by others.

What the critics say

EI has been one of the most debated concepts in organizational psychology. Edwin Locke argued in 2005 that the concept was defined so broadly that it had become almost meaningless, and that "intelligence" was the wrong word for what are really skills. Frank Landy raised concerns that early claims relied on commercial data not open to scientific scrutiny. John Antonakis and colleagues questioned claims that EI is essential for leadership. Popular claims that EI matters "twice as much as IQ" are not supported by the peer-reviewed evidence.

What the evidence says

Meta-analyses give a more balanced picture than either enthusiasts or critics. A 2010 meta-analysis by Dana Joseph and Daniel Newman found that EI predicts job performance mainly in jobs with high emotional labor, such as health care and customer service, and less so in other jobs. A 2011 meta-analysis by Ernest O'Boyle and colleagues found that EI adds some predictive power beyond cognitive ability and the Big Five, though the added amount is modest, and more so for mixed and self-report measures that overlap with personality. The fair summary: emotional skills matter at work, especially in people-intensive roles, but EI is not a magic ingredient, and how it is measured changes what it predicts.

For leaders in health, wellness and service businesses, the practical message is encouraging. The component skills, such as noticing a client's anxiety, calming yourself before a hard conversation and understanding why a colleague is frustrated, are valuable and can be practiced. What you should avoid is buying expensive EI assessments that promise to identify future stars or using a single EI score to make hiring decisions.

Character strengths

Much of the personality research above describes people in neutral terms. The positive psychology movement introduced in Chapter 1 asked a different question: what is best about people? In 2004 Christopher Peterson and Martin Seligman published Character Strengths and Virtues, a handbook that aimed to do for human strengths what diagnostic manuals had done for mental disorders. After reviewing philosophical and religious traditions across many cultures, they proposed six broad virtues that appear widely, and 24 more specific character strengths through which those virtues show up in daily life. The work is known as the VIA classification, named after the Values in Action Institute that supported it.

Signature strengths

Peterson and Seligman proposed that each person has a handful of signature strengths: strengths that feel authentic ("this is the real me"), that energize rather than drain the person when used, and that the person is eager to use. The practical claim is that people do better and feel better when their work allows them to use these strengths often. In a 2005 online study, Seligman and colleagues found that participants who identified their signature strengths and used one in a new way each day for a week reported higher happiness and fewer depressive symptoms for up to six months compared with a placebo exercise. Workplace research, including studies by Claudia Harzer and Willibald Ruch, has linked using four or more signature strengths at work with experiencing work as a calling and with higher job satisfaction.

Consider how this looks in practice. A chiropractic office manager high in fairness and prudence may be the natural person to redesign the policy for handling late cancellations. A physical therapy aide high in humor and kindness may be the best person to welcome nervous first-time patients. A coach high in love of learning may thrive when asked to bring new research to staff meetings. None of these assignments requires a change in job title; they require a leader who notices strengths and shapes tasks around them, a practice sometimes called job crafting when employees do it themselves.

Limits of the strengths approach

The VIA framework is useful, but honest users should know its limits. First, statistical analyses of the VIA survey have not neatly reproduced the six-virtue structure; many studies find that the 24 strengths group into fewer, differently shaped clusters, so the six virtues are better treated as a helpful organizing scheme than a proven structure. Second, most evidence about strengths comes from self-report measures and relatively short studies. Third, strengths can be overused. Adam Grant and Barry Schwartz, in a 2011 review, argued that many positive traits follow an inverted-U pattern: helpful up to a point, harmful beyond it. Honesty without kindness can become bluntness that wounds; perseverance without perspective can become stubbornness about a failing plan. Finally, a strengths focus must not become an excuse to ignore genuine skill gaps. A clinician who dislikes documentation still has to document accurately.

Common mistake

Treating a strengths profile as a fixed label. Telling a team member "you're a creativity person, not a details person" can limit their growth and give them permission to neglect important work. Use strengths as a starting point for conversation: where do you feel most energized, and how can we design more of your week around that while still covering what the role requires?

Building self-awareness

Everything in this chapter applies to you, the leader, as much as to the people you lead. You have a personality profile with its own advantages and costs. You make attribution errors. You catch and spread emotions. You have signature strengths you may overuse. Self-awareness is the ability to see yourself clearly: your traits, values, emotions, strengths and effects on others. It sounds simple, but most of us are less self-aware than we think.

Two kinds of self-awareness

Organizational psychologist Tasha Eurich, drawing on her team's research, distinguishes internal self-awareness, knowing your own values, feelings and reactions, from external self-awareness, knowing how other people see you. Her team reported that the two were largely unrelated: some people understand their inner world well but have little idea how they come across, while others are keenly aware of their image but unclear about what they value. Leaders need both. Eurich also suggests that asking yourself "what" questions, such as "what situations drain me?", tends to be more productive than endless "why" questions, which can lead to rumination and self-justification. This advice is plausible and consistent with research on rumination, though it comes largely from her own studies and deserves further testing.

Research on 360-degree feedback, in which people are rated by their managers, peers and direct reports as well as by themselves, supports the importance of external self-awareness. Studies by Leanne Atwater, Francis Yammarino and others found that leaders who rate themselves much higher than others rate them tend to be less effective, while leaders whose self-ratings roughly match others' ratings tend to perform better. Overrating yourself is not just embarrassing; it is associated with poorer leadership outcomes.

The Johari window

A simple tool for thinking about self-awareness is the Johari window, created by psychologists Joseph Luft and Harrington Ingham in 1955 and named by combining their first names. It crosses what you know about yourself with what others know about you, producing four areas.

The open area holds what both you and others know, such as your role and visible habits. The blind area holds what others see but you do not, such as a tendency to interrupt or a tone that sounds impatient when you are tired. The hidden area holds what you know but have not shared, such as uncertainty about a decision or a personal pressure. The unknown area holds what nobody yet sees, such as untested abilities. For leaders, the blind area is the most dangerous, because the people around you may be reluctant to point out your blind spots, especially if you hold power over them. Chapter 4 on psychological safety explains why.

Practical routes to greater self-awareness

Several practices have reasonable support. Structured feedback from several sources, such as a well-run 360-degree process, reduces blind spots, especially when followed by a development conversation rather than simply a report. Asking specific questions works better than asking "any feedback for me?": try "what is one thing I do in meetings that makes it harder for you to speak up?" Reflection routines, such as a brief written review after important conversations, help you notice patterns in your emotional reactions. Validated assessments of Big Five traits or character strengths can give you a vocabulary for discussing your tendencies, provided you treat the results as prompts rather than verdicts. Finally, watch your attributions. When you catch yourself thinking "she is just careless," ask the three Kelley questions before acting.

Case

Elena owns Summit Strength, a fictional strength-and-conditioning facility with eleven coaches. She saw herself as approachable and encouraging, and her own character strengths profile highlighted zest and perseverance. When she ran an anonymous feedback round for the first time, several coaches wrote that she was "hard to disagree with" and that meetings felt like pep rallies where problems were not welcome. Elena was stung. After a few days she recognized a pattern in her blind area: her zest, a genuine strength, was overwhelming quieter coaches, and her perseverance made her defend plans long after problems appeared. She began opening meetings by asking one coach to name a risk in the current plan, and she waited through the silence. Within two months, coaches were raising scheduling and safety concerns earlier. Her strengths had not changed. Her awareness of how they landed had.

Try it this week

Choose two colleagues who see you work regularly and who will be honest. Ask each one the same two questions: "What is one thing I do that helps you do your best work?" and "What is one thing I could do differently that would help you more?" Listen, thank them and do not explain or defend. Afterward, write down which answer surprised you most and which area of the Johari window it came from. Then choose one small behavior to try differently for the next two weeks.

Self-awareness is not a single breakthrough but an ongoing practice. The goal is not to become a different person. It is to see your tendencies clearly enough to use your strengths deliberately, manage your risks honestly and read other people more fairly. That sets up the next chapter, where we ask what actually motivates people, including you, to do their best work.

Key takeaways

  • Behavior reflects both the person and the situation; strong situations reduce the influence of personality, and weak situations increase it.
  • The Big Five (openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness and emotional stability) are continuous traits; conscientiousness is the most consistent personality predictor of job performance, but all effects are modest.
  • Type-based tools such as the Myers-Briggs are fine for conversation but should not be used for hiring or promotion.
  • Perception is an active construction; shortcuts such as the halo effect, stereotyping and contrast effects produce predictable errors.
  • The fundamental attribution error leads leaders to blame people for problems caused by situations; Kelley's consensus, distinctiveness and consistency questions help correct it.
  • Emotions spread and accumulate at work; heavy surface acting is linked to burnout in emotionally demanding jobs.
  • Emotional intelligence skills matter most in emotionally demanding roles, but the concept has been oversold, and ability and mixed models measure different things.
  • Character strengths offer a positive vocabulary for development, but strengths can be overused, and self-awareness, especially of how you affect others, requires deliberate feedback.

Self-check

Reflect and apply

  1. Think of a colleague whose working style differs sharply from yours. Using the Big Five, describe the difference in neutral terms. What advantages does their style bring to your team that yours does not?
  2. Recall a recent time you judged a coworker's behavior as a character flaw. Apply Kelley's three questions. Is there a situational explanation you did not consider at the time?
  3. Which roles in your organization carry the heaviest emotional labor? What is one change to scheduling, breaks or support that could reduce the need for surface acting?
  4. Identify two of your own signature strengths. Describe one situation where each serves your team well and one where overusing it might be creating a blind spot.
