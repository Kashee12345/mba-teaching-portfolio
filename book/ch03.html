Motivation, Engagement and the Science of Flourishing

Why people give their best effort, why a raise alone rarely buys it, and how a leader can build work that people want to throw themselves into.

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish content theories of motivation (Maslow, Herzberg, self-determination theory) from process theories (expectancy and goal-setting) and explain what each is good for.
  • Diagnose a motivation problem in your own workplace by checking autonomy, competence, relatedness, expectancy, instrumentality and valence.
  • Compare work engagement, job satisfaction and flourishing, including the PERMA model and the main criticisms of each.
  • Design a strengths-based feedback conversation and a goal that is specific, challenging and matched to the complexity of the task.

Marisol runs the front desk team at a busy outpatient physical therapy clinic. Last spring the owner, worried about turnover, gave every coordinator a two-dollar-an-hour raise. For about three weeks the mood lifted. Then the old patterns returned: missed callbacks, a shrug when the schedule broke down, one of her best people quietly updating a resume. The owner was baffled. "We pay better than anyone in town," he said. "What more do they want?" This chapter is about that question. The honest answer is that pay matters a great deal, but mostly in a particular way, and that the things that make people lean into their work are usually cheaper, harder and more interesting than a raise.

What motivation is, and why leaders get it wrong

In plain language, motivation is whatever gets you moving, points you in a direction and keeps you going when the work gets hard. Researchers formalize this as three parts: direction (what you choose to spend effort on), intensity (how hard you try) and persistence (how long you keep trying). Ruth Kanfer and others who reviewed decades of work motivation research use roughly this definition. It is useful because it reminds you that a "motivation problem" can live in any of the three. A massage therapist who works hard on the wrong thing has a direction problem. A trainer who starts every new program with energy but drops it after two weeks has a persistence problem.

A second plain distinction does a lot of work: intrinsic motivation means doing something because the activity itself is interesting or satisfying, and extrinsic motivation means doing it to get something separate from the activity, such as pay, praise or avoiding trouble. Most real work mixes both. A nurse may find patient teaching deeply satisfying and also need the paycheck. Leaders go wrong when they treat motivation as one dial they can turn with money, or when they assume that people who are not visibly excited simply lack drive. Usually, the conditions around the person explain far more than their character does. That is the fundamental attribution error from Chapter 2, showing up again.

The theories in this chapter fall into two broad families. Content theories ask what people need or want from work: Maslow's hierarchy, Herzberg's two factors and self-determination theory. Process theories ask how people decide where to put effort: expectancy theory and goal-setting theory. You need both. Content theories tell you what fuel is available. Process theories tell you how the engine uses it.

Needs and the two-factor idea

Maslow, briefly and honestly

Abraham Maslow proposed in 1943 that human needs form a hierarchy: physiological needs, then safety, then love and belonging, then esteem, and finally self-actualization, the drive to become what you are capable of becoming. His core claim was that lower needs, when unmet, dominate attention, and that higher needs become motivating once lower ones are reasonably satisfied. The pyramid drawing that appears in nearly every management textbook was added later by other writers; Maslow did not draw it himself.

The idea has lasting intuitive value. A medical assistant who cannot make rent is not going to be inspired by a talk about the clinic's mission. But the strict hierarchy has weak empirical support. A widely cited 1976 review by Wahba and Bridwell found little evidence that needs cluster into five neat levels or that people move up them in order. People pursue belonging and meaning even while financially insecure, and many seek esteem before they feel fully safe. Treat Maslow as a reminder to check the basics, not as a staircase you must climb in sequence.

Herzberg's two factors

In 1959, Frederick Herzberg, with Bernard Mausner and Barbara Snyderman, asked engineers and accountants to describe times they felt exceptionally good and exceptionally bad about their jobs. The good stories were mostly about achievement, recognition, the work itself, responsibility and growth. The bad stories were mostly about company policy, supervision, pay, working conditions and relationships with coworkers. Herzberg concluded that satisfaction and dissatisfaction are not two ends of one scale. They are two separate scales.

He called the first group motivators: when present, they create satisfaction and effort. He called the second group hygiene factors: when poor, they create dissatisfaction, but fixing them mostly brings people to neutral rather than to enthusiasm. The metaphor is public sanitation. Clean water does not make you healthy and strong; it just stops you from getting sick.

Return to Marisol's clinic. The raise was a hygiene fix. It likely did reduce one source of dissatisfaction, which matters, since underpaid people leave. But it did nothing about what her coordinators described when she finally asked: they never heard how patients did, they had no say in the scheduling system they wrestled with all day, and nobody noticed when they rescued a chaotic morning. Those are motivator gaps.

Herzberg's theory has real critics. His critical-incident method invites people to credit themselves for good events and blame the environment for bad ones, a pattern known as self-serving attribution, so the two-factor split may partly reflect how people tell stories rather than how motivation works. Later studies did not consistently replicate the clean separation, and pay in particular can act as a motivator when it signals recognition and achievement. Still, the practical lesson has held up well: removing irritants and building meaningful work are different jobs, and a leader who does only the first will get calm but not commitment.

Self-determination theory: autonomy, competence, relatedness

The most thoroughly researched content theory today is self-determination theory, developed by Edward Deci and Richard Ryan beginning in the 1970s and laid out in their 1985 book and a much-cited 2000 article. In plain terms, it says people have three basic psychological needs, and when work supports them, people bring more of themselves to it. The needs are:

  • Autonomy: feeling that you are the author of your actions, that you endorse what you are doing rather than being pushed. Autonomy is not the same as independence or working alone. You can feel autonomous while following a protocol you understand and agree with.
  • Competence: feeling effective, growing in skill, and being able to handle the challenges in front of you.
  • Relatedness: feeling connected to and cared about by others, and feeling that you matter to them.

The theory's most useful idea for managers is that extrinsic motivation is not one thing. Deci and Ryan describe a continuum based on how much a person has taken in, or internalized, the reason for an activity. At one end is amotivation: no real reason to act. Then comes external regulation, doing it only for a reward or to avoid punishment. Then introjected regulation, doing it to avoid guilt or protect your self-image ("I would feel like a bad employee if I did not"). Then identified regulation, doing it because you value the outcome ("Charting carefully protects my patients"). Then integrated regulation, where the value has become part of who you are. At the far end sits intrinsic motivation. Identified and integrated forms are still extrinsic, since the payoff is outside the task, but they are autonomous, and they behave much more like intrinsic motivation: people persist, do better quality work and feel better doing it.

What does autonomy support look like on a Tuesday morning? Research on autonomy-supportive managers, including field work by Deci, Connell and Ryan in the late 1980s, points to a short list: explain the reason behind requests, acknowledge that people may not like a task, offer meaningful choices where they exist, and use invitational rather than controlling language ("Could we try..." rather than "You must..."). None of these remove standards. A clinic can require a sterile technique checklist and still explain why each step matters, invite staff to suggest improvements and let them choose how to learn it.

What the evidence says

Can rewards reduce intrinsic motivation? A 1999 meta-analysis by Deci, Koestner and Ryan, combining 128 experiments, found that tangible rewards given for simply doing an interesting task, or for completing it, tended to reduce people's later free-choice interest in that task. Verbal praise, by contrast, tended to increase it. This is the "undermining effect." Judy Cameron and David Pierce, who had earlier reviewed similar studies, argued that the negative effects are narrower and easier to avoid than critics claim. Most of the experiments involved students and short puzzles, not paid employees in ongoing jobs. A fair reading for managers: rewards are not poison, but rewards that feel controlling, especially for work people already enjoy, can shift their reason for doing it from "I care about this" to "I am doing this for the bonus." Rewards that inform people about their competence, and that arrive as acknowledgment rather than as a lever, are much safer.

Expectancy and goals: how people decide where effort goes

Vroom's expectancy theory

Victor Vroom's 1964 book Work and Motivation proposed that people are motivated to put effort into something when three beliefs line up. Plainly: "If I try, can I do it?" "If I do it, will it actually lead to something?" and "Do I care about that something?" Vroom's terms are expectancy, the belief that effort leads to performance; instrumentality, the belief that performance leads to outcomes; and valence, how much the person values those outcomes. In the original model the three multiply, so if any one is near zero, motivation collapses no matter how strong the others are.

Expectancy theory is a superb diagnostic tool. Imagine a fitness studio that offers instructors a bonus for raising class retention. Instructor A believes she cannot influence retention because the schedule, not her teaching, drives attendance: low expectancy. Instructor B has heard about bonuses before that never got paid because the criteria changed mid-quarter: low instrumentality. Instructor C is saving for nothing in particular and cares more about getting the Saturday morning slot than about cash: low valence for money, high valence for scheduling. One incentive, three different failures, and three different fixes.

The research record is mixed on the theory's precise mathematics. A 1996 meta-analysis by Wendelien Van Eerde and Henk Thierry found that expectancy, instrumentality and valence each related to effort and performance, but that multiplying them together did not predict better than simply considering them separately. People are not calculators. The theory works best as a checklist of beliefs to explore, not as a formula.

Goal-setting theory

Edwin Locke and Gary Latham built one of the most robust findings in all of organizational psychology across roughly four decades of research, summarized in their 1990 book and a 2002 article in American Psychologist. The plain version: specific, difficult goals lead to higher performance than vague "do your best" goals or easy goals, as long as people are committed to the goal and have the ability to reach it. Goals work through four mechanisms: they direct attention toward what matters, they energize effort, they increase persistence, and they prompt people to search for better strategies.

Several conditions, called moderators, shape how well goals work:

  • Commitment. A goal nobody accepts is just a number. Commitment rises when people believe the goal matters and believe they can reach it (notice the overlap with expectancy theory).
  • Feedback. People need to know how they are tracking. Goals plus feedback outperform either alone.
  • Task complexity. For complex, unfamiliar tasks, a hard performance goal can backfire by pushing people to grind rather than learn. Research by Gerard Seijts and Latham found that setting learning goals, such as "identify three strategies for reducing no-shows," works better than outcome goals when people do not yet know how to do the task.
  • Ability and resources. A goal that exceeds what the system allows is demoralizing, not stretching.

You may know the acronym SMART (specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, time-bound). It came from a 1981 management article by George Doran, not from Locke and Latham, and it quietly softens their key finding: "achievable" can drift into "easy." Goal-setting research says goals should be difficult but attainable. Keep the stretch.

Common mistake

Setting a single, narrow, high-stakes numeric goal and walking away. In a 2009 article titled "Goals Gone Wild," Lisa Ordóñez and colleagues argued that goal-setting has side effects that enthusiasts downplay: narrow focus that neglects unmeasured parts of the job, unethical shortcuts when people are close to but short of a target, and reduced learning. Locke and Latham replied that these are problems of badly designed goals rather than goals as such. Both sides agree on the practical fix: pair outcome goals with quality and ethics guardrails, use learning goals for complex work, and talk about how the goal is being pursued, not only whether it is hit. A chiropractic office that sets a visits-per-day target without a patient-outcome check is inviting the wrong kind of creativity.

Engagement: bringing your whole self to work

Satisfaction asks, "Are you content with your job?" Engagement asks something more active: "Are you pouring energy into it?" A person can be satisfied and disengaged, comfortably coasting. A person can also be engaged and somewhat dissatisfied, working with intense energy while frustrated by a broken scheduling system.

William Kahn introduced the idea of personal engagement in 1990 after studying summer camp counselors and architecture firm employees. He described engagement as people harnessing their physical, cognitive and emotional selves in their work roles, and disengagement as withdrawing and defending the self. Kahn found three conditions that made engagement more likely: meaningfulness (is this worth investing in?), safety (can I show myself without fear of negative consequences?) and availability (do I have the physical and emotional resources right now?). His "safety" condition is an early form of the psychological safety you will study in Chapter 4.

A second influential definition comes from Wilmar Schaufeli, Arnold Bakker and colleagues around 2002. They define work engagement as a positive, fulfilling, work-related state of mind with three parts: vigor (high energy and resilience), dedication (a sense of significance, enthusiasm and pride) and absorption (being happily engrossed so that time passes quickly). They developed a widely used survey to measure these, and they positioned engagement as the positive counterpart of burnout.

The job demands-resources model

Why are some people engaged and others burned out in the same organization? The job demands-resources (JD-R) model, developed by Evangelia Demerouti, Arnold Bakker and colleagues from 2001 onward, offers a clear answer. Every job has demands, the parts that require sustained effort and cost energy, such as patient volume, emotional labor, time pressure and physical strain. Every job also has resources, the parts that help people achieve goals, reduce the cost of demands or support growth, such as supportive colleagues, feedback, autonomy, coaching and a clear role. High demands with low resources drive a health-impairment process toward exhaustion and burnout. Plentiful resources drive a motivational process toward engagement. Resources also buffer demands: a demanding shift feels very different when the team has your back.

The JD-R model gives leaders an action map. You often cannot reduce demands much, since the patients still need to be seen and the season still gets busy. But you can almost always add resources: a ten-minute huddle, a clearer handoff, a coach who observes and gives specific feedback, a say in how the schedule is built. Bakker and Demerouti also describe job crafting, where employees themselves reshape tasks, relationships and how they think about their work. Research by Amy Wrzesniewski and Jane Dutton on hospital cleaners who saw themselves as part of the healing team is the classic illustration.

How much does engagement matter for results? A frequently cited 2002 meta-analysis by James Harter, Frank Schmidt and Theodore Hayes, using Gallup data from thousands of business units, found that units with higher engagement scores tended to have better customer satisfaction, productivity, profit, safety and retention. Two cautions apply. First, these links are correlational and at the unit level; high performance can also raise engagement, not only the reverse. Second, commercial engagement surveys are sold products, and published figures such as "only a minority of workers are engaged" depend heavily on how each vendor defines and cuts its scale. Academic critics, including Allan Macey and Benjamin Schneider in 2008, have also asked whether engagement is truly distinct from older ideas such as job involvement, commitment and satisfaction. The concept is useful, but hold the numbers loosely.

Case

Harbor Sports Performance, a fictional strength and conditioning center serving high school and college athletes, had high coach turnover. An exit review showed that pay was middling but not the main complaint. Coaches described back-to-back sessions with no time to plan, no feedback from the head coach unless something went wrong, and no idea whether athletes actually improved once they left. Using the JD-R lens, the director kept the session load the same (a demand the business needed) but added three resources: a fifteen-minute planning block every half day, a monthly film review where each coach picked one session to discuss, and a simple dashboard of athlete performance gains that coaches could see. Within two quarters, two coaches who had been interviewing elsewhere stayed, and one began mentoring new hires. The director also raised pay modestly at the next budget cycle, which removed a remaining source of dissatisfaction. Notice the pattern: hygiene fixed, resources added, motivators made visible.

Flourishing and the PERMA model

Positive organizational scholarship, introduced in Chapter 1, asks not only how to prevent harm but how to help people and organizations thrive. The psychological idea at its center is flourishing: living within an optimal range of human functioning, feeling good and functioning well. Sociologist Corey Keyes described flourishing in 2002 as the presence of high emotional, psychological and social wellbeing, not merely the absence of mental illness. He found that many people without any diagnosable disorder were still "languishing," neither ill nor thriving.

Martin Seligman, a founder of positive psychology, offered a popular framework in his 2011 book Flourish. He proposed five elements of wellbeing, abbreviated PERMA:

  • Positive emotion: feeling joy, gratitude, interest, hope and contentment.
  • Engagement: being absorbed in activities that use your strengths, sometimes reaching what Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi called flow, a state of deep concentration where challenge and skill are well matched.
  • Relationships: having warm, supportive connections with others.
  • Meaning: belonging to and serving something bigger than yourself.
  • Accomplishment: pursuing and achieving goals, mastery and competence for their own sake.

For a leader, PERMA is a handy audit of what a workplace offers beyond a paycheck. Does a typical week at your yoga studio or dental practice include some moments of genuine positive emotion? Chances for staff to be absorbed in work that fits their strengths? Real connection among colleagues? A clear line from daily tasks to something that matters, such as patients walking without pain? Visible accomplishment? If several elements are missing, even well-paid staff may languish.

Notice how much PERMA overlaps with the earlier theories. Relationships mirrors relatedness in self-determination theory. Accomplishment mirrors competence and Herzberg's achievement. Engagement is, in part, the same vigor and absorption Schaufeli measures. That overlap is a strength when you are trying to see the big picture: several research traditions converge on similar ingredients.

The overlap is also a weakness, and critics have said so. A 2018 study by Fallon Goodman and colleagues found that PERMA scores correlated so highly with standard measures of subjective wellbeing (life satisfaction and positive emotion) that the five elements may not add much beyond them. Other scholars note that PERMA was proposed as a framework rather than derived from data, and that positive psychology as a movement has sometimes run ahead of its evidence, with some well-known interventions shrinking in effect when studied more rigorously. Barbara Ehrenreich and others have also warned that an emphasis on positivity can slide into blaming people for unhappiness caused by bad conditions. Use PERMA as a structured conversation, not as a scientific scorecard, and never as a substitute for fixing unfair workloads or pay.

Strengths-based feedback

All of these theories converge on one leadership behavior that you can practice immediately: how you give feedback. Feedback touches competence (self-determination), expectancy (can I do it?), goal-setting (am I on track?) and engagement (the JD-R model counts feedback as a key resource). It is also where many well-intentioned managers do damage.

Start with a sobering finding. In 1996, Avraham Kluger and Angelo DeNisi published a meta-analysis of more than 600 effect sizes on feedback interventions. On average feedback improved performance, but in roughly a third of cases it made performance worse. Their explanation was that feedback helps when it directs attention to the task and how to do it, and it hurts when it directs attention to the self: "Am I good enough? Does my boss think I am incompetent?" Feedback that threatens self-image drains energy into self-protection.

Strengths-based feedback is a response to that problem. In plain language, it means spending most of your feedback attention on what people do well, helping them understand why it works and how to use it more, and addressing weaknesses mainly where they block performance. Herman Aguinis, Ryan Gottfredson and Harry Joo laid out a practical version in 2012. Their core recommendations include: identify strengths in terms of behaviors and results, not personality labels; connect strengths to the goals of the team; when addressing weaknesses, focus on specific behaviors and knowledge gaps that can change; and make the conversation a two-way plan rather than a verdict. The idea connects to the character strengths you met in Chapter 2 and to Donald Clifton's long-running work at Gallup on talents.

Strengths-based does not mean praise-only. A physical therapist who is superb at building rapport but inconsistent at documentation still needs to hear about documentation, because documentation protects patients and the practice. The difference is in proportion and framing. You lead with what is working, make the gap concrete ("Three of last week's notes were missing the functional goal"), connect the fix to something the person already cares about ("Your patients' insurance approvals depend on it"), and invite them to use their strength to close the gap ("You are great at explaining things to patients. Could you walk them through their goals at the end of the visit and then chart that conversation?").

A four-step conversation

  1. Observe precisely. Describe a specific behavior and its effect: "When the new member froze on the rower, you slowed the whole group down and turned it into a teaching moment. She signed up for three more classes."
  2. Name the strength. Help the person see the pattern: "That is a real skill at reading people and adjusting on the fly."
  3. Extend it. Ask where else it could help: "Where else could we use that? Our intro sessions have the highest drop-off."
  4. Address the one gap that matters most. Keep it about the task, specific and forward-looking, and agree on a next step together.
Try it this week

Pick two people you supervise or work closely with. For each, write down one specific moment from the past two weeks when they did something well, the strength it showed and a new place they could apply it. Deliver both observations in person, in under two minutes each, with no "but" attached. Then, separately and later in the week, ask each person one expectancy question ("What gets in the way of doing your best work here?") and one valence question ("What part of this job do you most want more of?"). Note which link in the expectancy chain their answers point to.

Back at Marisol's clinic, the owner eventually tried a different approach. The coordinators started receiving a weekly note from therapists about a patient whose progress their scheduling made possible. Marisol gave the team authority to redesign the callback process and set a learning goal, not an outcome goal, for the first month: test three ways to reduce same-day cancellations. She began each one-on-one by naming something specific the person had done well. Within a quarter, cancellations were down and the coordinator who had been job hunting asked to lead the next process project. The raise mattered. It was just not the whole answer.

Key takeaways

  • Motivation has direction, intensity and persistence; a problem can sit in any of the three, and conditions usually explain more than character.
  • Maslow is a reminder to check the basics, not a strict ladder. Herzberg shows that removing dissatisfaction and building satisfaction are separate jobs.
  • Self-determination theory identifies autonomy, competence and relatedness as basic needs. Autonomous forms of extrinsic motivation behave much like intrinsic motivation.
  • Rewards that feel controlling can undermine interest in work people already enjoy; informational recognition is safer.
  • Expectancy theory is a diagnostic chain: can I do it, will it pay off, do I care? Fix the weakest link.
  • Specific, difficult goals with feedback raise performance; use learning goals for complex tasks and add guardrails against narrow, unethical pursuit.
  • Engagement (vigor, dedication, absorption) grows when job resources keep pace with demands; flourishing and PERMA widen the lens, though both carry measurement critiques.
  • Strengths-based feedback focuses on the task, leads with what works, and still addresses the gap that matters most.

Self-check

Reflect and apply

  1. Think of a time your own motivation at work dropped sharply. Which was weakest: autonomy, competence or relatedness? What would have restored it?
  2. List the rewards and incentives your organization uses. Which ones feel informational to employees, and which feel controlling? Is any of them rewarding work people already enjoyed?
  3. Choose one goal your team is currently working toward. Is it specific and challenging? Is the task complex enough that a learning goal would serve better? What guardrails protect quality and ethics?
  4. Map your team's main job demands and job resources. Which single resource could you add in the next month at little cost, and how would you know whether it helped?
