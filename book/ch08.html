Leading Change

Most change does not fail because the idea was bad. It stalls because people were not ready, their concerns were treated as obstacles, or the effort ran out of energy after launch. This chapter gives you the classic models, an honest view of their limits, and practical tools for building readiness and making change stick.

Learning objectives

  • Explain Lewin's field theory, force field analysis and the unfreeze, change, refreeze idea, including how scholars now question its origins.
  • Apply Kotter's eight steps and the five components of a readiness message to a change you are planning.
  • Interpret resistance to change as information about the change, its process and relationships, rather than as a personal defect.
  • Critique linear step models and design an iterative approach that uses psychological safety, motivation and small wins to sustain change.

On a Monday in March, the owner of a busy three-location sports medicine practice announced that the practice would switch to a new online scheduling and intake system in four weeks. The software was good. It would cut phone traffic, reduce double booking and let patients complete forms at home. By June, the front desk was still taking most bookings by phone, two clinicians had built private workarounds on paper, and the owner was calling the staff "resistant to change." When an outside facilitator interviewed the team, a different picture emerged. Nobody had asked the front desk how appointments actually flowed. Training had been a single video. Early glitches had embarrassed staff in front of patients, and when they raised them, they were told to "give it a chance." The staff were not against the change. They were against a change that made their jobs harder and their concerns invisible. This chapter is about leading change so that this story ends differently.

What change asks of people

In plain language, organizational change is any deliberate shift in how an organization works: its structure, technology, processes, roles, services or culture. Change ranges from small adjustments, such as a new intake form, to transformations, such as merging two clinics or moving a fitness brand from studio classes to a hybrid digital model. Karl Weick and Robert Quinn (1999) drew a useful distinction between episodic change, which is infrequent, intentional and often dramatic, and continuous change, which is ongoing, cumulative and emergent, the everyday adjustments people make as they learn. Most of the classic models in this chapter were built for episodic change. Many of the challenges you face as a leader are continuous.

Whatever the scale, change asks three things of people. It asks them to let go of routines that felt competent and safe. It asks them to tolerate a period of uncertainty and reduced performance while they learn. And it asks them to invest effort in something whose payoff is not yet visible. William Bridges (1991) captured the human side of this with his distinction between change, the external event, and transition, the internal psychological process. Transition begins with an ending, moves through a confusing "neutral zone," and only then reaches a new beginning. Leaders who plan the change but not the transition are often surprised that a technically successful rollout still feels like a failure.

What the evidence says

You may have heard that "70 percent of change initiatives fail." Mark Hughes (2011) traced this claim through the literature and found no valid, reliable empirical evidence behind it; the figure appears to have been repeated from consultancy estimates and loosely worded sources. That does not mean change is easy. It does mean you should be skeptical of dramatic statistics used to sell change programs, and that "success" and "failure" need to be defined before you can measure either.

Lewin's foundations: fields, forces and three steps

Almost every modern change model traces back, at least in name, to the social psychologist Kurt Lewin. Lewin's broader work, developed in the 1930s and 1940s, included field theory (the idea that behavior results from the whole field of forces acting on a person or group), research on group dynamics, and action research, a cycle of planning, acting, observing and reflecting in partnership with the people affected.

Force field analysis

Lewin suggested that any stable situation is a kind of balance, a "quasi-stationary equilibrium," held in place by forces pushing for change and forces pushing against it. In plain language: things stay the way they are because the pushes and the pulls cancel out. Force field analysis makes these forces visible. You list the driving forces that push toward the desired change and the restraining forces that hold the current situation in place, and you estimate their strength.

Lewin's key practical insight was that adding more driving force, such as more pressure, deadlines or incentives, tends to raise tension and provoke counterforce. Reducing restraining forces is often more effective. In the sports medicine example, the owner kept pushing (a firm deadline, reminders, frustration) instead of reducing the restraints: extra front desk cover during the first month, hands-on practice before go-live, and quick fixes for the glitches that embarrassed staff.

Unfreeze, change, refreeze

The model most often attributed to Lewin describes three phases. Unfreezing means disturbing the current equilibrium so people see that the present way is not adequate and feel safe enough to try something new. Changing (or moving) means learning and adopting new behaviors. Refreezing means stabilizing the new pattern through norms, systems and reinforcement so it does not slide back. Lewin also emphasized that group decisions tend to produce more durable change than individual persuasion, a finding from his wartime studies of food habits.

This three-step model has been criticized as too static for a world where the ice never really refreezes. It has also been reexamined historically. Stephen Cummings, Todd Bridgman and Kenneth Brown (2016) showed that Lewin wrote about the three steps only briefly, and that the tidy "unfreeze, change, refreeze" model was largely constructed and popularized by later writers. Bernard Burnes (2004) argued that Lewin's approach should be read as a whole, with field theory, group dynamics and action research, rather than as a three-box recipe. The fair summary: Lewin gave us durable ideas about forces, participation and learning, and a simple three-phase shorthand that is useful as long as you do not treat it as a full theory.

Kotter's eight steps

The most widely taught change model in business schools comes from John Kotter of Harvard Business School. In a 1995 Harvard Business Review article and his 1996 book Leading Change, Kotter drew on his observation of more than a hundred companies attempting transformation and described eight common errors. He turned their opposites into eight steps.

1. Establish a sense of urgency. Help people see why the status quo is more dangerous than change, using honest data and real stories. Kotter warned that complacency was the most common reason transformations stalled. 2. Form a guiding coalition. Assemble a group with enough credibility, expertise and relationships to lead the change, not just the most senior people. 3. Develop a vision and strategy. Describe a clear, desirable picture of the future and a sensible route to it. Kotter suggested that if you cannot explain the vision in about five minutes, it is not yet clear. 4. Communicate the vision. Communicate far more than feels necessary, through every channel, and above all through leaders' behavior. 5. Empower broad-based action. Remove obstacles such as structures, systems and supervisors that undercut the vision, and encourage people to try new ideas. 6. Generate short-term wins. Plan for visible improvements within months, and recognize the people who made them. 7. Consolidate gains and produce more change. Use the credibility of early wins to tackle bigger systems, and resist declaring victory too soon. 8. Anchor new approaches in the culture. Connect new behaviors explicitly to results, and make sure leadership succession and hiring reinforce them, echoing Schein's embedding mechanisms from Chapter 7.

Kotter's model is popular for good reasons. It is memorable, it covers both analytic and emotional sides of change, and many of its steps line up with other research: urgency relates to what readiness researchers call discrepancy, short-term wins relate to research on progress and self-efficacy, and anchoring relates to culture embedding. In his 2014 book Accelerate, Kotter revised the model into eight "accelerators" that run concurrently, and proposed a "dual operating system" in which a volunteer network works alongside the normal hierarchy to drive change. That revision partly answers the criticism that the original was too linear and top-down.

Kotter in a small organization

Kotter studied large corporations, but the steps translate to a ten-person clinic or a community sports club if you scale them sensibly. Urgency may be a single honest conversation about falling member renewals, using the club's own numbers rather than a dramatic presentation. The guiding coalition may be three people: the owner, the most respected clinician and the front desk lead, whose informal influence often exceeds their title. The vision may fit on an index card. Short-term wins may be as small as "no double bookings this week." What matters in a small setting is that the steps are not skipped because everyone "already knows." In small organizations, people often assume that because they talk every day, the reasons for a change are obvious. They rarely are, especially to the people whose routines will change most.

Readiness for change

Before people change what they do, they have to believe the change makes sense. In plain language, readiness for change is the degree to which people are both willing and able to take on a change. Achilles Armenakis, Stanley Harris and Kevin Mossholder (1993) described readiness as the cognitive precursor to either resistance or support, and argued that leaders should build it deliberately rather than assume it. In a later paper, Armenakis and Harris (2002) proposed that an effective change message addresses five beliefs.

Discrepancy is the belief that change is needed: there is a real gap between where we are and where we should be. Appropriateness is the belief that this particular change is the right response to that gap. Efficacy is the belief that we are capable of making it work, drawing on Bandura's concept of self-efficacy. Principal support is the belief that leaders and respected colleagues are genuinely committed, not simply announcing. Personal valence is the answer to "What is in it for me?", the belief that the change brings personal benefit or at least a fair balance of costs and benefits.

These five beliefs make an excellent checklist. In the opening story, the owner had made a strong case for discrepancy (phone chaos) and appropriateness (good software), but had done almost nothing for efficacy (one training video), principal support (the owner never used the system himself), or personal valence (the front desk's workload went up before it went down).

Bryan Weiner (2009), writing for health care implementation, offered a complementary theory of organizational readiness built on two shared beliefs: change commitment, a shared resolve to implement the change, and change efficacy, a shared belief in the group's collective ability to do it. Weiner emphasized that readiness is a team-level state, not just the sum of individual attitudes, and that it is strongest when people value the change for reasons they genuinely endorse. That point links directly to self-determination theory from Chapter 3: commitment built on autonomous motivation is sturdier than compliance built on pressure.

Try it this week

Take a change you are leading or about to lead. Write one sentence for each of the five readiness beliefs as your team would currently answer it, not as you would. Rate each from 1 (weak) to 5 (strong). Then ask two team members to do the same privately and compare. Pick the weakest pillar and design one concrete action to strengthen it, such as a hands-on practice session for efficacy or visible use of the new system by leaders for principal support.

Resistance as information

"Resistance to change" is one of the most common phrases in management and one of the least helpful. It usually implies that the problem lies in the people who are not changing. A generation of research suggests a more useful view: resistance is information.

Rethinking resistance

Jeffrey Ford, Laurie Ford and Angelo D'Amelio (2008) argued that the standard story, in which rational change agents meet irrational resisters, is one-sided. Change agents can contribute to resistance by breaking agreements, failing to restore trust, and communicating poorly. Labeling people as resistant can also be a self-serving way for change leaders to explain disappointing results. Most importantly, what looks like resistance can be a resource: it keeps a topic alive, surfaces problems, and increases engagement with the change.

Sandy Kay Piderit (2000) pointed out that responses to change are multidimensional: people have thoughts, feelings and intentions about a change, and these do not always agree. A nurse may think a new handoff protocol is sensible, feel anxious about using it, and intend to comply while privately doubting it will last. Piderit argued that ambivalence, holding positive and negative reactions at once, is common and can be productive, because ambivalent people are still engaged and open to influence.

People also differ in general disposition. Shaul Oreg (2003) developed a measure of dispositional resistance to change, which includes routine seeking, emotional reaction to imposed change, short-term focus and cognitive rigidity. Disposition matters, but it is only one part of the picture. A review of six decades of research on change recipients' reactions (Oreg, Vakola and Armenakis, 2011) found that how change is managed, including participation, communication, trust in management and perceived fairness, consistently shaped people's reactions.

Reading what resistance is telling you

When you encounter resistance, ask what it is telling you. It often signals one of four things. First, a flaw in the change: the people doing the work can see a problem the planners missed. Second, a flaw in the process: people were not consulted, timelines are unrealistic, training is thin. Third, a real loss: status, expertise, relationships or a way of working that gave meaning. Fourth, a relationship or trust problem: past changes were badly handled, so people discount this one. Each has a different response. A flawed change should be fixed. A flawed process should be repaired. A loss should be acknowledged honestly and, where possible, compensated or honored. A trust problem is repaired slowly, through keeping promises.

Fairness deserves special attention because it cuts across all four. People judge not only whether a change leaves them better or worse off, but whether the process was fair and whether they were treated with dignity along the way; Chapter 9 develops these ideas as distributive, procedural and interactional justice. Change recipients will often accept an outcome they dislike if they had a genuine voice, received an honest explanation, and saw the rules applied consistently. Conversely, a change that benefits most people can still provoke strong opposition if it is announced without consultation or if exceptions seem to favor insiders.

Case

Summit Youth Soccer Club (fictional) decided to move its coaches to a new player development curriculum emphasizing small-sided games. Several longtime volunteer coaches objected loudly at a meeting. The director's first instinct was to see them as old-school holdouts. Instead, she met each one individually and asked what worried them. Three themes emerged. Coaches feared looking incompetent in front of parents while running unfamiliar drills (efficacy and loss of status). Field space was allocated in a way that made small-sided games impractical on two nights a week (a flaw in the change). And several remembered a previous curriculum that was dropped after one season (a trust problem). The director fixed the field schedule, paired each longtime coach with a younger coach who had trained in the new method, invited the most vocal objector to help adapt the curriculum for the youngest age group, and committed publicly to a two-season trial with a review. The loudest resister became one of the curriculum's best advocates, partly because his concerns had visibly improved it.

Common mistake

Leaders often respond to objections by repeating the case for change more forcefully: more slides, more data, more urgency. This addresses discrepancy when the real concern is usually efficacy, fairness or loss. Before you persuade, ask. A good first question is "What would have to be true for this to work well for you?" The answer tells you which restraining force to reduce.

Sustaining change: safety, motivation and small wins

Many changes launch well and then fade. The new huddle format drifts back to the old one; the new documentation standard is followed for a month. Sustaining change depends heavily on the ideas from earlier chapters: psychological safety, motivation and team conditions.

Psychological safety makes learning possible

Every change involves a learning period in which people are less competent than before. If making visible mistakes is risky, people avoid the new behavior or hide problems with it. Amy Edmondson, Richard Bohmer and Gary Pisano (2001) studied cardiac surgery teams adopting a new minimally invasive technique that required surgeons, nurses, anesthesiologists and perfusionists to work together in unfamiliar ways. Teams that implemented it successfully had leaders who framed the change as a team learning challenge, not a technical add-on; who selected team members deliberately; who held early practice sessions; and who created an environment in which nurses and junior staff could speak up about problems during procedures. Teams with equally skilled surgeons but without these conditions often reverted to the old technique. The lesson generalizes: psychological safety is not a nice extra during change. It is how the organization finds and fixes the problems that every change produces.

Motivation that lasts

Self-determination theory (Chapter 3) suggests that change is more sustainable when it supports people's needs for autonomy, competence and relatedness. Autonomy is supported when people have real choices about how to implement a change, even if the what is fixed. Competence is supported through practice, coaching and feedback. Relatedness is supported when people learn together rather than alone. Lynne Herscovitch and John Meyer (2002) described three forms of commitment to change: affective (wanting to support it because you believe in it), normative (feeling obligated) and continuance (feeling you have no choice). In their studies, affective and normative commitment were associated with going beyond compliance, for example championing the change, whereas continuance commitment was associated mostly with minimal compliance.

The power of small wins and visible progress

Karl Weick (1984) argued that large social problems often feel overwhelming, and that reframing them as a series of "small wins," concrete, completed outcomes of moderate importance, reduces anxiety and builds momentum. Teresa Amabile and Steven Kramer (2011) analyzed nearly 12,000 daily diary entries from 238 knowledge workers and found that, of all the events that could lift people's inner work life, making progress in meaningful work was the most powerful. For change leaders this means designing the change so that progress is frequent and visible, and making a habit of noticing and naming it.

Goal-setting research (Locke and Latham, Chapter 3) adds a practical refinement. Specific, challenging goals with feedback improve performance on well-understood tasks, but when people are still learning a new way of working, learning goals ("identify three ways to make online intake faster for older patients") often work better than outcome goals ("reach 80 percent online bookings"). Early in a change, set learning goals; once competence builds, shift toward performance goals. Track a small number of measures that people can see and influence, and review them together, so that measurement feels like shared feedback rather than surveillance.

What the evidence says

Evidence on sustaining change is stronger for its ingredients than for any single model. Research reviews consistently find that participation, high-quality communication, trust in leaders and perceived fairness predict more positive reactions to change (Oreg, Vakola and Armenakis, 2011). Studies of surgical teams show that leader framing and psychological safety shape whether new practices are adopted (Edmondson, Bohmer and Pisano, 2001). Diary research links visible progress to motivation (Amabile and Kramer, 2011). What we have less of is controlled evidence comparing complete change models head to head. Treat models as organizing frameworks and these well-supported ingredients as the active parts.

Beyond step models: critiques and an adaptive approach

Step models are appealing because they turn a messy human process into a checklist. That is also their weakness. Critics have raised several concerns. First, linearity: real change loops back, stalls and restarts, and steps overlap. Second, top-down bias: most step models cast senior leaders as the source of change and everyone else as recipients, even though front-line staff often see problems and solutions first. Third, thin evidence: Steven Appelbaum and colleagues (2012), reviewing research related to Kotter's model, found support for many individual steps but little rigorous testing of the model as a whole. Rune Todnem By (2005) argued more broadly that much popular change-management guidance rests on limited empirical evidence. Fourth, a stable end state: in fast-moving environments, the idea of refreezing may be unrealistic.

None of this means you should discard the classic models. Lewin helps you diagnose forces; Kotter reminds you not to skip urgency, coalition-building and early wins; Armenakis and Harris help you craft the message. The critiques suggest using these models as lenses and checklists rather than as fixed recipes, and combining them with a more iterative approach.

Iterative change: test, learn, adapt

Health care has developed a well-established iterative method in the Model for Improvement (Langley and colleagues, first published 1996), which builds on the work of Walter Shewhart and W. Edwards Deming. It asks three questions: What are we trying to accomplish? How will we know that a change is an improvement? What change can we make that will result in improvement? It then uses rapid Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) cycles: plan a small test, do it, study the results, act on what you learned by adopting, adapting or abandoning the idea. A clinic might test a new intake process with one clinician for three mornings before rolling it out to everyone.

Iterative change fits the evidence in this chapter. Small tests reduce the fear of failure and make mistakes cheap, which supports psychological safety. They produce frequent small wins. They give front-line staff real influence, which builds autonomy and commitment. And they turn resistance into data, because concerns can be tested rather than argued about. A reasonable limitation is that iteration without a clear aim can drift; that is where the urgency and vision from the step models remain valuable.

A practical synthesis

For most readers, a workable approach combines the models in this chapter. Diagnose the forces with a force field analysis, and involve the people closest to the work. Build readiness by addressing all five beliefs, especially efficacy and personal valence, which leaders tend to neglect. Form a coalition that includes informal leaders and likely skeptics, not only managers. Test small with PDSA cycles before scaling. Listen to resistance as information and adjust. Protect psychological safety during the learning dip by thanking people who report problems. Make progress visible and celebrate small wins with specifics. Anchor the change by aligning attention, measures, rewards and hiring, as Chapter 7 described. Chapter 10 returns to this synthesis and asks you to build a full change plan.

Case

Returning to the sports medicine practice from the opening, the owner paused the rollout for three weeks. The front desk lead and one clinician from each location formed a small working group. They mapped the actual appointment flow, found that the software's default settings could not handle the practice's split sessions, and got the vendor to adjust them. They ran a PDSA test at one location on Tuesdays only, with the owner personally booking his own patients through the system. Glitches were logged on a shared board, and the owner thanked staff by name at each huddle for every one they reported. Within ten weeks, online bookings rose from under a fifth to about two thirds of appointments at the pilot site, and the other locations asked to start early. The software had not changed much. The approach had.

Key takeaways

  • Change is an external event; transition is the internal process of letting go, living with uncertainty and starting again. Plan for both.
  • Be skeptical of the claim that 70 percent of changes fail; it lacks a sound empirical basis.
  • Lewin's force field analysis shows that reducing restraining forces often works better than adding pressure. The tidy three-step model is useful shorthand but partly a later construction.
  • Kotter's eight steps offer a memorable sequence for large changes; they have limited direct testing and were later revised into concurrent accelerators.
  • Readiness rests on five beliefs: discrepancy, appropriateness, efficacy, principal support and personal valence. Leaders most often neglect efficacy and valence.
  • Resistance is information about the change, the process, real losses or trust. Ask before you persuade.
  • Psychological safety, autonomy-supportive motivation and visible small wins are the best-supported ingredients for making change stick.
  • Use step models as lenses and combine them with iterative test-and-learn cycles that involve the people doing the work.

Reflect and apply

  1. Recall a change at your workplace that stalled. Draw a quick force field analysis for it. Which restraining force, if reduced, would have made the biggest difference?
  2. For a change you are planning, write your readiness message using the five components. Which component was hardest to write honestly, and what does that tell you?
  3. Think of a colleague you have privately labeled as resistant. What might their resistance be telling you about the change, the process, a loss, or trust? What question could you ask them this week?
  4. Design a first PDSA cycle for a change in your team: what you will test, with whom, for how long, what you will measure, and how you will make it safe for people to report problems.
