Organizational Culture and Positive Climate

Culture is what people in your organization assume without thinking, and climate is what they feel every day. This chapter shows you how to read both, how leaders shape them whether they mean to or not, and how to build a climate where people and performance can flourish.

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish organizational culture from organizational climate and explain why leaders need to attend to both.
  • Analyze an organization using Schein's three levels of culture, separating artifacts, espoused values and basic assumptions.
  • Evaluate the embedding mechanisms a leader is actually using, and judge whether they match the culture the leader says they want.
  • Design a small set of positive practices, suited to your own team or subculture, that could strengthen a positive climate over one quarter.

Two physical therapy clinics sit four miles apart in the same town. They have the same equipment, similar pay, and the same electronic records system. Both have a framed poster in the break room listing the values "Compassion, Excellence, Teamwork." Yet if you spent one morning in each, you would leave with two very different feelings. In the first clinic, the front desk greets returning patients by name, therapists pop into each other's treatment rooms to ask for a second opinion, and when a scheduling mistake happens the owner says, "Let's figure out how the system let that happen." In the second, therapists keep their doors closed, the front desk apologizes for things that are not their fault, and when a mistake happens everyone quietly waits to find out who will be blamed. Same poster, same software. What differs is culture and climate, and the difference shows up in turnover, patient reviews, and whether anyone mentions the near miss before it becomes an incident.

Culture and climate: two lenses on the same place

In plain language, culture is "the way we do things around here, and the reasons we never question." It is the pattern of shared beliefs and habits that a group has learned over time because those beliefs and habits seemed to work. New members pick it up the way children pick up an accent: not from a manual, but by watching, listening and being gently corrected. Edgar Schein, whose work anchors this chapter, defined organizational culture as a pattern of shared basic assumptions that a group learned as it solved its problems of adapting to the outside world and holding itself together inside, assumptions that worked well enough to be taught to new members as the correct way to perceive, think and feel (Schein, 1985, with later editions through 2017).

Climate is closer to the surface. In plain language, climate is "what it feels like to work here, and what people believe gets rewarded, supported and expected." Researchers such as Benjamin Schneider describe organizational climate as the shared perceptions employees hold about the policies, practices and procedures they experience, and about the behaviors that get rewarded and supported (Schneider, Ehrhart and Macey, 2013). If culture is the deep current, climate is the weather on the water. You can measure climate fairly directly with a well-designed survey, because it is made of perceptions people can report. Culture is harder to survey, because its deepest layer is made of assumptions people do not realize they hold.

Why keep both terms? Because they point to different leadership work. Climate responds within months to how you run meetings, handle complaints and set policies. Culture responds over years, as those repeated experiences settle into beliefs about "how things really work here." Work only on climate and you may get a pleasant season that fades under pressure; talk only about culture and you may never change the daily experiences it is built from. Good leaders shape climate deliberately and watch whether the pattern sinks in deeply enough to become culture.

Climates for something

One of the most useful ideas from climate research is that climate is usually a climate for something. Dov Zohar's early work on safety climate (Zohar, 1980) showed that workers in industrial plants formed shared views about how much management really prioritized safety compared with speed, and those shared views predicted safety behavior. Schneider and colleagues studied service climate in bank branches and found that employee reports of service practices were linked to customer ratings of service quality (Schneider, White and Paul, 1998). In sport, coaches talk about a mastery climate versus an ego climate. The practical lesson: when you want to improve something specific, such as patient safety, customer experience or learning, ask what the climate for that specific thing looks like. A clinic can have a warm social climate and a weak safety climate at the same time.

Culture is learned, not declared

The key word in Schein's definition is "learned." A group does not choose its culture the way it chooses a logo; it learns it by noticing what works. If a small fitness studio survives its first year because the founder answered every complaint within the hour, the team learns "we win by being personally responsive," and that assumption may persist long after the studio has 900 members. Culture change is hard because it asks people to unlearn something that once helped them succeed.

Schein's three levels of culture

Schein's most widely taught contribution is a simple model with three levels. Picture an iceberg, or the layers of an onion. The model helps you avoid the most common error in reading culture, which is to confuse what you can see with what is actually driving behavior.

Level 1: Artifacts

Artifacts are everything you can see, hear and touch: the architecture, the dress code, the jargon, the way people address the owner, the awards on the wall, the rituals at the Monday huddle, the stories people tell. Artifacts are easy to observe but, Schein warned, hard to decipher. An open-plan office might mean the company values collaboration, or it might mean the lease was cheap. A sports club that holds a big end-of-season dinner might value belonging, or it might be a sponsor obligation that everyone privately dislikes. As an outsider or newcomer, you should collect artifacts eagerly but hold your interpretations lightly until you understand the deeper levels.

Level 2: Espoused beliefs and values

Espoused values are what the organization says it believes: the mission statement, the stated values, the strategy deck, the reasons leaders give when explaining a decision. Some espoused values are genuinely held and have been tested by experience. Others are aspirations, or public relations, or the values of a previous leader. The tell is whether behavior lines up with them under pressure. A wellness center that espouses "whole-person care" but books every practitioner in 15-minute slots and ranks them on visits per day has an espoused value that is not being enacted. Schein's point is not that such organizations are hypocritical in a cartoon sense. It is that espoused values sit on top of something deeper, and when the two conflict, the deeper level usually wins.

Level 3: Basic underlying assumptions

Basic underlying assumptions are beliefs so taken for granted that people no longer notice them or debate them. Schein described several broad domains they tend to cover: the nature of reality and truth (do we decide based on data, on the senior person's judgment, or on tradition?), the nature of time (are deadlines firm or flexible?), the nature of human nature (are people basically trustworthy and eager to work, or do they need watching?), the nature of human relationships (is this a place for individual stars or for the group?), and the organization's relationship to its environment (do we shape our market or react to it?).

You surface assumptions by noticing inconsistencies between levels. When espoused values and artifacts do not match, ask what belief would make the observed behavior sensible. If a veterinary practice says it values open communication, yet staff never raise concerns in meetings and instead complain in the parking lot, a plausible underlying assumption might be "raising problems in public makes the owner look bad, and making the owner look bad is dangerous." That assumption, not the stated value, explains the behavior.

Common mistake

The most common mistake in culture work is treating the espoused level as if it were the whole culture. Leaders run an off-site, agree on five inspiring values, print them on posters and lanyards, and declare the culture changed. Nothing below the waterline has moved. Worse, if daily behavior contradicts the new values, the posters become artifacts that teach cynicism: people learn that "values talk is what leaders do instead of changing things." Before announcing values, check which of your own routine decisions would have to change for the values to be true, and change those first.

How leaders embed and transmit culture

If culture is learned, the natural question is: who teaches it? Schein's answer is that leaders are the most powerful teachers, and that they teach mostly through what they do rather than what they say. He distinguished primary embedding mechanisms, which directly teach the group what matters, from secondary articulation and reinforcement mechanisms, which support the lesson only when they are consistent with the primary ones.

The six primary mechanisms

What leaders pay attention to, measure and control. If the first question at every clinic meeting is "How many visits did we do?" and the last, if there is time, is "How are patients doing?", staff learn which one matters. Measurement is attention made permanent.

How leaders react to critical incidents and crises. When a fitness studio loses its biggest corporate contract, does the owner cut the newest trainers, share the pain equally, or open the books and ask for ideas? Whatever she does will be retold for years as "what happens here when times get hard."

How leaders allocate resources. Budgets reveal assumptions. A sports academy that says development is its priority but has not funded coach education in three years is teaching that development is a slogan. Time is a resource too.

Deliberate role modeling, teaching and coaching. Leaders teach by example, including the example of admitting mistakes, asking for help, taking a lunch break or not. A practice owner who says "take your breaks" while eating at her desk every day is modeling the real norm.

How leaders allocate rewards and status. Promotions, bonuses, praise in front of the group, the choice of who gets the good schedule: these signal what the organization values more clearly than any statement. If the person who hits the numbers while belittling colleagues keeps getting rewarded, everyone learns that results excuse behavior.

How leaders recruit, select, promote and remove people. Over time, the people in an organization become its culture. Schneider's attraction-selection-attrition idea (Schneider, 1987) captures this: people are attracted to organizations that seem to fit them, organizations select people who seem to fit, and people who do not fit tend to leave. The cycle makes cultures more homogeneous, which can strengthen purpose but also narrow thinking.

The secondary mechanisms

The secondary mechanisms (structure, systems, rituals, physical space, stories and formal statements) are real levers: a new peer recognition ritual can make gratitude visible. But they work only when they are consistent with the primary ones. If the formal statement says "we learn from mistakes" and the owner's reaction to the last mistake was a public reprimand, people believe the reprimand.

Try it this week

Keep a simple attention log for five working days. Each evening, write down the first three questions you asked your team that day, one decision you made about time or money, and one thing you praised or corrected. On Friday, read the log as if you were a new employee trying to figure out what matters here. Compare that conclusion with the values your organization espouses. Pick one primary mechanism where the gap is largest and change one routine behavior next week.

Positive practices and a positive climate

So far we have described culture neutrally. This book takes a positive lens, drawing on positive organizational scholarship introduced in Chapter 1. The question here is: what kinds of practices, repeated consistently, produce a climate in which people and organizations flourish? Kim Cameron, one of the founders of positive organizational scholarship, has written extensively on this question.

Cameron's four strategies

In Positive Leadership (Cameron, 2008), Cameron described four broad strategies that leaders can use to enable what he calls positively deviant performance, meaning performance that goes well beyond the ordinary. The first is positive climate: fostering compassion, forgiveness and gratitude as normal parts of working life, so that emotions such as appreciation and hope outnumber cynicism and fear. The second is positive relationships: building networks of energizing connections and helping people build on their strengths. The third is positive communication: using supportive, affirming language and giving feedback in ways that build people up even when the message is corrective, a topic we explored in Chapter 4. The fourth is positive meaning: helping people connect their work to personal values, to an important purpose and to a sense of community.

Positive practices in daily life

Cameron and colleagues later tried to measure specific positive practices in organizations. Their list included caring for colleagues as friends, providing support when someone is struggling, avoiding blame and forgiving mistakes, inspiring one another at work, emphasizing the meaningfulness of the work, and treating one another with respect, integrity and gratitude (Cameron, Mora, Leutscher and Calarco, 2011). None of these is exotic. What makes them powerful, the argument goes, is consistency: they are practiced routinely, by many people, including when it is inconvenient.

Cameron often uses the metaphor of the heliotropic effect, the tendency of living things to turn toward light and energy. The idea is that people are drawn toward positive energy and away from depleting interactions. Related research on positive energizers supports part of this intuition. Wayne Baker, Rob Cross and colleagues mapped energy networks in organizations and found that people who energize others tend to be higher performers and to help others perform well (Baker, Cross and Wooten, 2003). A later study found that relational energy from a leader was associated with employee engagement and job performance (Owens, Baker, Sumpter and Cameron, 2016). Being an energizer is not about being bubbly; it is about being present and helping people make progress.

What the evidence says

Cameron and colleagues (2011) studied positive practices in a financial services company and in a sample of health care units. Units with higher ratings of positive practices tended to show better effectiveness indicators, and in the health care sample, improvements in positive practices over time were associated with improvements in outcomes such as turnover, patient satisfaction and work climate. This is encouraging, and it fits a larger body of research linking supportive climates with engagement and retention. But be honest about the limits: the studies are correlational, practices were self-reported, the number of organizations was modest, and the researchers were also advocates of the approach. It remains possible that successful units simply find it easier to be kind. The fair conclusion is that positive practices are plausibly beneficial, low in cost, and worth testing in your own setting with outcomes you track.

Positive does not mean nice

A positive climate is sometimes misunderstood as a climate where nobody delivers bad news. That is not what the research describes. Forgiveness, in Cameron's sense, means not humiliating people for mistakes; it does not mean ignoring them. A genuinely positive climate is also a high-standards climate. The combination of high support and high standards is what distinguishes flourishing teams from both harsh teams (high standards, low support) and comfortable teams (high support, low standards). The positive leader's job is to raise both at once.

Common mistake

Some leaders adopt "positivity" as a rule that everyone must feel good, and they treat concerns as negativity. This produces what critics call toxic positivity, and it quietly destroys psychological safety. If staff learn that raising a problem earns a lecture about attitude, they stop raising problems. Positive leadership amplifies what is going well and addresses what is not. Build rituals of gratitude and rituals of honest review side by side.

Culture in founder-led and small organizations

Many readers of this book work in, or lead, small organizations: a chiropractic or dental practice, a boutique gym, a family-owned pharmacy, a club, a consulting firm of twelve people. In these settings culture is not an abstraction. It is often, to a remarkable degree, the founder's personality and history written onto a group.

Founders as culture creators

Schein (1983) argued that founders play a special role in creating culture. A founder arrives with strong assumptions about what the business is for, how customers should be treated, how decisions should be made and what kind of people can be trusted. In the early days, the founder hires people who share or accept those assumptions, and makes almost every important decision personally. When the business survives, the group concludes that the founder's way works. The assumptions become shared and then taken for granted. Sociologist Arthur Stinchcombe (1965) called the broader version of this pattern imprinting: organizations tend to carry features of the conditions under which they were founded, long after those conditions have changed.

A striking body of evidence comes from the Stanford Project on Emerging Companies, which followed about two hundred young technology firms. James Baron, Michael Hannan and Diane Burton found that founders held distinct employment "blueprints," for example a commitment model built on emotional attachment and belonging, a star model built on hiring exceptional talent, or an engineering model built on challenging work. These early blueprints shaped later practices, and firms that changed their blueprint experienced higher employee turnover, even when the change was toward a model that looked better on paper (Baron, Hannan and Burton, 2001). The lesson for small-business leaders is not "never change." It is that founding choices are sticky, and changing them carries real human costs that should be planned for.

Growth stages and the founder's shadow

Founder culture is a strength when the organization is small, because it provides clarity and speed. It can become a constraint as the organization grows. Assumptions that fit a team of five, such as "the owner decides everything" or "we handle problems through personal relationships," strain in a team of fifty. Growth brings specialists, managers, procedures and new subcultures, and espoused values like "we are a family" stop matching artifacts like a formal review system.

The most delicate moment is succession, when the founder hands over leadership, whether to a child, a partner, a buyer or a hired manager. The new leader inherits a culture they did not create and assumptions they may not share. Successors who announce sweeping change often trigger resistance, because people hear it as a judgment on the founder and themselves; those who study the culture first and name which assumptions no longer fit tend to fare better.

Case

Ridgeview Family Chiropractic (fictional) was founded by Dr. Maria Okafor, who built the practice over eighteen years by personally knowing every patient and every family. Her staff learned to bring every problem straight to her. When she opened a second location with Dr. James Lin as clinic director, staff kept calling her directly, and Dr. Lin's new written procedures felt cold to the original team; two long-serving assistants left. Mapping Schein's levels, the team saw that everyone shared the espoused value of patient-centered care. The conflict was an assumption: "real authority and real care come from Maria personally." Dr. Okafor began redirecting calls to Dr. Lin and praising him publicly; Dr. Lin co-wrote procedures with the most experienced assistant and added a "patient story of the week" ritual. The culture shifted through repeated, visible actions by both leaders, not by announcement.

Subcultures: many cultures under one roof

Talking about "the culture" of an organization is convenient, but it is often misleading. Most organizations of any size contain subcultures, groups whose shared assumptions differ in meaningful ways from those of the wider organization. In a hospital, nursing, surgery, pharmacy, billing and facilities each have their own languages, rituals and taken-for-granted beliefs. In a professional sports club, the front office, the coaching staff and the medical and performance team may have strikingly different views about what matters most in the same injury decision.

Where subcultures come from

Subcultures form around occupations, functions, locations, shifts and shared history. John Van Maanen and Stephen Barley (1984) described occupational communities, groups of people who identify with their occupation, share values and norms drawn from it, and often feel more loyalty to their profession than to their employer. A physical therapist's professional identity comes partly from training and licensure, not from whichever clinic employs them. Schein (1996) described three recurring management subcultures: an operator culture of front-line people who know that work depends on teamwork and adjusting to surprises; an engineering or design culture that prefers elegant systems and sees people as a source of error; and an executive culture focused on finances and survival. Many failed changes, he argued, come from these groups talking past each other.

Three ways to see culture

Joanne Martin (1992) offered a helpful way to hold all this together. She described three perspectives researchers and managers use. The integration perspective sees culture as consistent and shared across the organization, with clear agreement on values. The differentiation perspective sees the organization as a collection of subcultures that may agree internally but differ from, or conflict with, one another. The fragmentation perspective sees culture as ambiguous and in flux, with people agreeing on some issues and not others, and meanings shifting over time. Martin's point was that each perspective captures something real, and a wise leader uses all three. Your clinic may be integrated around patient care, differentiated between clinicians and administrative staff about scheduling, and fragmented about how to use the new software.

Leading across subcultures

Subcultures are not a problem to be eliminated; they often carry expertise and useful standards. The leader's task is to find the deeper purpose they share, such as patient wellbeing, and build bridges where assumptions collide: cross-functional huddles, job shadowing, shared metrics each group helped design, and mixed project teams (Chapter 5). The phrase "they just don't get it" usually marks a subculture boundary and is an invitation to translate rather than judge.

Reading and shifting culture

How do you describe a culture in a way that allows comparison and planning? One widely used tool is the Competing Values Framework, developed by Robert Quinn and John Rohrbaugh (1983) from research on organizational effectiveness and later applied to culture by Kim Cameron and Robert Quinn (1999, revised 2011). The framework places organizations on two dimensions. One runs from flexibility and discretion to stability and control. The other runs from an internal focus on integration to an external focus on differentiation and the market. Crossing the two dimensions produces four culture types.

A clan culture emphasizes collaboration, loyalty, mentoring and belonging; many small practices and community sports clubs lean this way. An adhocracy culture emphasizes innovation, experimentation and adaptability, as in a startup building a new wellness app. A market culture emphasizes results, competition and customer focus, as in a sales-driven fitness chain. A hierarchy culture emphasizes reliability, standard procedures and efficiency, as in a pharmacy or a surgical center where consistency is a matter of safety. The framework's name reflects its central insight: these values compete. Pushing hard on control can stifle creativity; pushing hard on market results can erode cohesion. The healthiest organizations tend to hold these tensions deliberately rather than maximizing one quadrant.

Cameron and Quinn's assessment instrument compares members' views of the culture now with the culture they prefer. You do not need the formal tool: asking a team "Where do we sit on these two dimensions now, and where does our strategy need us to be?" often starts a rich discussion.

What the evidence says

Does culture predict performance? John Kotter and James Heskett (1992) studied large firms over roughly a decade and found that a strong culture on its own was only weakly related to long-term performance. What mattered more was whether the culture was adaptive: whether it valued customers, employees and owners and encouraged leadership and change. A meta-analysis of studies using the Competing Values Framework by Chad Hartnell, Amy Ou and Angelo Kinicki (2011) found that clan cultures were most strongly associated with employee attitudes such as satisfaction and commitment, and that all three of the more studied types (clan, adhocracy and market) showed positive associations with various effectiveness criteria. The same study found that the four types were more strongly correlated with one another than the theory predicts, which suggests that real organizations blend them and that "culture type" labels should be held loosely. Most studies are cross-sectional, so we cannot be sure whether culture drives performance or performance shapes culture. The popular line "culture eats strategy for breakfast," often credited to Peter Drucker, has no documented source in his writing, and it overstates the case: culture and strategy work together.

A realistic approach to shifting culture

If culture is learned through experience, then it changes through new experiences that succeed. You cannot talk people out of assumptions that have worked for them. You can, however, create conditions in which a new way of working is tried, produces good results, and is recognized, until it becomes the new "way we do things." Several practical principles follow from this chapter.

Start with a business problem, not with culture in the abstract. Schein advised beginning with a concrete issue, such as rising no-shows or poor handoffs, and asking which assumptions help or hinder solving it. Build on existing strengths: change that frames the past as bad invites defensiveness, while "we will keep what made us good and adapt how we do it" invites participation. Align the primary mechanisms so that what you attend to, fund, reward and model all point the same way, consistently, for months. Make positive practices routine, because repetition is what turns climate into culture. Involve subcultures in translating the shared purpose into their own practices. And measure climate along the way, through pulse surveys, turnover and client feedback, since assumptions move slowly. Chapter 8 takes up how to lead a change process of this kind.

Case

Harbor Strength and Conditioning (fictional), a gym with four locations, had a strong market culture: coaches were ranked on monthly personal-training sales, and the leaderboard hung in every staff room. Member retention was falling and coach turnover was high. The owner, Theo Brandt, asked each location to describe its culture on the two Competing Values dimensions. Coaches placed the gym firmly in the market quadrant and said they wanted more clan and adhocracy: more mentoring and freedom to try new class formats. Theo kept sales targets but changed what he attended to first, opening each meeting with member progress stories; he funded a quarterly coach-designed pilot class, added peer-nominated recognition, and moved the leaderboard from the staff room to the managers' dashboard. Eighteen months later, coach turnover had dropped and retention had improved.

Key takeaways

  • Culture is a pattern of shared, learned assumptions; climate is the shared perception of what is rewarded, supported and expected. Climate changes faster, culture more slowly.
  • Climate is usually a climate for something, such as safety, service or learning. Diagnose the specific climate that matters for your goal.
  • Schein's three levels (artifacts, espoused values and basic assumptions) help you avoid confusing what is visible or stated with what actually drives behavior.
  • Leaders embed culture mainly through primary mechanisms: attention and measurement, reactions to crises, resource allocation, role modeling, rewards and status, and selection and removal. Formal statements help only when they match.
  • Cameron's positive leadership strategies (positive climate, relationships, communication and meaning) describe repeatable practices; evidence for their benefits is promising but mostly correlational.
  • Founders imprint strong cultures that help early and can constrain later; growth and succession are the moments when founding assumptions need honest review.
  • Most organizations contain subcultures. Leaders should find shared purpose and build bridges rather than trying to erase differences.
  • Culture shifts when new ways of working are tried, succeed and are recognized repeatedly, starting from a concrete business problem and building on existing strengths.

Self-check

Reflect and apply

  1. Walk through your workplace as if you were a visitor. List ten artifacts. For three of them, write two different values they could express, then decide which basic assumption best explains them.
  2. Think about the last time something went seriously wrong at work. How did the most senior person react, and what did the team learn from that reaction about "how things really work here"?
  3. Identify two subcultures in your organization. What does each group take for granted that the other does not? Describe one practical bridge you could build between them this quarter.
  4. Choose one of Cameron's four strategies that is weakest in your team. Design one repeatable practice for it, decide how often it will happen, and name one climate indicator you will track to see whether it is working.
