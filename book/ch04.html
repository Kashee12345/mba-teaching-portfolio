Psychological Safety, Trust and Voice

How teams come to believe it is safe to speak up, why that belief protects patients, clients and profits, and what a leader does every day to build it without lowering the bar.

Learning objectives

  • Explain psychological safety as a shared team belief and distinguish it from trust, niceness and low standards.
  • Analyze why employees choose voice or silence, using the ideas of perceived safety, perceived efficacy and implicit voice theories.
  • Apply supportive communication principles and structured speaking-up tools such as SBAR and graded assertiveness to your own setting.
  • Develop a practical plan to set the stage, invite participation and respond productively when people speak up.

It is 4:40 on a Friday afternoon at a family medicine clinic. Jordan, a medical assistant three months into the job, is preparing a patient's injection and notices that the vial on the tray is a different concentration from the one in the order. The physician, who has practiced for twenty years and is known for being brisk, is already walking into the room. Jordan has a few seconds to decide. If Jordan speaks and is wrong, it will be embarrassing in front of a patient. If Jordan speaks and is right, the physician might still be annoyed. If Jordan stays quiet, the odds are that it is fine. This small moment, repeated millions of times a day in clinics, gyms, kitchens and offices, is what this chapter is about. Whether Jordan speaks depends less on Jordan's courage than on what the team has learned, over many earlier moments, about what happens to people who speak up.

What psychological safety is, and what it is not

In plain language, psychological safety is the feeling, shared across a team, that you will not be humiliated, punished or quietly written off for asking a question, admitting a mistake, raising a concern or offering an unfinished idea. Amy Edmondson of Harvard Business School gave the concept its modern form in a 1999 study of work teams, defining team psychological safety as a shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk taking. Earlier, Edgar Schein and Warren Bennis had written in 1965 about the need for psychological safety in organizational learning, and William Kahn in 1990 described it at the individual level as feeling able to show yourself without fear of negative consequences to self-image, status or career.

Three features of Edmondson's definition are easy to miss.

  • It is shared. Psychological safety is a property of a group, not of one person's personality. Members of the same team tend to hold similar beliefs, and different teams in the same organization can differ sharply. A hospital can have one unit where nurses speak freely and another down the hall where they do not.
  • It is about interpersonal risk. The risks in question are the social costs of looking ignorant, incompetent, negative or disruptive. Those four fears map neatly onto the four things people most often avoid: asking questions, admitting errors, critiquing the status quo and offering new ideas.
  • It is a belief, often taken for granted. People rarely think about it consciously. They just notice, over time, what happens to colleagues who speak up, and adjust.

Psychological safety is often misunderstood. It is not niceness. A team where everyone is pleasant but nobody disagrees is often low in psychological safety, because politeness has become a reason to stay quiet. It is not the absence of accountability. Edmondson is emphatic that safety and high standards are separate dimensions, and the best teams have both. It is not comfort. Learning, candor and disagreement are frequently uncomfortable. A safe team is one where discomfort does not turn into personal risk. And it is not a guarantee that everyone will speak up. It lowers a barrier; it does not create the motivation to cross it.

Figure 4.1 is the single most useful picture for leaders worried that psychological safety means going soft. Look at the upper left: high standards with low safety produces the anxiety zone, where people work hard but hide problems. Many high-pressure clinics, sales teams and elite sports programs live there. The lower right is the comfort zone: people feel good but are not stretched. The goal is the upper right, where people are asked to do hard things and know they can speak honestly while doing them.

What the evidence shows

Edmondson came to the concept almost by accident. In a study published in 1996, she examined medication errors in eight hospital nursing units and expected that units with better leadership and relationships would make fewer errors. The data showed the opposite: the better-led units reported more errors. Further observation suggested why. In those units, nurses felt able to report and discuss mistakes. In units with punitive managers, errors were hidden. The better units were probably not making more mistakes; they were catching and talking about more of them. This paradox, that a healthy team can look worse on a reporting metric, is one of the most important lessons in this chapter.

Her 1999 study of 51 work teams in a manufacturing company found that team psychological safety was associated with learning behavior, such as seeking feedback, discussing errors and experimenting, and that learning behavior in turn was associated with team performance. Since then, the research base has grown large. A 2017 meta-analysis by M. Lance Frazier and colleagues, drawing on well over a hundred studies, found that psychological safety was associated with information sharing, learning behavior, engagement, and task performance, and that leadership behaviors and supportive work contexts were among its strongest predictors.

The best-known popular evidence comes from Google's internal Project Aristotle, which studied what made some of its teams more effective than others and reported in 2015 that psychological safety was the most important of five factors it identified. That report brought the idea to a wide business audience. It is worth remembering that it was an internal company study described in a blog-style summary, not a peer-reviewed article, so it is best treated as a well-publicized illustration consistent with the academic research rather than as independent proof.

What the evidence says

The research strongly supports an association between psychological safety and learning, voice and performance, but three limits deserve honesty. First, most studies are cross-sectional surveys, so causation is hard to establish; performing well may make people feel safe, not only the reverse. Second, the benefits appear larger for work that is uncertain, complex and interdependent, where learning matters, than for routine independent tasks. Third, a few studies suggest psychological safety can have side effects when paired with weak accountability, such as reduced effort or more tolerance for rule bending. Edmondson's own framework anticipates this: safety without standards produces the comfort zone. Treat psychological safety as a necessary condition for candor, not as a magic ingredient that produces excellence on its own.

Voice and silence

Employee voice is speaking up with ideas, suggestions, concerns or opinions intended to improve how things work. Linn Van Dyne and Jeffrey LePine described voice in 1998 as a form of challenging but constructive extra-role behavior: it goes beyond the job description, and it aims to improve rather than merely complain. Later researchers distinguished two flavors. Promotive voice offers new ideas for improvement ("What if we sent intake forms by text before the first visit?"). Prohibitive voice raises concerns about practices or incidents that could cause harm ("We are skipping the second identifier check when it gets busy"). Work by Wu Liu, Jian Liang and colleagues around 2012 found that the two have somewhat different triggers, and prohibitive voice, though often less welcome, is frequently the kind that prevents disasters.

The opposite is employee silence: withholding information, questions or concerns that could be useful. Elizabeth Morrison and Frances Milliken argued in 2000 that silence can become a collective property of an organization, which they called organizational silence, when leaders' fear of negative feedback and beliefs that employees are self-interested shape structures that discourage upward communication. Van Dyne, Soon Ang and Isabel Botero described in 2003 several different motives for silence:

  • Acquiescent silence: withholding because you believe speaking will not make a difference. "Why bother? Nothing changes."
  • Defensive silence: withholding out of fear, to protect yourself. "I am not going to be the one who gets blamed."
  • Prosocial silence: withholding to protect others or the organization, for example keeping confidential information private or not embarrassing a colleague.

The distinction matters because the cures differ. Defensive silence responds to safety. Acquiescent silence responds to evidence that voice works. Prosocial silence is sometimes appropriate, but it can also shield a colleague whose unsafe practice needs addressing.

The voice calculus

Research by Morrison and others suggests that, in any given moment, people weigh two questions before speaking. The first is about efficacy: "Will speaking up make any difference?" The second is about safety: "What will it cost me?" Both need a favorable answer. Studies by James Detert and Ethan Burris in 2007 found that restaurant employees spoke up more when their managers were open to input and led in a transformational way, and that the effect ran largely through employees' sense of psychological safety.

People also carry beliefs about voice from earlier jobs and from the wider culture. Detert and Edmondson in 2011 called these implicit voice theories: taken-for-granted rules such as "Do not bypass your boss," "Do not criticize an idea your manager came up with," "You need solid data before you raise something" and "Speaking up in front of others embarrasses the leader." These rules are often learned in previous workplaces and can persist even under a leader who genuinely welcomes input. That is why a manager who says "My door is always open" is often surprised that nobody walks through it.

Trust: the willingness to be vulnerable

Trust and psychological safety are related but different. In plain terms, trust is about whether I am willing to rely on you, knowing you could let me down. The most widely cited definition, from Denise Rousseau and colleagues in 1998, describes trust as a psychological state involving the intention to accept vulnerability based on positive expectations of another's intentions or behavior. Psychological safety, by contrast, is about how the group as a whole will treat me if I take a risk in front of it. Trust usually runs between two parties; psychological safety is a shared climate.

The ability, benevolence and integrity model

What makes a person seem trustworthy? Roger Mayer, James Davis and F. David Schoorman proposed an integrative model in 1995 that remains the standard starting point. They argued that we judge trustworthiness on three dimensions:

  • Ability: the skills and competence to do what is needed in a particular domain. You may trust a colleague's clinical judgment but not their spreadsheet skills.
  • Benevolence: the belief that the other party wants to do good for you, apart from any selfish motive.
  • Integrity: the belief that the other party follows principles you find acceptable, such as honesty, fairness and keeping promises.

These perceptions combine with each person's propensity to trust, a general tendency some people have to extend trust readily and others to withhold it. Trust then leads to risk taking in the relationship, such as sharing information, delegating or admitting a mistake, and the outcome of that risk feeds back into future perceptions. Trust is built in small cycles of risk and reliability.

Trust in leaders matters. A 2002 meta-analysis by Kurt Dirks and Donald Ferrin found that trust in direct leaders was associated with job performance, organizational citizenship behavior, job satisfaction and commitment, and with lower intention to quit. Trust also matters for how breaches are repaired. Research by Peter Kim and colleagues in 2004 found that when a violation seemed to be about competence, apologizing and taking responsibility tended to repair trust better than denial. When it seemed to be about integrity, the picture was more complicated, because admitting guilt confirmed a character flaw. For leaders, the practical lesson is simple: be clear about what went wrong, own your part, and back your words with a visible change. An apology without a change in behavior is a second breach.

Case

Cedar Street Veterinary and Wellness, a fictional three-doctor practice, had a medical director who was highly skilled (strong on ability) and deeply devoted to patients (strong on benevolence toward animals and clients). Staff trust was nonetheless low. The problem was integrity in the narrow sense of the model: she frequently promised schedule changes, new equipment and weekend coverage that never arrived. Staff stopped raising problems because they assumed nothing would happen, a textbook case of acquiescent silence. A new practice manager suggested a simple rule: every commitment made in the Monday huddle went onto a visible board with an owner and a date, and the director reported back on each one the following week, including the ones she could not deliver and why. Within two months, the number of issues raised in huddles roughly doubled. Nothing about the director's skill or compassion had changed. What changed was the evidence that promises would be kept or honestly explained.

Positive communication and supportive feedback

Psychological safety and trust are built or destroyed mostly through ordinary conversations. Kim Cameron, a founder of positive organizational scholarship, identifies positive communication as one of four core strategies of positive leadership, alongside positive climate, positive relationships and positive meaning. In his 2008 book Positive Leadership and in his teaching with David Whetten, Cameron describes a set of skills called supportive communication: ways of delivering even hard messages that preserve or strengthen the relationship.

The core principles, in plain language, are:

  • Be congruent. Say what you actually think and feel. People sense when words and body language do not match, and it erodes trust.
  • Describe rather than evaluate. "The last three client files were missing consent signatures" invites problem solving. "You are careless with paperwork" invites defense.
  • Focus on the problem, not the person. Talk about behaviors and situations people can change, not traits.
  • Validate the other person. Communicate respect, flexibility and the sense that their view is worth hearing, even when you disagree.
  • Be specific. Vague praise and vague criticism are both hard to act on.
  • Be conjunctive. Link your message to what the other person just said, so the conversation builds rather than jumps.
  • Own your statements. Use "I think" and "I noticed" rather than hiding behind "people are saying" or "management feels."
  • Listen supportively. Listen at least as much as you speak, and choose responses such as probing and reflecting before advising.

Cameron also stresses positive energy in relationships. Research by Rob Cross, Wayne Baker and Andrew Parker in 2003 mapped energy networks in organizations and found that people who energize others tend to be higher performers and help others perform better. Jane Dutton's work on high-quality connections adds that even brief interactions, such as a moment of genuine attention at a handoff, can build vitality and mutual regard. In a busy clinic or gym, the difference between a de-energizing and an energizing leader is often visible in the first thirty seconds of a conversation.

The trouble with magic ratios

You may have heard that high-performing teams use about five or six positive statements for every negative one. That idea traces to a 2004 study by Marcial Losada and Emily Heaphy, who coded business team meetings and reported that high-performing teams showed a much higher ratio of positive to negative comments than low performers. It was later extended, with Barbara Fredrickson, into a claim of a precise "positivity ratio" threshold of about 2.9 for flourishing, supported by mathematical modeling. In 2013, Nicholas Brown, Alan Sokal and Harris Friedman published a detailed critique showing that the mathematical modeling was invalid, and the modeling portion of that paper was formally withdrawn. Fredrickson has maintained that higher ratios of positive to negative emotion are broadly beneficial, but no precise tipping point is supported. The sensible takeaway is modest: teams generally do better when positive interactions clearly outnumber negative ones, and negative interactions tend to carry more psychological weight than positive ones. Do not count comments to hit a target.

Common mistake

Declaring psychological safety instead of building it. A studio owner announces at a staff meeting, "This is a safe space. You can tell me anything," and then, two days later, visibly sighs and changes the subject when a front desk worker points out that the new membership software double-charges some clients. Everyone in the room learns the real rule from the sigh, not from the announcement. A second version of the mistake is equating safety with never delivering bad news: withholding clear feedback to keep things pleasant moves a team toward the comfort zone and leaves people uncertain where they stand. Safety comes from how you respond to the hard things people say, and from saying hard things yourself with care.

Speaking up in clinical settings

Health care has been one of the main laboratories for this chapter's ideas, for a grim reason. The Institute of Medicine's 1999 report To Err Is Human estimated that tens of thousands of hospital patients in the United States died each year from preventable medical errors, and it argued that most errors come from faulty systems rather than bad individuals. A recurring finding in reviews of serious incidents is that someone on the team noticed something was wrong and either did not speak up or spoke up in a way that was not heard.

Part of the problem is the authority gradient, a term borrowed from aviation for the difference in perceived status and power between members of a team. When the gradient is steep, junior members hesitate to challenge senior ones, and seniors may discount what juniors say. Aviation confronted this after accidents in the 1970s in which crew members had noticed dangers but deferred to the captain. The resulting training, known as crew resource management, taught pilots and crews to share information, cross-check and challenge each other regardless of rank. Health care began adapting these methods in the 1990s and 2000s.

Several tools from that work are now common in clinics and hospitals, and they transfer well to fitness, sports medicine and other settings where safety depends on teamwork:

  • SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) is a structured way to communicate a concern. It originated in the U.S. Navy and was adapted for health care by Kaiser Permanente in the early 2000s. It helps a junior team member deliver a concern concisely and gives the listener a predictable format.
  • Graded assertiveness means escalating the strength of a concern if it is not heard. The TeamSTEPPS program developed by the U.S. Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality and the Department of Defense teaches the "CUS" words, stating that you are Concerned, then Uncomfortable, then that this is a Safety issue, as a shared signal that everyone agrees must stop the action.
  • The two-challenge rule, also from TeamSTEPPS, says that a concern should be voiced at least twice if it is not acknowledged, and that a team member may escalate further if it is still ignored.
  • Briefings and checklists, such as the World Health Organization's Surgical Safety Checklist, create a scheduled moment for every team member to speak. In a 2009 study across eight hospitals in different countries, Alex Haynes and colleagues found that introducing the checklist was associated with reductions in complications and deaths. Part of its value appears to be that the introductions and briefing flatten the authority gradient, although later studies found that results depend heavily on how seriously teams use it.

Leader behavior matters as much as tools. In a 2006 study of neonatal intensive care units, Ingrid Nembhard and Amy Edmondson introduced the idea of leader inclusiveness: words and deeds by leaders that invite and appreciate others' contributions. They found that physician inclusiveness helped offset the negative effect of professional status differences on psychological safety, and that safer units were more engaged in quality improvement. Status differences did not disappear, but inclusive leaders kept them from silencing people.

Return to Jordan, the medical assistant. Whether Jordan speaks depends on whether earlier moments taught the team that questions are welcome. It also depends on whether Jordan has a script. A clinic that has trained everyone to say, "I need to pause: I am concerned the concentration on this vial does not match the order," and whose physicians have been coached to reply "Thank you for checking, let's look," has converted an act of courage into a routine. That is the goal: make voice ordinary.

Building it as a leader

Edmondson's 2019 book The Fearless Organization summarizes a practical toolkit in three parts. It works as a cycle rather than a checklist, because every response you give shapes what people risk next time.

Set the stage

Begin by framing the work honestly. When work is uncertain or interdependent, say so: "Every patient's recovery is a bit different, and we are going to learn as we go. I need to hear what you are seeing." Framing work as a learning problem rather than an execution problem signals that questions and observations are part of the job, not a sign of weakness. Name the stakes clearly, too. People speak up more readily when they understand that silence has costs for patients, athletes or customers.

Invite participation

Next, make input easy and expected. Edmondson calls the key attitude situational humility: acknowledging that you do not have all the answers, not because you lack competence but because the situation is complex. A head coach who says "I may be missing something from the sidelines. What are you seeing on the field?" has lowered the cost of speaking. Ask genuine questions that you do not already know the answer to, and prefer open questions ("What concerns you about this plan?") over closed ones ("Any concerns? No? Great."). Build structures that make voice routine: a standing agenda item for near misses, a round in which everyone contributes before the most senior person speaks, or an anonymous channel for issues that still feel too risky to raise openly.

Respond productively

Finally, and most importantly, respond in ways that reward risk taking. Thank people for speaking before you evaluate what they said. Treat honest mistakes and intelligent failures in new territory as information, not as occasions for blame. Edmondson distinguishes between blameworthy failures, such as deliberately violating a safety rule, and praiseworthy ones, such as a thoughtful experiment that did not work. Responding productively does not mean excusing everything. Clear, deliberate violations should have consequences, and applying them fairly actually increases psychological safety, because people see that the rules protect everyone.

Try it this week

In your next team meeting or huddle, try three small moves. First, open with a framing sentence that names the uncertainty in your work ("Here is what I am not sure about this week..."). Second, ask one genuine open question and then wait at least five seconds before speaking again, even if the silence feels long. Third, when someone raises a concern or a mistake, make your first words a thank you ("Thanks for flagging that") before any evaluation. Afterward, jot down who spoke, who did not, and how your response felt to you. Repeat for three meetings and look for changes.

These practices apply far beyond hospitals. A personal training studio where coaches feel safe telling the owner that a popular program is causing injuries can fix the program before a lawsuit. A small accounting firm where junior staff can say "I do not understand this client's entries" catches problems before tax season. A youth sports organization where assistant coaches can raise concerns about a head coach's conduct protects children. In each case, the leader's daily responses determine whether the most important information in the building reaches the people who can act on it.

Key takeaways

  • Psychological safety is a shared team belief that interpersonal risk taking is safe. It is not niceness, comfort or low standards.
  • Safety and accountability are separate dimensions. The learning zone requires both; safety alone produces the comfort zone.
  • Healthy teams may report more errors because they surface them. Read reporting metrics with that paradox in mind.
  • People speak up when they believe it will matter (efficacy) and that it is safe. Acquiescent silence needs visible follow-through; defensive silence needs safer responses.
  • Trust is a willingness to be vulnerable to a specific party, built on perceived ability, benevolence and integrity and updated after every risk.
  • Supportive communication is descriptive, problem-focused, specific and owned. Aim for more positive than negative interactions, but distrust precise magic ratios.
  • In clinical and other high-stakes settings, tools such as SBAR, graded assertiveness, the two-challenge rule and briefings turn voice from courage into routine.
  • Leaders build safety by setting the stage, inviting participation and, above all, responding productively when people speak up.

Reflect and apply

  1. Place your current team on Figure 4.1. What specific recent events make you place it there, and what one change would move it toward the learning zone?
  2. Think of a time you stayed silent at work when you had a concern. Was it acquiescent, defensive or prosocial silence? What would have needed to be true for you to speak?
  3. Which implicit voice theories operate in your workplace? Write down two unwritten rules about speaking up and consider where they came from.
  4. Choose a high-stakes moment in your setting, such as a handoff, a client intake or a new exercise progression. Draft a short SBAR-style script or signal phrase your team could use to raise a concern in that moment.
