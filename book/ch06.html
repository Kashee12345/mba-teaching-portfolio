Servant and Transformational Leadership

Some leaders get compliance; others get commitment. This chapter explains the best-studied approaches to leading through purpose and service, what the evidence really shows and where the popular versions go wrong.

Learning objectives

  • Explain the difference between transactional and transformational leadership as described by Burns and Bass.
  • Identify the four I's of transformational leadership in examples of everyday leader behavior.
  • Compare servant, transformational and authentic leadership in their focus, strengths and limits.
  • Evaluate critiques of these models and assess which leadership behaviors fit a specific situation in your own work.

Marisol Vega took over as head athletic trainer for a fictional minor league baseball club, the Riverside Otters, in a season when morale was low. Her predecessor had been technically excellent and ran a tight ship: clear protocols, strict documentation, praise for hitting targets and sharp words for missing them. Nothing was broken, exactly, but the staff did the minimum. Marisol kept the protocols. What she added was a story about why the work mattered, time with each staff member about where they wanted to grow, and a habit of asking, "What would you do?" before giving her own answer. By midseason, two assistant trainers had proposed a new return-to-play tracking system on their own time, and players had started seeking out the training room rather than avoiding it. Marisol had not stopped managing. She had started leading in a way that researchers have studied for nearly fifty years.

From managing to leading

People often draw a sharp line between "managers" and "leaders," as if one were dull and the other inspiring. That contrast is overdrawn. In practice, anyone responsible for other people's work needs both sets of skills. Management, in plain terms, is about making complex work run reliably: planning, budgeting, organizing and controlling. Leadership is about influencing people toward shared goals, especially when those goals require change, extra effort or commitment beyond what any contract could spell out. John Kotter made a version of this distinction in 1990, and he was careful to say organizations need both.

This chapter focuses on a family of leadership approaches that emerged from the late 1970s onward and that put purpose, values and people's growth at the center. They go by different names, including transformational, servant and authentic leadership, and they overlap more than their fans sometimes admit. They also share a common rival: a purely exchange-based approach to leading, in which the leader trades rewards for performance. Understanding that rival first makes the others easier to see.

A word on how to read this chapter. Leadership research is a field where popular books run far ahead of the evidence. Many claims you will hear in workshops are sensible but untested, and some well-known models have weaker support than their fame suggests. Following the evidence-based management approach from Chapter 1, we will describe each model, what research supports, and where honest critics have pushed back.

Transactional leadership: the exchange at the core

In 1978, the political scientist James MacGregor Burns published Leadership, a Pulitzer Prize-winning study of political leaders. Burns distinguished two kinds of leadership. Transactional leadership is based on exchange: the leader offers something followers want, such as pay, a promotion, a vote or approval, in return for something the leader wants, such as effort or loyalty. Transforming leadership, by contrast, engages followers' higher needs and values so that both leader and followers are raised to greater motivation and morality.

In workplace terms, transactional leadership is simply making the deal clear and keeping your side of it. It shows up in three main forms, which later research by Bernard Bass and Bruce Avolio helped define.

  • Contingent reward: the leader clarifies what is expected and what people will receive when they deliver. "If the clinic hits ninety percent plan-of-care completion this quarter, the team gets the bonus pool." Done well, this is fair, motivating and clear.
  • Management by exception, active: the leader watches for mistakes and deviations and steps in to correct them. Think of a quality lead who audits charts every week.
  • Management by exception, passive: the leader waits until problems become serious before acting. "If it isn't on fire, don't bother me."

Beyond these lies laissez-faire leadership, which is really the absence of leadership: avoiding decisions, being unavailable, failing to respond when people need direction. Studies consistently find laissez-faire leadership among the most damaging patterns for employee satisfaction and wellbeing, and it is linked to more workplace conflict and bullying, because nobody is addressing problems.

It would be a mistake to treat transactional leadership as the villain. Contingent reward, in particular, has solid support. Clear expectations and fair, reliable follow-through are the foundation that more inspirational approaches build on. The limitation is that exchange alone rarely produces the discretionary effort, creativity and commitment that organizations increasingly need. People do what they are paid to do, and not much more.

Transformational leadership and the four I's

In 1985, Bernard Bass took Burns's idea into organizational psychology in his book Leadership and Performance Beyond Expectations. Bass renamed it transformational leadership and made an important change. Where Burns saw transactional and transforming leadership as opposite ends of a single line, Bass argued they are separate dimensions. The best leaders do both: they set clear exchanges and also inspire people to go beyond them. Bass called this the augmentation effect: transformational behavior adds to, rather than replaces, the effect of good transactional behavior.

Working with Bruce Avolio, Bass described transformational leadership through four components, widely known as the four I's.

Idealized influence

The leader acts as a role model whom followers respect and trust. This includes behaving consistently with stated values, putting the group's needs ahead of personal gain and taking responsibility when things go wrong. Plainly: people see the leader walking the talk. This component overlaps with what is often called charisma, and early versions of the model used that term.

Inspirational motivation

The leader communicates an appealing vision and high expectations, and expresses confidence that the team can meet them. This is not about speeches. It is about helping people see why their work matters. A cardiac rehab director who reminds staff that each completed program means fewer readmissions and more grandparents at graduations is using inspirational motivation.

Intellectual stimulation

The leader encourages people to question assumptions, reframe problems and try new approaches, without public criticism when ideas fail. Marisol's habit of asking "What would you do?" before giving her own view is intellectual stimulation. So is a gym owner asking coaches to challenge the way class programming has always been done.

Individualized consideration

The leader pays attention to each person's needs for achievement and growth, acting as a coach or mentor. This means listening, delegating in ways that develop people and recognizing that different people need different kinds of support. It echoes the self-determination theory needs of competence and relatedness from Chapter 3.

The full range leadership model

Bass and Avolio arranged all these behaviors into what they called the full range leadership model. It runs from laissez-faire at one end, through the passive and active forms of management by exception, to contingent reward and then the four I's. The model predicts that behaviors further along the range are both more active and more effective. It has been measured most often with a commercial questionnaire, the Multifactor Leadership Questionnaire, in which followers rate how often their leader shows each behavior.

What the evidence says

A widely cited 2004 meta-analysis by Timothy Judge and Ronald Piccolo combined results from well over a hundred studies. Transformational leadership showed substantial positive relationships with follower satisfaction, motivation and leader effectiveness ratings, with an average corrected correlation in the .40s. Contingent reward was nearly as strongly related to many outcomes, a finding that surprised some advocates and supports Bass's point that good exchange matters. Laissez-faire leadership was clearly negative. Two limits are important. First, most studies are correlational and many measure leadership and outcomes from the same raters at the same time, which inflates relationships. Second, relationships with objective performance were generally weaker than with satisfaction and ratings. Some field experiments, in which leaders were trained in transformational behaviors and their units compared with control groups, have found improvements in follower commitment and performance, which strengthens the causal case modestly.

The dark side of inspiration

Inspirational leadership can be used for harmful ends. Bass himself later distinguished authentic transformational leadership from pseudo-transformational leadership, in which a leader uses the appearance of vision and charisma to serve personal power rather than the group. Jay Conger's writing on the dark side of charismatic leadership in 1990 described leaders whose visions reflected personal ambition, who managed impressions rather than realities and who tolerated no dissent. In health and fitness businesses, this can look like a founder whose inspiring mission masks unsafe workloads, or a charismatic coach whose players are afraid to report injuries. Values and accountability, not charisma, are what keep inspiration honest.

Can transformational leadership be learned?

If transformational leadership were simply a personality trait, it would matter for hiring but not for development. The evidence suggests it is at least partly learnable. In a well-known 1996 field experiment, Julian Barling, Tom Weber and Kevin Kelloway randomly assigned bank branch managers either to transformational leadership training, a one-day workshop followed by individual booster sessions, or to a control group. Months later, employees of trained managers reported higher organizational commitment and saw their managers as more transformational, and the trained managers' branches showed better results on some financial measures. The study was small, and one experiment is never the last word, but later training studies in settings from the military to health care have found similar, if modest, effects.

The settings that readers of this book work in have their own evidence. Systematic reviews of nursing leadership, such as those led by Greta Cummings in 2010 and 2018, found that relationally focused leadership styles, including transformational leadership, were associated with higher nurse job satisfaction, lower intention to leave and healthier work environments, while task-focused styles alone were associated with poorer outcomes. In sport, studies of coaches have linked transformational coaching behaviors with athlete satisfaction, effort and team cohesion. As in the broader literature, most of these studies are correlational, but the pattern is consistent enough to take seriously.

Case

Two directors, one merger. When two fictional home health agencies merged, each kept its clinical director for the transition year. Director A gave rousing all-staff talks about "becoming the best home health team in the state" but rarely met with individual nurses and did not change the confusing new scheduling rules. Director B gave fewer speeches. She held short one-on-ones with every nurse, fixed the scheduling rules within a month, explained honestly what the merger meant for travel time and invited nurses to redesign the patient handoff form. A year later, turnover on Director B's side was less than half of Director A's. Director A had borrowed the language of inspirational motivation; Director B practiced all four I's and got the transactional basics right.

Servant leadership: leading by serving

In 1970, Robert Greenleaf, a retired management researcher who had spent decades at AT&T, published an essay titled The Servant as Leader. Greenleaf was inspired by a novel by Hermann Hesse in which a humble servant on a journey turns out to be the group's true leader. His central idea was that great leaders are servants first. Their primary motivation is to serve others, and leadership grows out of that. Greenleaf offered a test that has become famous in paraphrase: do those served grow as persons, becoming healthier, wiser, freer and more likely themselves to serve? And what is the effect on the least privileged in society?

Plainly put, servant leadership is an approach in which the leader's first priority is the growth and wellbeing of followers and the communities the organization touches, with performance treated as an outcome of that care rather than the leader's primary aim.

What servant leaders do

Greenleaf wrote essays, not a measurement model, so later scholars had to translate his ideas into behaviors. Larry Spears, who led the Greenleaf Center, drew ten characteristics from Greenleaf's writing in the 1990s: listening, empathy, healing, awareness, persuasion, conceptualization, foresight, stewardship, commitment to the growth of people and building community. Researchers such as Robert Liden and colleagues, in 2008, developed validated measures that capture behaviors like helping subordinates grow and succeed, putting subordinates first, behaving ethically, emotional healing, empowering and creating value for the community.

In a clinic, servant leadership looks like a practice owner who takes the least popular shift during a staffing crunch, who asks each clinician "What do you need from me to do your best work?" and actually acts on the answer, and who measures her success partly by how many of her staff go on to lead elsewhere. In a sports organization, it looks like a head coach who publicly credits assistants, protects players' long-term health over short-term wins and invests in the local youth program even when it does not help this season's record.

What the evidence says

For years servant leadership was more philosophy than science. That has changed. A 2019 systematic review by Nathan Eva and colleagues examined hundreds of studies and concluded that servant leadership is linked to follower outcomes such as engagement, organizational citizenship behavior, commitment and helping, and to team and service outcomes. Importantly, a 2018 meta-analysis by Julia Hoch and colleagues compared servant, authentic and ethical leadership with transformational leadership. Authentic and ethical leadership overlapped so heavily with transformational leadership that they added little unique predictive value. Servant leadership overlapped less and explained additional variance in outcomes beyond transformational leadership, suggesting it captures something distinctive. As with most leadership research, many studies rely on surveys at a single point in time, so causal conclusions remain cautious.

How servant leadership differs from transformational leadership

Because both approaches care about followers, people often ask whether servant leadership is just transformational leadership with a humbler name. Scholars such as Gregory Stone, Robert Russell and Kathleen Patterson argued in 2004 that the difference lies in the leader's focus. A transformational leader develops followers mainly so that they can achieve the organization's vision. A servant leader treats the followers' growth as an end in itself and trusts that good performance will follow. The difference sounds subtle, but it shows up in hard moments. When a talented assistant coach is offered a head coaching job elsewhere, a leader focused on the organization's goals may see a loss to be prevented. A servant leader is more likely to see a success to be celebrated.

One of the more convincing studies of how servant leadership works in business came from Robert Liden and colleagues in 2014. Studying a chain of restaurants, they found that servant leadership by restaurant managers was associated with a serving culture in the restaurant, a shared norm of putting customers' and colleagues' needs first, which in turn was linked to better restaurant performance, stronger employee identification with the restaurant and lower turnover intentions. The key insight for small business owners is that servant leadership may work less through one-to-one kindness and more through the climate it builds: people copy what their leader models.

Case

Northgate Family Chiropractic. The fictional owner of a three-clinician practice noticed that her front-desk staff turned over every year. Instead of raising pay alone, she began a monthly "what's in your way" meeting where staff named obstacles and she took responsibility for removing at least one before the next meeting. She paid for a front-desk lead to complete a practice management certificate and gave her authority over the scheduling system. Within eighteen months, turnover had stopped, and the front-desk lead had trained two new hires using the same approach. The owner described her job as "making the people who serve patients able to do it well," which is Greenleaf's idea in plain clothes.

Criticisms of servant leadership

Servant leadership faces thoughtful objections. The name itself strikes some people as a contradiction, and in some cultures "servant" carries connotations that make the label hard to use. Early definitions were loose and varied from author to author, which made research hard to compare. Some critics argue that a leader who always puts followers first may neglect hard performance decisions or organizational survival, and there is growing interest in the personal cost to leaders who give constantly without replenishing themselves, a theme we return to in Chapter 10. Finally, "servant" language can be misused by leaders who expect staff to serve without limit while calling it a shared calling. Servant leadership done well includes boundaries, accountability and honest performance conversations.

Common mistake

Serving by rescuing. New leaders who embrace servant leadership sometimes interpret "serve your people" as "do their hard work for them" or "shield them from all feedback." A clinic manager who quietly covers a struggling therapist's documentation, never raising the issue, is not serving that therapist's growth; she is preventing it, and she is shifting unfairness onto the rest of the team. Greenleaf's test is whether people grow. Sometimes serving someone means a difficult, caring conversation about performance, followed by real support to improve.

Authentic leadership: knowing and being yourself

In the early 2000s, a group of scholars influenced by positive psychology, including Fred Luthans, Bruce Avolio and William Gardner, proposed authentic leadership as a root concept beneath positive forms of leadership. The executive Bill George popularized the idea in a 2003 book of the same name. The plain-language idea: authentic leaders know who they are, what they value and how they come across, and they act consistently with that self-knowledge, openly and fairly.

In a 2008 paper, Fred Walumbwa and colleagues described four components that became the standard research definition.

  • Self-awareness: understanding your own strengths, weaknesses, values and impact on others. This connects directly to Chapter 2.
  • Relational transparency: presenting your genuine self, sharing information and feelings appropriately rather than hiding behind a role.
  • Balanced processing: seeking out and fairly considering views that challenge your own before making decisions.
  • Internalized moral perspective: being guided by your own moral standards rather than by group or organizational pressure alone.

The authenticity paradox

Authentic leadership is appealing, but it raises hard questions. What if your "true self" is impatient, conflict-avoidant or introverted in ways that hurt your team? Herminia Ibarra has written about what she calls the authenticity paradox: leaders who cling too tightly to a fixed sense of "this is just who I am" can resist the very growth that new roles require. Growing as a leader often feels inauthentic at first, because you are trying behaviors that are not yet natural. A more useful view treats authenticity as consistency with your values while staying open to changing your habits.

The research base also has specific problems. Critics such as Mats Alvesson and Katja Einola, in 2019, argued that the concept is philosophically muddled and too positive, since it assumes that a leader's "true self" will be good. Measures of authentic leadership overlap heavily with transformational and ethical leadership, as the Hoch meta-analysis found. And some influential studies in this literature were later corrected or retracted after concerns about data, a reminder to weigh any single study cautiously and to look for replications.

Fit, context and the critiques that matter

Is one style best everywhere?

The models in this chapter can sound universal: be inspiring, be a servant, be authentic, everywhere and always. Older traditions in leadership research argued the opposite, that effective leadership depends on the situation. Fred Fiedler's contingency model in the 1960s proposed that whether a task-focused or relationship-focused leader performs better depends on how favorable the situation is. Robert House's path-goal theory, first published in 1971, proposed that leaders should supply whatever followers and tasks lack, such as direction when the task is unclear or support when it is stressful. Paul Hersey and Kenneth Blanchard's situational leadership model, from the late 1960s, suggested adjusting directive and supportive behavior to followers' readiness.

These ideas remain intuitive and are widely taught. Their evidence is uneven. Situational leadership in particular is popular in training, but independent tests, such as a 2009 study by Robert Thompson and Robert Vecchio, have found limited support for its specific prescriptions. What survives from the situational tradition is a sound general principle: the same behavior can help in one setting and hinder in another.

Practical examples of situational fit are easy to find. In an emergency department during a mass-casualty event, clear direction matters more than intellectual stimulation; asking "What do you think?" at the wrong moment wastes seconds. A brand-new front-desk hire needs more structure and more frequent feedback than a ten-year veteran. A team of highly experienced specialists may need a leader who clears obstacles and then gets out of the way. Steven Kerr and John Jermier, in 1978, even described substitutes for leadership: features of the task, the people or the organization, such as strong professional training or clear procedures, that make some leader behaviors unnecessary.

Culture and context

The large GLOBE study of leadership across dozens of societies, led by Robert House and published in 2004, found that some attributes associated with charismatic, value-based leadership, such as being visionary, trustworthy and inspiring, were widely endorsed across cultures as contributing to outstanding leadership. But how those qualities are expressed differs. In some settings, a leader who solicits open challenge from juniors is seen as strong; in others, the same behavior may be read as indecisive. Leaders of diverse teams, including many clinics and sports organizations with international staff, benefit from asking what good leadership looks like to the people they lead, rather than assuming.

The big critiques of the transformational family

In 2013, Daan van Knippenberg and Sim Sitkin published a sharp critique of charismatic-transformational leadership research. Their key point was that the concept is often defined by its effects: a transformational leader is someone who inspires followers, and then research "finds" that transformational leaders inspire followers. That circularity makes it hard to know which specific behaviors cause which outcomes. They also argued that the four dimensions are lumped together without a clear theory of how each works, and that common survey measures cannot cleanly separate them. Their advice was to study specific, well-defined behaviors, such as communicating a vision or providing individualized support, rather than a global label.

A second critique is the heroic bias: the tendency of leadership models, and of organizations, to credit or blame the individual at the top for outcomes that are really shaped by teams, systems and luck. Research on the "romance of leadership," associated with James Meindl in the 1980s, showed that people attribute outcomes to leaders more strongly than evidence justifies, especially when outcomes are extreme. Chapter 5 showed how much team performance depends on design and context. A leader who believes that personal inspiration is the whole story will underinvest in structure.

These critiques do not mean the models are useless. They mean that you should treat them as a helpful set of behaviors to practice, not as personality types or labels, and that you should keep the transactional basics and the system in view.

Try it this week

Choose one of the four I's that you use least. Plan one specific behavior for the coming week. For intellectual stimulation, ask a team member to propose a solution before you give yours, and then use part of their idea. For individualized consideration, hold a fifteen-minute conversation with one person about where they want to grow in the next year. For inspirational motivation, open a meeting with a concrete story of how the team's work helped a patient, client or athlete. At the end of the week, write down what happened and what you would do differently.

Building your own leadership approach

After a tour of models, it is natural to ask: which one should I adopt? The honest answer from the research is that you do not need to pick a brand. The approaches in this chapter share a core set of behaviors that are consistently linked to better outcomes, and they each add a useful emphasis.

A practical synthesis

Get the transactional basics right. Clear expectations, fair rewards and prompt, respectful correction are the floor. Without them, inspiration feels hollow. Director B in the merger case succeeded partly because she fixed the scheduling rules.

Connect work to purpose. From transformational leadership, take the habit of explaining why the work matters in concrete, human terms. In health and wellness settings, the purpose is usually easy to find; leaders simply forget to say it out loud.

Develop people deliberately. From both servant and transformational leadership, take the habit of investing in each person's growth. Ask about goals, delegate stretch work and give strengths-based feedback of the kind described in Chapter 3.

Invite challenge. Intellectual stimulation and balanced processing both mean seeking out views that differ from yours. Combined with the psychological safety practices of Chapter 4, this protects you from the dark side of charisma and from your own blind spots.

Know and govern yourself. From authentic leadership, take self-awareness and consistency with your values, while staying open to growing beyond your current habits.

Read the situation. From the contingency tradition, take the habit of adjusting how much direction and support you give based on the task, the person and the moment.

Remember the system. From the critiques, take humility. Your behavior matters, but team design, organizational context and culture matter too. The next chapter examines culture directly, including how leaders shape it through what they pay attention to and reward.

Marisol, revisited

Look again at the opening story. Marisol kept her predecessor's protocols and fair rewards, which is contingent reward and active monitoring. She added a story about why the work mattered (inspirational motivation), one-on-one conversations about growth (individualized consideration) and the "What would you do?" habit (intellectual stimulation). Her consistency and willingness to admit what she did not know built trust (idealized influence and relational transparency). The two assistant trainers who built a new tracking system were showing the discretionary effort that exchange alone rarely buys. None of it required charisma in the movie sense. It required a set of learnable behaviors practiced consistently, which is the most encouraging conclusion this body of research offers.

Key takeaways

  • Burns distinguished transactional leadership, based on exchange, from transforming leadership, which raises followers' motivation and values; Bass reframed these as complementary dimensions.
  • Transformational leadership consists of four I's: idealized influence, inspirational motivation, intellectual stimulation and individualized consideration.
  • Contingent reward is well supported and forms the foundation; laissez-faire leadership is consistently harmful.
  • Servant leadership, from Greenleaf, puts followers' growth first and shows predictive value beyond transformational leadership in recent meta-analytic work.
  • Authentic leadership emphasizes self-awareness, transparency, balanced processing and internal moral standards, but overlaps heavily with other models and faces conceptual and data concerns.
  • Situational models are intuitive but have uneven evidence; the durable lesson is that behaviors must fit the task, people and moment.
  • Major critiques include circular definitions, heroic bias and the dark side of charisma; treat these models as sets of learnable behaviors rather than labels.

Reflect and apply

  1. Think of the best leader you have worked for. Which of the four I's did that person show most clearly? Give one concrete example of their behavior.
  2. Where in your workplace are the transactional basics, such as clear expectations and fair follow-through, weakest? What would fixing them make possible?
  3. Apply Greenleaf's test to your own leadership: in the past year, which of the people you lead have grown, and what did you do that helped or hindered that growth?
  4. Describe one situation at work where your natural leadership style fits poorly. What behavior would fit better, and what makes it hard for you to use?
