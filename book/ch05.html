Teams That Work: Development, Roles and Conflict

Great teams are rarely an accident. This chapter shows you how teams grow, what conditions let them succeed, and how to turn disagreement into better work instead of damaged relationships.

Learning objectives

  • Describe the Tuckman and Jensen stages of group development and explain how the punctuated equilibrium model refines them.
  • Apply Hackman's enabling conditions to diagnose why a real team in your workplace is or is not performing.
  • Differentiate task, relationship and process conflict, and select a conflict-handling approach that fits a given situation.
  • Design a team charter that addresses the specific risks of virtual and hybrid work.

The physical therapy clinic at Harbor Point had six talented clinicians, a skilled front-desk lead and a practice manager who genuinely cared. Yet every Monday huddle felt like a small war. Two senior therapists argued about scheduling blocks, the newest clinician never spoke, and the front desk quietly rebooked patients to avoid friction. Nobody on the team lacked ability. What they lacked was a shared picture of what the team was for, clear agreements about how decisions got made, and a way to disagree about the work without it becoming personal. Within four months, with the same people and no new hires, Harbor Point's huddles were short, its no-show rate had dropped and the newest clinician was running a case review each week. This chapter is about what changed, and why.

What makes a group a team

In everyday speech we call almost any collection of coworkers a "team." The front office is a team, the Tuesday spin instructors are a team, the whole company is "one big team." For leadership purposes it helps to be more precise, because the tools that make a real team work are different from the tools that coordinate a loose group.

A useful plain-language definition is this: a team is a small number of people who share a common purpose, depend on one another to get the work done, and hold themselves mutually accountable for the result. Jon Katzenbach and Douglas Smith popularized a version of this definition in the early 1990s, and it has held up well. The key word is depend. Researchers call this interdependence, meaning the degree to which each person's work relies on what others do.

Consider two settings. In a group fitness studio, each instructor teaches a separate class. They share a brand and a schedule, but a great Wednesday yoga class does not depend on the Tuesday kickboxing instructor. That is a working group: people who coordinate and share information but whose results are mostly individual. Now consider a surgical team, a basketball squad or the care team in a small cardiac rehab program. The outcome is jointly produced. If the nurse misses a warning sign, the physician's plan fails, and the exercise physiologist's session may put the patient at risk. That is a real team in the sense that matters here.

The distinction matters because building a team has costs in meetings, shared decisions and conflict. If the work is not truly interdependent, forcing people into team structures slows them down. A good leader first asks, "Does this work actually need a team?" and only then invests in building one.

Why teams can outperform individuals, and why they often do not

Teams bring together different knowledge, skills and viewpoints. That diversity of information can produce better decisions and more creative solutions than any single member could reach. This is the promise of synergy, when the whole is greater than the sum of its parts.

But teams also suffer process losses, the performance a team leaves on the table because of coordination problems and motivation problems. Ivan Steiner described this idea in 1972, and the concept remains central. Coordination losses happen when people duplicate work, wait on each other or miscommunicate. Motivation losses include social loafing, the tendency to put in less effort when individual contributions are hard to see. Bibb Latané and colleagues demonstrated the effect in the late 1970s, and a large meta-analysis by Steven Karau and Kipling Williams in 1993 confirmed that loafing is common but shrinks when individual contributions are identifiable, when the task is meaningful and when people value the group.

The practical lesson is that a team's actual performance equals its potential minus its process losses. Much of team leadership is simply the craft of reducing those losses while protecting the diversity that creates the potential in the first place.

How teams develop over time

Anyone who has joined a new team knows the early weeks feel different from the later months. People are polite at first, then tensions surface, then routines settle in. Bruce Tuckman captured this pattern in a 1965 review of studies, many of them from therapy and training groups. He proposed four stages with memorable rhyming names. In 1977, Tuckman and Mary Ann Jensen revisited the literature and added a fifth stage.

The five stages

Forming is the orientation stage. Members are figuring out what the task is, who everyone is and what behavior is acceptable. People tend to be cautious and to look to the leader for direction. In a new clinic team, this looks like careful introductions and questions about "how things work here."

Storming is the stage of friction. Once people feel safe enough to show their real opinions, disagreements surface about goals, roles, methods and influence. Storming is uncomfortable, and many leaders try to suppress it. That is usually a mistake. Teams that never storm often never really commit, because the disagreements are simply pushed underground.

Norming is when the team settles on shared expectations. Members agree, explicitly or implicitly, on how to communicate, make decisions and handle problems. Cohesion grows. A norm is an informal rule the group enforces, such as "we start huddles on time" or "we never criticize a colleague in front of a patient."

Performing is the stage of focused, effective work. Energy goes into the task rather than into managing relationships. Members adapt roles fluidly and solve problems together.

Adjourning, added in 1977, describes the ending of a temporary team: wrapping up the work, recognizing contributions and dealing with the sense of loss that can come when a good team disbands. Project teams, seasonal sports staffs and short-term task forces all adjourn. Handled well, adjourning captures lessons for the next team. Handled poorly, it leaves people feeling used.

What the stage model gets right and wrong

The Tuckman model is popular because it normalizes friction. When a manager tells a new team, "Some disagreement right now is expected, and it is part of how we get good," people relax. That alone is valuable.

The model also has real limits. Tuckman's original review drew heavily on therapy and laboratory groups rather than workplace teams, and he said so. Real teams do not always move neatly from stage to stage; some storm again when membership changes, and many teams with deadlines follow a different rhythm entirely.

That different rhythm was documented by Connie Gersick in 1988. Studying project teams from their first meeting to their deadline, she found that teams quickly settled on an approach, often in the very first meeting, and stuck with it through a long first phase. Then, remarkably close to the halfway point of their allotted time, most teams went through a burst of change: they dropped old patterns, sought outside input and adopted a new approach that carried them to completion. Gersick called this punctuated equilibrium, borrowing a term from evolutionary biology.

For leaders, the two models are complementary, not competing. The stage model reminds you that relationships and norms must be worked out. Gersick's model reminds you that first meetings matter enormously, because the early pattern tends to lock in, and that the midpoint is a natural moment to step back and ask, "Is our approach working?"

Case

The eight-week launch at Summit Strength. Summit Strength, a fictional regional gym chain, formed a five-person team to launch a new youth sports conditioning program in eight weeks. The first meeting was rushed; the team defaulted to the marketing lead's plan for a big launch event. For three weeks everyone worked hard on that plan. In week four, the operations manager scheduled a "halftime review" and invited two local high school coaches. Their feedback was blunt: parents wanted small trial sessions, not an event. The team pivoted, and the program launched with a waitlist. The team leader later said that the most important decision was simply scheduling the halftime review in advance, rather than hoping someone would raise doubts.

The conditions for team success

If you asked most managers what makes a team succeed, they would talk about the leader's behavior in meetings: encouraging people, running good discussions, resolving disputes. Richard Hackman, a Harvard social psychologist who studied teams for four decades, argued that this focus is often misplaced. In his 2002 book Leading Teams, he proposed that team effectiveness depends mostly on a set of conditions that the leader puts in place, many of them before the team even meets.

Hackman defined an effective team using three criteria, not one. First, the team's output meets or exceeds the standards of the people who receive it, such as patients, clients or the next department. Second, the team becomes more capable of working together over time rather than burning itself out. Third, the experience contributes to the learning and wellbeing of individual members. A team that hits its numbers while exhausting and alienating everyone is not, in Hackman's view, fully effective.

The five enabling conditions

1. A real team. The work is genuinely interdependent, membership is clear (people know who is on the team) and membership is reasonably stable. Teams with fuzzy or constantly changing membership struggle to build shared routines.

2. Compelling direction. The team's purpose is clear, challenging and consequential. Clear means people can describe it; challenging means it stretches them; consequential means it matters to someone. Hackman emphasized that leaders should be firm about the ends, the purpose, while giving the team latitude about the means.

3. An enabling structure. This includes a well-designed task, the right mix and number of members, and a small set of clear norms of conduct. Structure is where many teams quietly fail: too many members, a task split into disconnected pieces, or no agreement on how decisions will be made.

4. A supportive organizational context. The team has the information, training and material resources it needs, and the reward system recognizes team performance rather than only individual stars. A clinic team told to "work together" while every clinician is paid solely on individual visit counts is being pulled in two directions.

5. Expert coaching. Coaching here means helping the team use its collective resources well. Hackman argued coaching works best when timed to the team's natural rhythms: motivational coaching at the start, strategy coaching near the midpoint (echoing Gersick) and learning-focused coaching at the end.

What the evidence says

Hackman and his colleague Ruth Wageman offered a memorable rule of thumb from their field studies, sometimes called the 60-30-10 rule: roughly 60 percent of the difference in how well teams perform is set by prework and design, about 30 percent by how the team is launched, and only about 10 percent by coaching once work is underway. These percentages are an informed heuristic from their research, not a precise law, and later writers have debated them. Still, the direction of the finding has been echoed across studies of teams as different as flight crews, senior leadership teams and intelligence analysts: structural conditions predict team performance more strongly than a leader's in-the-moment style. In 2016, Martine Haas and Mark Mortensen argued that modern teams that are dispersed, digital and dynamic also need a fourth essential condition, a shared mindset, meaning a common identity and common understanding among members.

At Harbor Point, the practice manager started with structure, not speeches. She clarified the purpose as "every patient finishes their plan of care," agreed three norms with the team, including a weekly ten-minute slot for scheduling disputes, and added team-level recognition for plan-of-care completion. Only then did her coaching start to stick.

Roles, size and who does what

A role is a set of expected behaviors attached to a position on a team. Some roles are formal, written into a job description: the head athletic trainer, the charge nurse, the bookkeeper. Others are informal and emerge from how people behave: the person who always summarizes a discussion, the one who checks on how everyone is doing, the one who plays devil's advocate.

Task roles and maintenance roles

As early as 1948, Kenneth Benne and Paul Sheats sorted informal team roles into three families. Task roles move the work forward: proposing ideas, seeking information, summarizing and testing decisions. Maintenance roles, sometimes called relationship or social roles, keep the group healthy: encouraging, harmonizing, gatekeeping so quieter members get airtime and easing tension. A third family, which they called individual roles, serves a member's personal needs at the group's expense, such as dominating, blocking or seeking recognition.

Healthy teams need both task and maintenance roles, and the roles do not have to belong to one person permanently. In a strong team, members notice what is missing and step in. If a discussion is drifting, someone summarizes. If a quiet colleague has not spoken, someone invites her in. Leaders can make this explicit by saying, "We have lots of ideas on the table; could someone pull them together?"

Belbin's team roles, and a caution

Meredith Belbin's work in the 1970s and 1980s, based on management team exercises in the United Kingdom, proposed that effective teams need a balance of behavioral contributions, such as someone who generates ideas, someone who coordinates, someone who drives, someone who evaluates carefully and someone who finishes details. It gives people a friendly language for their differences, but treat it as a conversation tool rather than a scientific classification: independent studies have questioned the psychometric quality of its self-report inventory, and evidence that a "balanced" role mix causes better performance is mixed. The deeper idea, that teams benefit when members value their different contributions, fits well with the strengths-based approach from Chapter 2.

Role clarity and role conflict

Two role problems show up again and again. Role ambiguity occurs when people are unsure what is expected of them. Role conflict occurs when expectations clash, either between two people's demands (the owner wants speed, the clinical director wants thoroughness) or between two roles held by the same person (a trainer who is also the gym's salesperson). Both are consistently linked in research to stress, lower satisfaction and weaker performance. A simple remedy is a responsibility chart that lists key decisions and marks, for each, who does the work, who decides, who must be consulted and who simply needs to be informed. Many organizations use a template of this kind; the value lies less in the template than in the conversation it forces.

Team size

Bigger is not better. Each added member adds communication links, and the number of links grows much faster than the number of people. A team of five has ten pairwise links; a team of ten has forty-five. Larger teams also make social loafing easier. Hackman often suggested that teams should rarely exceed about ten members and that many perform best somewhat smaller. When more people need input, a small core team plus a wider circle of advisors is usually more effective than one large team.

Conflict: the useful and the corrosive

Conflict is simply the perception that one party's interests, ideas or goals are incompatible with another's. In teams it is inevitable, because the diversity that makes teams valuable also produces disagreement. The leadership question is not how to avoid conflict but which kinds to encourage, which to prevent and how to handle each.

Three types of team conflict

Karen Jehn's research in the mid-1990s distinguished conflict by what it is about. Task conflict is disagreement about the content of the work: which treatment protocol to use, which marketing channel to fund, what the data mean. Relationship conflict is interpersonal friction: dislike, annoyance, personal clashes and feelings of disrespect. Jehn later added process conflict, disagreement about how the work will be done: who does what, schedules and how resources are divided. Researchers have since described a fourth type, status conflict, which involves disputes over people's relative standing in the group.

The early hope was simple: task conflict is good, relationship conflict is bad. The evidence turned out to be more interesting.

What the evidence says

A 2003 meta-analysis by Carsten De Dreu and Laurie Weingart found that, on average, both task conflict and relationship conflict were negatively related to team performance and member satisfaction. That surprised many people. A larger 2012 meta-analysis by Frank de Wit, Lindred Greer and Karen Jehn refined the picture: relationship and process conflict were reliably harmful, but task conflict was not consistently harmful and was positively related to performance under certain conditions, especially when it was not tangled up with relationship conflict and in groups such as top management teams making complex decisions. A key practical finding across studies is that task and relationship conflict tend to travel together. A debate about the work can easily be heard as a personal attack, and once that happens, the benefits of task conflict disappear.

So the goal is to keep task conflict "clean." That requires psychological safety, which we examined in Chapter 4, and skills for separating the problem from the person. Phrases such as "I see the data differently; can I show you why?" or "Help me understand what you are weighing" keep attention on the work. Norms such as "argue hard about ideas, assume good intentions about people" make the expectation explicit.

Conflict-handling styles

People differ in how they respond when their concerns clash with someone else's. A widely used way to describe these responses comes from Robert Blake and Jane Mouton's managerial grid in the 1960s, developed further by Kenneth Thomas and Ralph Kilmann in the 1970s. Their framework uses two dimensions. Assertiveness is how much you try to satisfy your own concerns. Cooperativeness is how much you try to satisfy the other person's concerns. Crossing the two produces five broad styles.

  • Competing (high assertiveness, low cooperativeness): pressing for your own position. Useful in emergencies or when an unpopular but necessary decision must be made, such as enforcing an infection-control rule.
  • Accommodating (low assertiveness, high cooperativeness): yielding to the other party. Useful when the issue matters much more to them, or when you realize you are wrong.
  • Avoiding (low on both): sidestepping or postponing. Useful for trivial issues or when emotions need to cool, harmful when important problems are left to fester.
  • Collaborating (high on both): working to find a solution that fully satisfies both parties. Best for important issues where both sets of concerns are too important to compromise, but it takes time and trust.
  • Compromising (moderate on both): each side gives up something. Useful for quick, workable solutions when goals are moderately important and power is roughly balanced.

These styles are described here conceptually. Several commercial assessments measure them, but the core insight does not require any instrument: no style is best in every situation, and most people overuse one or two. The skill is flexibility, matching the style to the stakes, the relationship and the time available.

Common mistake

Confusing harmony with health. Many leaders, especially in caring professions, read a quiet team as a healthy team. In reality, a lack of visible disagreement may mean avoiding has become the team's default style. Warning signs include decisions that are agreed in the meeting and undermined in the parking lot, side conversations after huddles and a sudden flood of complaints when someone finally leaves. Irving Janis's classic work on groupthink in the early 1970s described how cohesive groups can suppress dissent in pursuit of agreement; while later research questioned parts of his model, the risk of premature consensus is well documented. Invite dissent on purpose: ask "What would make this fail?" before you close a decision.

When a conflict needs active handling, one idea from Roger Fisher and William Ury's work on principled negotiation in the early 1980s is especially useful: separate interests from positions. A position is what someone says they want ("I need Tuesday afternoons blocked"); an interest is why ("my pediatric caseload only comes after school"). Frame the issue as a shared problem, look for options that meet the most important interests on both sides, and set a date to check whether the agreement is working.

Virtual and hybrid teams

Many teams now work partly or entirely at a distance. A multi-site physiotherapy group coordinates by video, a national sports analytics staff spans three time zones, and even a small wellness studio may have a remote bookkeeper and marketing contractor. Research on virtual teams long predates the recent shift to remote work, and its findings are practical.

What changes when a team is not in the same room

Virtual work is not simply in-person work over a screen. Researchers such as Luis Martins, Lucy Gilson and Travis Maynard, in a 2004 review, identified several persistent challenges. Communication loses many cues: tone, posture and the small signals that tell you a colleague is confused or upset. Information is spread unevenly, so each member may hold a different picture of the situation. Informal interaction, the chat at the coffee machine that quietly builds trust, largely disappears unless it is designed back in.

Richard Daft and Robert Lengel's media richness idea, from the mid-1980s, offers a useful rule of thumb. Rich media, like face-to-face conversation or video, carry many cues and allow instant feedback, so they suit ambiguous or emotionally charged topics. Lean media, like email or a task board, suit routine and clear information. A common error is delivering critical feedback in a chat message.

Trust at a distance

Temporary and virtual teams often have to trust each other before they have a history together. Debra Meyerson, Karl Weick and Roderick Kramer called this swift trust in 1996: a provisional trust based on roles and professional expectations rather than personal relationships. Later studies of virtual teams found that swift trust can form quickly but is fragile. It holds up when early communication is frequent, responsive and task-focused, and when members follow through on small commitments. It breaks down quickly when people go silent or miss early deadlines.

The special risks of hybrid teams

Hybrid teams, where some members are together and others are remote, have their own problems. The in-person group can form an inner circle without meaning to. Decisions get made in hallway conversations that remote colleagues never hear about. Researchers have described this pattern with the idea of faultlines, first proposed by Dora Lau and Keith Murnighan in 1998: hypothetical dividing lines that split a team into subgroups when several member attributes line up. When location lines up with other differences, such as role, seniority or tenure, subgroup divides grow sharper. There is also the risk often called proximity bias, the tendency of leaders to rate and reward the people they see most, which can quietly penalize remote staff.

Practical countermeasures include a "one remote, all remote" rule for key meetings, a single written decision record everyone can access, rotating meeting times across time zones and deliberately scheduled informal connection.

Case

Clearwater Wellness Group. Clearwater, a fictional group of four integrative health clinics, formed a hybrid quality committee. Members at the main clinic met in a conference room while others dialed in. Within two months, remote members were contributing less and several stopped attending. An anonymous check-in revealed the problem: the room group talked over each other, the remote audio was poor, and decisions often got finalized "after the call." The committee chair moved to a rule that everyone joined individually by video, added a shared decision log and asked a different member to facilitate each month. Attendance recovered, and the committee produced its first cross-site protocol within the quarter.

Launching well: team charters

Gersick's research showed that early patterns tend to lock in. Hackman argued that the launch is where a large share of a team's future performance is set. Put those together and you arrive at one of the most practical tools in this book: the team charter, a short written agreement, created by the team itself, that captures its purpose, goals, roles and ways of working.

What a good charter contains

A charter need not be long. One or two pages is usually enough. A strong charter answers a handful of questions:

  • Purpose: Why does this team exist, and for whom? State it in one or two sentences that a new member could remember.
  • Goals and measures: What will success look like in concrete terms, and by when? This links directly to the goal-setting research in Chapter 3.
  • Membership and roles: Who is on the team, who leads what, and who decides which kinds of issues?
  • Decision rules: Will the team decide by consensus, by majority, or by a designated person after consultation? Different decisions may use different rules.
  • Communication norms: Which channels are used for what, how quickly people should respond and when meetings happen. For virtual and hybrid teams this section is essential.
  • Conflict norms: How will disagreements be raised and resolved? Who can a member go to if they cannot resolve an issue directly?
  • Accountability: How will the team check progress, and what happens if someone repeatedly misses commitments?
  • Review date: When will the team revisit the charter? A natural choice, following Gersick, is around the midpoint of a project.
What the evidence says

In a 2009 study, John Mathieu and Tammy Rapp followed teams of graduate business students working on a semester-long business simulation. Teams that produced high-quality charters and clear performance strategies at the start had better performance trajectories over time than teams that did not. The study involved student teams rather than workplace teams, and it measured quality of charters rather than randomly assigning teams to write them, so it cannot prove a charter causes success on its own. Still, it fits a broader body of research showing that deliberate team launches and explicit norms help teams coordinate, especially when members are new to one another or work at a distance.

How to run a charter session

The process matters as much as the document. A charter written by the leader and emailed to the team is a memo, not a charter. Set aside sixty to ninety minutes. Start with purpose, then roles and decisions, then norms. For norms, ask each member to recall the best and worst team they have been on and what specific behaviors made the difference; those stories quickly produce concrete norms like "we disagree in the meeting, not after it." Revisit the charter whenever membership changes significantly, because in Tuckman's terms the team has partly returned to forming.

Try it this week

Pick one team you lead or belong to. At your next meeting, spend fifteen minutes on two questions: "In one sentence, what is this team for?" and "What is one agreement about how we work together that would make us better?" Write the answers down where everyone can see them. Then put a fifteen-minute "halftime review" on the calendar for four to six weeks from now. You have just drafted the first two sections of a charter and scheduled Gersick's midpoint on purpose.

Bringing it together at Harbor Point

Harbor Point was a real team that lacked compelling direction and an enabling structure. Its scheduling fights were process conflict that had spilled into relationship conflict, avoiding had become the front desk's default style, and the newest clinician's silence signaled low psychological safety and hardened informal roles.

The practice manager's changes lined up closely with the research: purpose, a charter, a structured home for process disputes, one change to the reward context, coaching at natural moments and a deliberate shift in informal roles when she invited the newest clinician to lead case reviews. None of this required new people or a large budget. It required treating team effectiveness as something a leader designs, rather than something that simply happens when good people are put in a room together.

Key takeaways

  • A real team is defined by shared purpose, interdependence and mutual accountability; not every group needs to be a team.
  • Team performance equals potential minus process losses, such as coordination problems and social loafing.
  • Tuckman and Jensen's stages normalize friction, while Gersick's punctuated equilibrium shows that first meetings and midpoints are critical moments.
  • Hackman's research suggests that team design and launch shape performance more than a leader's in-the-moment behavior; coaching works best on a sound structure.
  • Relationship and process conflict reliably harm teams; task conflict can help, mainly when it stays separate from personal friction.
  • No conflict-handling style is always best; effective leaders match style to the stakes, the relationship and the time available.
  • Virtual and hybrid teams need deliberate practices for communication, swift trust and fair inclusion of remote members.
  • A team-written charter, reviewed at the midpoint, is one of the simplest tools for launching and sustaining a strong team.

Self-check

Reflect and apply

  1. Think of a group you work in that is called a team. Using the definition in this chapter, is it a real team or a working group? What would change if everyone agreed on the answer?
  2. Rate a team you know on each of Hackman's five conditions from weak to strong. Which one condition, if improved, would make the biggest difference, and what is one step you could take to improve it?
  3. Recall a recent disagreement at work. Was it task, relationship or process conflict, or did it start as one and become another? What might have kept it on the task path?
  4. Which conflict-handling style do you use most often under pressure? Describe one upcoming situation where a different style would serve you and your team better.
