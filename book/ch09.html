Ethics, Purpose and Leading with Data

Good leaders do the right thing, do it fairly, and can show their working. This chapter gives you the frameworks, the fairness research and the simple numbers you need to make hard calls and explain them honestly.

Learning objectives

  • Explain ethical leadership as both personal conduct and deliberate management of what others see rewarded and punished.
  • Apply consequence, duty, virtue, justice and care frameworks to a workplace dilemma and compare the answers they give.
  • Distinguish distributive, procedural and interactional justice and predict how each shapes reactions to a hard decision.
  • Calculate a break-even point and a simple staffing trade-off, then communicate the decision in a way that is honest about costs and uncertainty.

Maya Okafor owns Riverbend Physical Therapy, a three-clinician practice with a waiting list that grows every month. Her clinicians are good and tired. Her accountant has told her the practice is "fine but thin." One Friday evening she sits with two options. She can ask her three physical therapists to pick up extra evening shifts at overtime pay, which the spreadsheet says is the cheaper way to see the waiting patients. Or she can hire a fourth therapist, which costs more up front and makes the numbers tighter for a while. Both options have ethical weight: one leans on people who are already stretched, the other puts the business at more financial risk. By the end of this chapter you will be able to run Maya's numbers, see the ethical questions underneath them, and explain the choice in a way her team experiences as fair.

Why ethics is a leadership skill, not a personality trait

Most people think of ethics as a matter of character: good people do good things and bad people do bad things. Character matters, but decades of research in behavioral ethics show that ordinary, decent people regularly make choices they would not endorse if they stepped back. They do this less because they are corrupt and more because of the situations they are in: time pressure, targets, unclear rules, a boss who seems to want a certain answer, or a problem that has been framed purely as a business question so the moral side never comes into view.

Ann Tenbrunsel and David Messick (2004) called this last effect ethical fading: the moral colors of a decision drain away when it is described in the language of numbers, targets or procedure. A clinic manager asked to "improve schedule utilization" may double-book patients without ever noticing that she has made a decision about patient safety and staff wellbeing. Nobody lied. The ethics simply faded out of the frame. A related idea, bounded ethicality, describes the systematic blind spots all of us have, such as favoring people like ourselves or judging our own conduct more generously than other people's.

If situations shape ethical behavior, then leaders, who design many of those situations, carry a large share of responsibility. You set the goals, the incentives and the stories people tell about what "really" gets rewarded. As Chapter 4 showed, you also decide whether speaking up is safe, and that applies to ethical concerns as much as to errors.

A process view of ethical behavior

James Rest (1986) proposed a simple model that is still a useful teaching tool. To act ethically, a person has to succeed at four separate steps. First, moral awareness: noticing that a situation has an ethical dimension at all. Second, moral judgment: working out what the right thing to do is. Third, moral intention: deciding to put the ethical choice ahead of competing goals such as hitting a number or keeping the boss happy. Fourth, moral action: actually following through, which can take courage and skill. Failure at any one step breaks the chain. Many workplace lapses fail at step one; people simply do not see the issue.

Thomas Jones (1991) added that some issues are easier to notice than others. He called this moral intensity. An issue feels more intense when harm is large, likely and soon, when the people affected are close to us, and when there is broad agreement that the act is wrong. Leaders can use this on purpose: make consequences concrete and name the patient, athlete or colleague affected.

Ethical leadership: being a moral person and a moral manager

What makes a leader "ethical" in the eyes of the people who work for them? Linda Treviño, Laura Hartman and Michael Brown (2000) interviewed senior executives and ethics officers and found that a reputation for ethical leadership rests on two pillars. The first is being a moral person: honest, trustworthy, fair, caring and principled in your own decisions. The second, and the one people often forget, is being a moral manager: actively using your role to make ethics visible. Moral managers talk openly about ethics and values, set clear standards, and use rewards and discipline to back those standards up.

A leader can be personally upright and still be seen as ethically neutral. If you never ask "is this the right thing to do?" and never visibly act when someone cuts a corner, your team may conclude that ethics has no place in the business. The worst combination, talking about values while behaving otherwise, Treviño and colleagues called hypocritical leadership.

The Brown and Treviño definition

Michael Brown, Linda Treviño and David Harrison (2005) built on this work to define ethical leadership as "the demonstration of normatively appropriate conduct" through personal actions and relationships, together with promoting that conduct to followers through two-way communication, reinforcement and decision making. In plain language: behave well, talk about it, reward it, and make decisions in ways people can see are principled. They grounded the idea in social learning theory (Bandura, 1977). People learn what is acceptable at work mostly by watching credible, attractive role models and noticing what gets rewarded and punished. A leader who holds power over pay and promotion is an especially powerful model.

What the evidence says

Meta-analyses of ethical leadership research report consistent positive links with follower trust, satisfaction, commitment, voice and citizenship behavior, and negative links with counterproductive behavior. Two honest limits apply. First, most studies are cross-sectional surveys in which the same people rate both their leader and their own attitudes, which can inflate relationships. Second, ethical leadership overlaps heavily with other "positive" leadership styles such as transformational and authentic leadership (Chapter 6), so it is not always clear what is unique about it. The broad pattern is credible; exact effect sizes should be read with care.

Research by David Mayer and colleagues (2009) suggests ethical leadership "trickles down" from top managers through supervisors to teams. In a small clinic or studio the chain is short, so the owner's behavior is almost the whole ethical climate.

Case

At Summit Strength, a fictional chain of four fitness studios, the regional manager Luis noticed that coaches at one location were quietly selling nutrition supplements with health claims the company had never approved. Sales there were strong and the manager was well liked. Luis talked to the manager privately and learned why (pressure to hit add-on revenue targets). He then removed the supplement target at all four studios, and on the next all-staff call explained, without naming anyone, why unapproved health claims were a line the company would not cross. What made this ethical leadership was turning a private correction into a visible standard and fixing the incentive that caused the problem.

Frameworks for hard choices

When a situation clearly has an ethical side, the next question is what to do. Philosophers have argued about this for over two thousand years, and you do not need to settle the debate. You need a small toolkit of lenses, each of which notices things the others miss.

Consequences

Consequentialism, of which utilitarianism is the best-known form (associated with Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries), judges actions by their outcomes. The right action is the one that produces the greatest overall good, or the least harm, for everyone affected. Most managers use this lens by instinct because it resembles cost-benefit analysis. Its weakness is that it can justify harming a few for the benefit of many, and it assumes outcomes are more predictable than they usually are.

Duties and rights

Deontology, most closely associated with Immanuel Kant (late eighteenth century), holds that some actions are right or wrong in themselves, regardless of consequences. We have duties, such as telling the truth and keeping promises, and people have rights that should not be traded away for convenience. In business terms, Kant's tests become: would you be comfortable if everyone in your position did this, and are you using someone merely as a means to your ends?

Character

Virtue ethics, going back to Aristotle, shifts the question from "what should I do?" to "what kind of person, or organization, do I want to be?" Virtues are stable traits such as honesty, courage, fairness and practical wisdom; Chapter 2's character strengths build on this tradition. The lens is useful when rules run out, because it asks whether a choice builds or erodes the habits you want.

Justice

The justice lens asks whether benefits and burdens are distributed fairly and whether the process treats people as equals. John Rawls (1971) suggested designing rules from behind a "veil of ignorance": would you accept this rule if you might end up as the most junior employee rather than the owner?

Care

The ethics of care, developed by Carol Gilligan (1982) and Nel Noddings (1984), starts from relationships rather than abstract rules. It asks how to meet the real needs of particular people who depend on us, which fits health and wellness settings naturally. Its risk is favoritism toward those closest to us.

Common mistake

Treating legality as the finish line. Law sets a slow-moving floor. Scheduling staff to the limit of labor rules every week, or marketing with carefully worded but misleading claims, can be legal and still fail every lens above. "It's legal" answers a compliance question, not the ethical one.

Organizational justice: why fairness drives reactions

Ethical frameworks help you decide. Organizational justice research helps you predict how people will react to what you decide. It studies employees' perceptions of fairness at work, and one of its most robust findings is that people care about fairness in three distinct ways. Each one can be managed.

Distributive justice: was the outcome fair?

Distributive justice is about the fairness of outcomes: pay, shifts, workloads, promotions, bonuses, layoffs. Its roots lie in J. Stacy Adams's equity theory (1965), which proposed that people compare the ratio of what they put in (effort, skill, time) to what they get out (pay, recognition) with the same ratio for others. When the ratios feel unequal, people try to restore balance by working less, asking for more or leaving. People also apply other rules, such as equality (everyone gets the same) or need, and trouble starts when a leader applies one rule and the team expects another.

Procedural justice: was the process fair?

Procedural justice concerns how decisions are made. John Thibaut and Laurens Walker (1975), studying legal disputes, found that people cared a great deal about having a voice in the process, even when they did not control the outcome. Gerald Leventhal (1980) listed criteria people use to judge whether a procedure is fair: it is applied consistently across people and time, it is free from personal bias, it is based on accurate information, there is a way to correct mistakes, it reflects the views of those affected (representativeness), and it fits prevailing ethical standards.

Interactional justice: was I treated well?

Robert Bies and Joseph Moag (1986) added that people also judge the quality of the treatment they receive when a decision is carried out. This interactional justice is now usually split into two parts (Greenberg, 1993; Colquitt, 2001). Interpersonal justice is about dignity and respect: being treated politely and without contempt. Informational justice is about explanations: receiving honest, timely and adequate reasons for the decision.

Why process matters most when news is bad

A meta-analysis by Jason Colquitt and colleagues (2001), pooling over a hundred studies, confirmed that the forms of justice are distinct and each predicts trust, satisfaction, commitment, performance and withdrawal. Distributive justice tends to predict reactions to the specific outcome, while procedural justice predicts reactions to the organization as a whole.

Joel Brockner and Batia Wiesenfeld (1996) reviewed dozens of studies and found an important interaction. When outcomes are favorable, people pay little attention to process. When outcomes are unfavorable, fair process and respectful treatment substantially soften negative reactions. In Jerald Greenberg's (1990) study of a manufacturer that cut pay by 15 percent at two plants, employee theft rose far less at the plant where managers gave a thorough, sincere explanation than at the plant given a brief, impersonal one. It was a single-company field study, so it should not be over-generalized, but later research supports its lesson. Leaders often cannot soften the outcome. They can almost always offer voice, a consistent process, an honest explanation and respect.

Purpose that means something

Many organizations now publish a purpose statement: a short answer to the question "why do we exist, beyond making money?" At its best, purpose connects daily tasks to a meaningful contribution. At its worst, it is a slogan nobody believes, and the gap between words and behavior looks like hypocrisy.

Evidence on purpose and meaningful work

Some of the clearest evidence comes from Adam Grant's field experiments. In one study with university fundraising callers, Grant and colleagues (2007) arranged for some callers to meet briefly with a student whose scholarship had been funded by the calls. A month later, those callers were spending considerably more time on the phone and raising considerably more money than callers who had not. The mechanism is perceived prosocial impact: seeing, concretely, that your work helps real people, which fits Chapter 3's self-determination theory and the "meaning" element of PERMA.

At the organizational level, Claudine Gartenberg, Andrea Prat and George Serafeim (2019) found in large employee surveys that companies where staff reported strong purpose and clear direction from management tended to show better later financial performance; purpose without clarity did not. The data are correlational, but the practical point is clear: purpose works when it is translated into specific choices.

Purpose as a decision tool

A good test of whether purpose is real is whether it ever costs you anything. A wellness studio whose purpose is "health habits that last" should refuse profitable crash-diet programs and measure twelve-month retention, not just sign-ups. Purpose that never excludes an option is decoration, and "purpose-washing," a lofty statement beside contradicting incentives, is worse: staff read the budget, not the poster. Riverbend's purpose ("get people moving again, safely and for the long term") points somewhere in Maya's decision: tired clinicians make more mistakes, and a practice that burns out its staff is not built for the long term.

Leading with data: break-even and staffing trade-offs made simple

Ethics and purpose tell you what matters; data tells you what things cost. We prefer data-informed to "data-driven": numbers inform the decision, but judgment and values still drive it. Here is Maya's decision, worked with phone-calculator arithmetic.

Step 1: fixed costs, variable costs and contribution margin

Start by sorting costs into two piles. Fixed costs stay the same each month whether you see many patients or few: rent, the salaries of salaried staff, insurance, software, loan payments. Variable costs rise with each unit of activity: for a clinic, things like single-use supplies, per-visit billing fees and card processing charges.

Riverbend's numbers, all monthly and fictional but realistic in shape, are:

  • Fixed costs: $42,000 (rent, the three therapists' salaries, front desk, insurance, software).
  • Average net revenue per visit, after insurance adjustments: $95.
  • Variable cost per visit: $23.
  • Current volume: 680 visits per month.

The contribution margin is what each visit contributes toward covering fixed costs once its own variable costs are paid: $95 minus $23, which is $72 per visit. As a ratio of price, that is $72 divided by $95, about 75.8 percent.

Step 2: the break-even point

The break-even point is the volume at which total revenue exactly equals total cost, so profit is zero. Because each visit contributes $72, you need enough visits to cover the $42,000 of fixed costs:

Break-even visits = fixed costs ÷ contribution margin = $42,000 ÷ $72 = 583.3 visits per month.

You cannot see a third of a patient, so in practice Riverbend needs 584 visits a month to cover its costs (at 584 visits it makes a profit of $48). In revenue terms, break-even is 583.3 × $95, about $55,417 a month. At its current 680 visits, Riverbend earns 680 × $72 = $48,960 in contribution, minus $42,000 of fixed costs, for an operating profit of $6,960 a month.

The gap between current volume and break-even is the margin of safety: 680 minus 583.3, or about 96.7 visits, which is roughly 14.2 percent of current volume. In plain words, if visits fell by about one in seven, Riverbend would stop covering its costs. This is what the accountant meant by "fine but thin."

Step 3: the staffing trade-off

The waiting list represents about 150 extra visits a month that Riverbend could provide if it had the clinician time. Maya has two ways to supply them.

Option A: hire a fourth therapist. Salary $84,000 a year plus benefits and payroll taxes of 22 percent, which is $102,480 a year, or $8,540 a month. This is a new fixed cost: Maya pays it whether the new therapist is fully booked or not. Each extra visit still brings its $72 contribution. The new hire pays for themselves at $8,540 ÷ $72 = 118.6, so 119 extra visits a month, well inside a full-time caseload of roughly 240 visits (12 a day over 20 working days). With 150 extra visits, the monthly gain is 150 × $72 − $8,540 = $2,260.

Option B: overtime for the current three. Therapists earn $42 an hour; overtime at time and a half is $63 an hour. A therapist sees about 1.5 visits an hour, so overtime labor adds $63 ÷ 1.5 = $42 per visit. That turns each overtime visit's contribution into $72 − $42 = $30. There is no new fixed cost, so 150 visits yield 150 × $30 = $4,500 a month.

On these numbers, overtime looks better by $4,500 − $2,260 = $2,240 a month. That is the answer a purely numbers-driven process would give, and it is where many decisions stop. A data-informed leader asks two more questions.

First, where do the lines cross? Hiring has a high fixed cost but a high margin per visit; overtime has no fixed cost but a low margin. The two options produce the same gain when 72 × visits − 8,540 = 30 × visits, which works out to 8,540 ÷ 42 = 203.3 extra visits a month, where both options yield $6,100. Below about 203 extra visits, overtime makes more money; from 204 upward, hiring does. Riverbend's waiting list has grown every month this year. If it keeps growing, the financial answer flips.

Second, what is the spreadsheet not counting? The 150 overtime visits require 150 ÷ 1.5 = 100 overtime hours a month, which is about 33 hours per therapist per month, or roughly 7.7 extra hours each per week, on top of full caseloads. Fatigue is associated with more errors in health care. Burnout (Chapter 10) raises turnover, and replacing a clinician typically means months of recruiting, vacancy and ramp-up. None of those costs appears in the $2,240 difference, but they are real, and they fall first on the three people least able to refuse.

Step 4: bring the lenses back in

Now Maya can hold the numbers and the ethics together. Consequences: overtime earns an extra $2,240 a month today but carries unpriced risks to safety, quality and retention, and the advantage disappears if demand keeps rising. Duties: she owes patients timely care and owes staff reasonable limits. Justice: overtime places the whole burden on three clinicians; hiring shares risk between the owner, who carries the fixed cost, and the team. Care: she knows that one of her therapists has young children and another is already showing signs of exhaustion. Purpose: "safely and for the long term."

A sensible data-informed decision might be a blend: short, capped, genuinely voluntary overtime while Maya recruits a fourth therapist, reviewed when the waiting list is re-measured in two months. The numbers did not make this decision. They showed the size of the trade-off and the point at which it reverses.

What the evidence says

Two well-known warnings apply to any leader who manages by numbers. Goodhart's law, from economist Charles Goodhart (1975), is often summarized as "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." Campbell's law (Donald Campbell, 1979) adds that the more a quantitative indicator is used for important social decisions, the more it will be corrupted and will distort the processes it was meant to monitor. Health care offers many documented examples of targets that improved the number while harming the underlying goal. The lesson is not to avoid metrics; it is to pair them, watch for gaming, and keep asking what each number is supposed to represent.

Habits of a data-informed leader

A few habits keep data useful rather than a weapon. Check the assumptions by testing what happens if each estimate is 10 percent worse; this sensitivity analysis shows which ones really matter. Separate the math from the values so people can argue about each honestly. Show your working. And count what the spreadsheet cannot, listing unpriced effects in words beside the priced ones.

Explaining hard trade-offs

The last step is often the hardest: telling people about a decision some will not like. Justice research gives practical guidance here.

A five-part structure

1. State the decision plainly. Lead with what is happening, not with a long preamble that makes people anxious. "We are going to hire a fourth therapist, and until that person starts, I'm asking for up to four voluntary overtime hours a week for the next eight weeks."

2. Explain the reasoning, including the numbers. This is informational justice. Share the real figures you are comfortable sharing, including the ones that cut against your choice. "Overtime alone would earn the practice about $2,240 a month more than hiring right now. I'm choosing not to rely on it long-term because it puts 100 extra hours a month on three people, and because if the waiting list keeps growing past about 200 visits, hiring becomes the better option anyway."

3. Name the costs honestly. Do not pretend a trade-off has no losers. "This means our margin will be tighter for several months, and it means some of you will still be busier than you want to be until the new person is settled in."

4. Describe the process and the voice people had or still have. This is procedural justice. "I talked with each of you last week about hours and limits, and that's why overtime is capped and voluntary. If the plan is not working for you, tell me, and we will look at it again at the eight-week review."

5. Treat people with dignity. This is interpersonal justice. Deliver the message in person where possible, acknowledge the effort people are already giving, and resist the temptation to blame "corporate," "the numbers" or "the market" as if you had no choice.

Try it this week

Pick one decision you have made or will make that some people will not like. Write a five-sentence explanation using the structure above: decision, reasoning with numbers, honest costs, process and voice, respect. Then read it aloud and ask: would I find this fair if I were on the receiving end? If any sentence hides a cost or blames an outside force, rewrite it.

Transparency with judgment

Not every number can be shared; salaries and patient information have legitimate limits. Be open about reasoning and trade-offs, and say plainly when something is confidential. People can tell "I can't share that" from "there's nothing to share."

Be honest about uncertainty too, using a simple formula: what we know, what we don't know, what we are assuming, and when we will check. "We know the waiting list is 150 visits a month. We don't know whether it will keep growing. We are assuming it will. We will re-measure in eight weeks." This protects your credibility when forecasts miss.

The thread running through this chapter is simple. Ethical leaders notice the moral side of business decisions, use more than one lens, look honestly at the numbers, and explain the result with respect. None of this makes hard choices easy. It makes them defensible, and it builds trust for the next one.

Key takeaways

  • Ethical lapses usually come from situations, framing and blind spots rather than from bad character, so leaders who shape situations carry real responsibility.
  • Rest's four steps (awareness, judgment, intention, action) show where ethical behavior can break down; awareness often fails first because of ethical fading.
  • Ethical leadership (Brown, Treviño and Harrison, 2005) means being a moral person and a moral manager who makes ethics visible through communication, rewards and decisions.
  • Consequences, duties, virtue, justice and care are complementary lenses; where they disagree, you have found the real dilemma.
  • People judge fairness by outcomes, process and treatment; when outcomes are bad, fair process and honest, respectful explanations matter most.
  • Purpose is real only when it shapes choices and sometimes costs you something.
  • Break-even equals fixed costs divided by contribution margin; staffing choices trade fixed costs against per-unit margins, and the better option can reverse as volume changes.
  • Explain hard trade-offs by stating the decision, showing the reasoning and numbers, naming the costs, describing the process and voice, and treating people with dignity.

Reflect and apply

  1. Think of a recent decision at your workplace that was framed mainly in numbers. Who was affected who never appeared in the discussion, and how might naming them have changed the decision?
  2. Rate your own leadership on the "moral manager" side: in the last month, when did you talk openly about ethics, and when did you visibly reward or correct behavior on ethical grounds?
  3. Choose a real trade-off in your organization and run it through all five lenses. Where do the lenses disagree, and what is the real dilemma they reveal?
  4. Build a simple break-even calculation for one service, program or product you are responsible for. What is your margin of safety, and which single assumption, if it were 10 percent worse, would worry you most?
