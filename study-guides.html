The Positive Lens on Organizational Behavior

Organizational behavior gives you a way to see why people at work act as they do. This chapter shows you how to look at the same workplace through two lenses, one that fixes what is broken and one that learns from what is already working, and how to hold both to the standard of good evidence.

You should be able to

  • Explain what organizational behavior studies and why it matters to people who lead clinics, studios, teams and small businesses.
  • Distinguish the individual, group and organizational levels of analysis and diagnose which level a workplace problem mainly lives at.
  • Apply the four sources of evidence used in evidence-based management to a real decision in your own workplace.
  • Evaluate the positive organizational scholarship and positive deviance approaches, including their strengths and their honest limits.

Chapter outline

  1. What organizational behavior is for
  2. Three levels of analysis
  3. Evidence-based management
  4. Positive organizational scholarship
  5. Positive deviance
  6. Limits and critiques of the positive lens
  7. Using both lenses: a working approach

Big ideas

  • Organizational behavior studies what people think, feel and do at work, and how those patterns affect performance, citizenship, withdrawal, wellbeing and service quality.
  • Problems can live at the individual, group or organizational level; many leadership errors come from treating a system problem as a personal failing.
  • Evidence-based management combines scientific research, organizational data, professional expertise and stakeholder values, then checks whether the decision worked.
  • Positive organizational scholarship studies what enables excellence and flourishing, closing the abundance gap rather than only the deficit gap.
  • Positive deviance inquiry finds people who succeed under the same constraints as others, discovers their uncommon practices and helps peers adopt them.
  • Bad tends to be stronger than good, so positive practices complement problem-solving rather than replace it.
  • Be wary of precise, catchy numbers such as the retracted positivity ratio; test ideas in your own setting.
  • The strongest approach looks both ways, names the level, gathers evidence and treats decisions as small experiments.

Practice questions

1. Every new hire on a fitness center's evening shift begins arriving late within a month of starting. At which level of analysis should a manager look first?

  1. A. The individual level, because each new hire lacks conscientiousness
  2. B. The group level, because a shared shift norm is probably teaching newcomers what on time means
  3. C. The organizational level, because the company's strategy is unclear
  4. D. No level; lateness is random
Show answer

B. When many different people in the same spot behave the same way, the cause is more likely a group norm than a string of individual flaws. The individual explanation is tempting because it is the fundamental attribution error in action.

2. Which set lists the four sources of evidence in evidence-based management?

  1. A. Intuition, benchmarking, best practices and consultant advice
  2. B. Surveys, interviews, focus groups and observation
  3. C. Scientific research, organizational data, professional expertise and stakeholder values
  4. D. Revenue, cost, profit and market share
Show answer

C. Barends, Rousseau and Briner describe these four sources. Surveys and interviews are ways of collecting data, not the four sources themselves, and copying best practices is exactly what the approach warns against.

3. A clinic has eliminated its billing errors. According to the deviance continuum, what has it achieved?

  1. A. It has closed the abundance gap
  2. B. It has reached positive deviance
  3. C. It has closed the deficit gap and returned to normal
  4. D. It has proven that positive practices work
Show answer

C. Fixing problems moves an organization from negative deviance to normal. Reaching exceptional performance is a separate move across the abundance gap, which is why removing problems alone does not produce excellence.

4. A gym owner notices one trainer has the highest client renewal rate. Before calling this positive deviance, what should the owner check?

  1. A. Whether the trainer faces the same constraints as others, such as a similar client mix
  2. B. Whether the trainer has the most years of experience
  3. C. Whether other trainers like this trainer
  4. D. Whether the trainer is willing to write a rule for everyone
Show answer

A. Positive deviance means succeeding under the same constraints as peers. If the trainer simply has easier clients, the high rate reflects circumstances, not uncommon practices others could adopt.

5. What happened to the claim that flourishing requires a positivity ratio of about 2.9 to 1?

  1. A. It was confirmed by a large meta-analysis
  2. B. Its mathematical basis was shown to be invalid and the modeling was formally partially retracted
  3. C. It was replaced by a ratio of 5 to 1
  4. D. It was never published in a scholarly journal
Show answer

B. Brown, Sokal and Friedman's 2013 critique led to a partial retraction of the modeling. The broader idea that positive emotions help remains, but the precise number should not be used. The lesson is to be skeptical of precise, catchy numbers about human behavior.

6. Which statement best captures a fair critique of the positive lens?

  1. A. Positive emotions have no measurable effects at work
  2. B. Positive approaches should replace problem-solving entirely
  3. C. Positive organizational scholarship is only relevant to large corporations
  4. D. Focusing on positivity can discourage people from raising real problems and can tempt researchers to accept weaker evidence
Show answer

D. Critics such as Fineman and Hackman raised these concerns. The right response is not to abandon the positive lens but to use it alongside problem-solving and to hold it to the same standard of evidence.

Apply it at work

  1. Think of a recurring frustration in your workplace. Write one sentence describing it at the individual level, one at the group level and one at the organizational level. Which description feels most accurate, and which would lead to the most useful action?
  2. Recall a recent decision your organization made, such as a new policy, tool or schedule. Which of the four sources of evidence were used, and which were missing? What would you have wanted to know?
  3. Identify one person, shift or location in your organization that does unusually well on an outcome you care about. What might they be doing that nobody has written down, and how could you find out respectfully?
  4. Where in your workplace might positive language be used, even unintentionally, to discourage people from raising concerns? What could you say differently the next time someone brings you bad news?

Individual Differences, Strengths and Self-Awareness

People differ in personality, in how they read situations, in how they handle emotion and in what they do best. This chapter gives you evidence-based tools for understanding those differences in others, and the harder skill of seeing them clearly in yourself.

You should be able to

  • Describe the Big Five personality traits and summarize what research says about their links to job performance and leadership.
  • Identify common perception and attribution errors, such as the fundamental attribution error and the halo effect, in everyday workplace judgments.
  • Critique the concept of emotional intelligence by comparing ability and mixed models and weighing the evidence for each.
  • Apply the VIA classification of character strengths and a feedback-based approach to build self-awareness in yourself and your team.

Chapter outline

  1. Person and situation
  2. The Big Five personality traits
  3. Perception and attribution
  4. Emotions at work
  5. Emotional intelligence and its critiques
  6. Character strengths
  7. Building self-awareness

Big ideas

  • Behavior reflects both the person and the situation; strong situations reduce the influence of personality, and weak situations increase it.
  • The Big Five (openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness and emotional stability) are continuous traits; conscientiousness is the most consistent personality predictor of job performance, but all effects are modest.
  • Type-based tools such as the Myers-Briggs are fine for conversation but should not be used for hiring or promotion.
  • Perception is an active construction; shortcuts such as the halo effect, stereotyping and contrast effects produce predictable errors.
  • The fundamental attribution error leads leaders to blame people for problems caused by situations; Kelley's consensus, distinctiveness and consistency questions help correct it.
  • Emotions spread and accumulate at work; heavy surface acting is linked to burnout in emotionally demanding jobs.
  • Emotional intelligence skills matter most in emotionally demanding roles, but the concept has been oversold, and ability and mixed models measure different things.
  • Character strengths offer a positive vocabulary for development, but strengths can be overused, and self-awareness, especially of how you affect others, requires deliberate feedback.

Practice questions

1. A clinic introduces a strict step-by-step medication handling protocol. According to the idea of situational strength, what should happen to the influence of staff personality on medication handling?

  1. A. It should increase, because people resist rules
  2. B. It should decrease, because the protocol makes expected behavior clear
  3. C. It should stay the same, because personality is fixed
  4. D. It should disappear entirely
Show answer

B. Strong situations with clear expectations reduce the room for individual differences to shape behavior. Personality does not vanish entirely, which is why the last option overstates the effect.

2. Which Big Five trait has most consistently predicted job performance across many types of jobs?

  1. A. Extraversion
  2. B. Openness to experience
  3. C. Agreeableness
  4. D. Conscientiousness
Show answer

D. Barrick and Mount's 1991 meta-analysis and later work point to conscientiousness. Extraversion is tempting because it is the strongest predictor of leadership emergence, which is a different outcome from job performance.

3. Every therapist at a clinic runs late on Tuesdays, but only on Tuesdays, and it happens every week. Using Kelley's model, what is the most likely cause?

  1. A. Something about the Tuesday situation
  2. B. The therapists' low conscientiousness
  3. C. One therapist is spreading a bad habit
  4. D. Random chance
Show answer

A. High consensus (everyone does it), high distinctiveness (only on Tuesdays) and high consistency (every week) point to an external, situational cause. Blaming the therapists' personalities would be the fundamental attribution error.

4. A front desk worker feels irritated but smiles and speaks warmly to every caller without changing how she feels inside. Which concept best describes this?

  1. A. Deep acting
  2. B. Emotional contagion
  3. C. Surface acting
  4. D. Self-serving bias
Show answer

C. Surface acting means displaying the required emotion without feeling it. Deep acting would involve trying to actually feel warmth, for example by reframing the caller's situation, and is less strongly linked to burnout.

5. Which statement about emotional intelligence best reflects the research evidence?

  1. A. EI matters twice as much as IQ for success in any job
  2. B. EI is a meaningless concept with no link to performance
  3. C. Ability and mixed models are essentially the same measure
  4. D. EI predicts performance best in emotionally demanding jobs, and its added value beyond personality and cognitive ability is modest
Show answer

D. Meta-analyses by Joseph and Newman and by O'Boyle and colleagues support this balanced view. The twice-as-much claim is a popular exaggeration without peer-reviewed support, and dismissing EI entirely ignores real findings.

6. A manager's direct reports consistently say she interrupts them, but she is unaware of it. In the Johari window, where does this behavior sit, and what best reduces it?

  1. A. Hidden area; reduced by disclosure
  2. B. Blind area; reduced by seeking feedback
  3. C. Open area; no action needed
  4. D. Unknown area; reduced by personality testing
Show answer

B. Something others see but you do not is in the blind area, and asking for specific feedback is the main way to shrink it. Disclosure shrinks the hidden area, which contains what you know but others do not.

Apply it at work

  1. Think of a colleague whose working style differs sharply from yours. Using the Big Five, describe the difference in neutral terms. What advantages does their style bring to your team that yours does not?
  2. Recall a recent time you judged a coworker's behavior as a character flaw. Apply Kelley's three questions. Is there a situational explanation you did not consider at the time?
  3. Which roles in your organization carry the heaviest emotional labor? What is one change to scheduling, breaks or support that could reduce the need for surface acting?
  4. Identify two of your own signature strengths. Describe one situation where each serves your team well and one where overusing it might be creating a blind spot.

Motivation, Engagement and the Science of Flourishing

Why people give their best effort, why a raise alone rarely buys it, and how a leader can build work that people want to throw themselves into.

You should be able to

  • Distinguish content theories of motivation (Maslow, Herzberg, self-determination theory) from process theories (expectancy and goal-setting) and explain what each is good for.
  • Diagnose a motivation problem in your own workplace by checking autonomy, competence, relatedness, expectancy, instrumentality and valence.
  • Compare work engagement, job satisfaction and flourishing, including the PERMA model and the main criticisms of each.
  • Design a strengths-based feedback conversation and a goal that is specific, challenging and matched to the complexity of the task.

Chapter outline

  1. What motivation is, and why leaders get it wrong
  2. Needs and the two-factor idea
  3. Self-determination theory: autonomy, competence, relatedness
  4. Expectancy and goals: how people decide where effort goes
  5. Engagement: bringing your whole self to work
  6. Flourishing and the PERMA model
  7. Strengths-based feedback

Big ideas

  • Motivation has direction, intensity and persistence; a problem can sit in any of the three, and conditions usually explain more than character.
  • Maslow is a reminder to check the basics, not a strict ladder. Herzberg shows that removing dissatisfaction and building satisfaction are separate jobs.
  • Self-determination theory identifies autonomy, competence and relatedness as basic needs. Autonomous forms of extrinsic motivation behave much like intrinsic motivation.
  • Rewards that feel controlling can undermine interest in work people already enjoy; informational recognition is safer.
  • Expectancy theory is a diagnostic chain: can I do it, will it pay off, do I care? Fix the weakest link.
  • Specific, difficult goals with feedback raise performance; use learning goals for complex tasks and add guardrails against narrow, unethical pursuit.
  • Engagement (vigor, dedication, absorption) grows when job resources keep pace with demands; flourishing and PERMA widen the lens, though both carry measurement critiques.
  • Strengths-based feedback focuses on the task, leads with what works, and still addresses the gap that matters most.

Practice questions

1. A clinic owner raises pay and complaints about pay stop, but effort and enthusiasm do not change. Which theory best predicts this?

  1. A. Goal-setting theory
  2. B. Herzberg's two-factor theory
  3. C. Maslow's hierarchy of needs
  4. D. Expectancy theory
Show answer

B. Herzberg treats pay as a hygiene factor: fixing it removes dissatisfaction but does not create satisfaction. Maslow is tempting because pay covers basic needs, but his theory does not separate dissatisfaction from satisfaction on two scales.

2. A trainer says, 'I document carefully because it keeps my clients safe, and that matters to me.' On the self-determination continuum this is best described as:

  1. A. External regulation
  2. B. Introjected regulation
  3. C. Identified regulation
  4. D. Amotivation
Show answer

C. Identified regulation means acting because you personally value the outcome. Introjected regulation is tempting, but it involves guilt or self-image pressure rather than genuinely endorsing the value.

3. Instructors do not chase a retention bonus because past bonuses were changed mid-quarter and never paid. Which link in Vroom's model is weak?

  1. A. Expectancy
  2. B. Valence
  3. C. Intrinsic motivation
  4. D. Instrumentality
Show answer

D. Instrumentality is the belief that performance will actually lead to the promised outcome. Valence is tempting, but the instructors may value the money; they simply do not believe it will arrive.

4. A new practice manager faces a complex, unfamiliar problem: reducing no-shows. Goal-setting research suggests the best initial goal is:

  1. A. Identify and test three strategies for reducing no-shows this month
  2. B. Cut no-shows by 40 percent this month
  3. C. Do your best on no-shows
  4. D. Keep no-shows from getting worse
Show answer

A. For complex tasks people do not yet know how to do, learning goals outperform hard outcome goals. The 40 percent target is specific and difficult, which is usually good, but on unfamiliar work it can push people to grind instead of learn.

5. According to the job demands-resources model, a leader who cannot reduce patient volume should most directly:

  1. A. Replace engagement surveys with satisfaction surveys
  2. B. Add job resources such as feedback, support and autonomy
  3. C. Offer a one-time bonus to offset the workload
  4. D. Accept that burnout is inevitable in high-volume settings
Show answer

B. Resources both build engagement and buffer the strain from demands. A bonus is tempting but is a single extrinsic reward that does not change the daily balance of demands and resources.

6. Kluger and DeNisi's 1996 meta-analysis found that feedback sometimes lowered performance. Their explanation was that harmful feedback tends to:

  1. A. Be delivered too frequently
  2. B. Include too much positive information
  3. C. Come from peers instead of supervisors
  4. D. Shift attention from the task toward the self
Show answer

D. Feedback that makes people ask 'Am I good enough?' diverts effort into self-protection. Frequency is a tempting answer, but their account centers on where feedback directs attention, not how often it arrives.

Apply it at work

  1. Think of a time your own motivation at work dropped sharply. Which was weakest: autonomy, competence or relatedness? What would have restored it?
  2. List the rewards and incentives your organization uses. Which ones feel informational to employees, and which feel controlling? Is any of them rewarding work people already enjoyed?
  3. Choose one goal your team is currently working toward. Is it specific and challenging? Is the task complex enough that a learning goal would serve better? What guardrails protect quality and ethics?
  4. Map your team's main job demands and job resources. Which single resource could you add in the next month at little cost, and how would you know whether it helped?

Psychological Safety, Trust and Voice

How teams come to believe it is safe to speak up, why that belief protects patients, clients and profits, and what a leader does every day to build it without lowering the bar.

You should be able to

  • Explain psychological safety as a shared team belief and distinguish it from trust, niceness and low standards.
  • Analyze why employees choose voice or silence, using the ideas of perceived safety, perceived efficacy and implicit voice theories.
  • Apply supportive communication principles and structured speaking-up tools such as SBAR and graded assertiveness to your own setting.
  • Develop a practical plan to set the stage, invite participation and respond productively when people speak up.

Chapter outline

  1. What psychological safety is, and what it is not
  2. What the evidence shows
  3. Voice and silence
  4. Trust: the willingness to be vulnerable
  5. Positive communication and supportive feedback
  6. Speaking up in clinical settings
  7. Building it as a leader

Big ideas

  • Psychological safety is a shared team belief that interpersonal risk taking is safe. It is not niceness, comfort or low standards.
  • Safety and accountability are separate dimensions. The learning zone requires both; safety alone produces the comfort zone.
  • Healthy teams may report more errors because they surface them. Read reporting metrics with that paradox in mind.
  • People speak up when they believe it will matter (efficacy) and that it is safe. Acquiescent silence needs visible follow-through; defensive silence needs safer responses.
  • Trust is a willingness to be vulnerable to a specific party, built on perceived ability, benevolence and integrity and updated after every risk.
  • Supportive communication is descriptive, problem-focused, specific and owned. Aim for more positive than negative interactions, but distrust precise magic ratios.
  • In clinical and other high-stakes settings, tools such as SBAR, graded assertiveness, the two-challenge rule and briefings turn voice from courage into routine.
  • Leaders build safety by setting the stage, inviting participation and, above all, responding productively when people speak up.

Practice questions

1. A clinic team is warm and collegial, rarely disagrees and has not improved any process in two years. In Edmondson's framework this team is most likely in the:

  1. A. Learning zone
  2. B. Anxiety zone
  3. C. Comfort zone
  4. D. Apathy zone
Show answer

C. High safety with low standards produces the comfort zone: pleasant but not stretched. The apathy zone is tempting, but that zone is marked by low safety as well as low standards, and this team feels safe.

2. Edmondson's 1996 hospital study found that better-led nursing units reported more medication errors. The most likely explanation is that:

  1. A. Better-led units detected and reported errors more openly
  2. B. Better-led units had less experienced nurses
  3. C. Better-led units had higher patient volumes
  4. D. Better-led units used more complex medications
Show answer

A. In safer units, nurses were willing to report and discuss mistakes, so more errors surfaced. Higher patient volume is tempting, but the pattern was explained by differences in reporting climate, not workload.

3. A physical therapy aide stops suggesting improvements because 'nothing I say ever gets done.' This is best described as:

  1. A. Defensive silence
  2. B. Prosocial silence
  3. C. Prohibitive voice
  4. D. Acquiescent silence
Show answer

D. Acquiescent silence comes from believing voice will not make a difference. Defensive silence is tempting, but it is driven by fear of consequences, not by resignation.

4. In Mayer, Davis and Schoorman's model, a skilled and caring manager who repeatedly breaks promises is weakest on:

  1. A. Ability
  2. B. Integrity
  3. C. Benevolence
  4. D. Propensity to trust
Show answer

B. Integrity concerns adherence to acceptable principles such as keeping promises. Benevolence is tempting because broken promises feel uncaring, but the manager's care is not in question; reliability of word is.

5. Which statement is the most supportive, in Cameron and Whetten's sense, when addressing a documentation problem?

  1. A. You are careless with paperwork and it has to stop

Teams That Work: Development, Roles and Conflict

Great teams are rarely an accident. This chapter shows you how teams grow, what conditions let them succeed, and how to turn disagreement into better work instead of damaged relationships.

Servant and Transformational Leadership

Some leaders get compliance; others get commitment. This chapter explains the best-studied approaches to leading through purpose and service, what the evidence really shows and where the popular versions go wrong.

You should be able to

  • Explain the difference between transactional and transformational leadership as described by Burns and Bass.
  • Identify the four I's of transformational leadership in examples of everyday leader behavior.
  • Compare servant, transformational and authentic leadership in their focus, strengths and limits.
  • Evaluate critiques of these models and assess which leadership behaviors fit a specific situation in your own work.

Chapter outline

  1. From managing to leading
  2. Transactional leadership: the exchange at the core
  3. Transformational leadership and the four I's
  4. Servant leadership: leading by serving
  5. Authentic leadership: knowing and being yourself
  6. Fit, context and the critiques that matter
  7. Building your own leadership approach

Big ideas

  • Burns distinguished transactional leadership, based on exchange, from transforming leadership, which raises followers' motivation and values; Bass reframed these as complementary dimensions.
  • Transformational leadership consists of four I's: idealized influence, inspirational motivation, intellectual stimulation and individualized consideration.
  • Contingent reward is well supported and forms the foundation; laissez-faire leadership is consistently harmful.
  • Servant leadership, from Greenleaf, puts followers' growth first and shows predictive value beyond transformational leadership in recent meta-analytic work.
  • Authentic leadership emphasizes self-awareness, transparency, balanced processing and internal moral standards, but overlaps heavily with other models and faces conceptual and data concerns.
  • Situational models are intuitive but have uneven evidence; the durable lesson is that behaviors must fit the task, people and moment.
  • Major critiques include circular definitions, heroic bias and the dark side of charisma; treat these models as sets of learnable behaviors rather than labels.

Organizational Culture and Positive Climate

Culture is what people in your organization assume without thinking, and climate is what they feel every day. This chapter shows you how to read both, how leaders shape them whether they mean to or not, and how to build a climate where people and performance can flourish.

You should be able to

  • Distinguish organizational culture from organizational climate and explain why leaders need to attend to both.
  • Analyze an organization using Schein's three levels of culture, separating artifacts, espoused values and basic assumptions.
  • Evaluate the embedding mechanisms a leader is actually using, and judge whether they match the culture the leader says they want.
  • Design a small set of positive practices, suited to your own team or subculture, that could strengthen a positive climate over one quarter.

Chapter outline

  1. Culture and climate: two lenses on the same place
  2. Schein's three levels of culture
  3. How leaders embed and transmit culture
  4. Positive practices and a positive climate
  5. Culture in founder-led and small organizations
  6. Subcultures: many cultures under one roof
  7. Reading and shifting culture

Big ideas

  • Culture is a pattern of shared, learned assumptions; climate is the shared perception of what is rewarded, supported and expected. Climate changes faster, culture more slowly.
  • Climate is usually a climate for something, such as safety, service or learning. Diagnose the specific climate that matters for your goal.
  • Schein's three levels (artifacts, espoused values and basic assumptions) help you avoid confusing what is visible or stated with what actually drives behavior.
  • Leaders embed culture mainly through primary mechanisms: attention and measurement, reactions to crises, resource allocation, role modeling, rewards and status, and selection and removal. Formal statements help only when they match.
  • Cameron's positive leadership strategies (positive climate, relationships, communication and meaning) describe repeatable practices; evidence for their benefits is promising but mostly correlational.
  • Founders imprint strong cultures that help early and can constrain later; growth and succession are the moments when founding assumptions need honest review.
  • Most organizations contain subcultures. Leaders should find shared purpose and build bridges rather than trying to erase differences.
  • Culture shifts when new ways of working are tried, succeed and are recognized repeatedly, starting from a concrete business problem and building on existing strengths.

Leading Change

Most change does not fail because the idea was bad. It stalls because people were not ready, their concerns were treated as obstacles, or the effort ran out of energy after launch. This chapter gives you the classic models, an honest view of their limits, and practical tools for building readiness and making change stick.

You should be able to

  • Explain Lewin's field theory, force field analysis and the unfreeze, change, refreeze idea, including how scholars now question its origins.
  • Apply Kotter's eight steps and the five components of a readiness message to a change you are planning.
  • Interpret resistance to change as information about the change, its process and relationships, rather than as a personal defect.
  • Critique linear step models and design an iterative approach that uses psychological safety, motivation and small wins to sustain change.

Chapter outline

  1. What change asks of people
  2. Lewin's foundations: fields, forces and three steps
  3. Kotter's eight steps
  4. Readiness for change
  5. Resistance as information
  6. Sustaining change: safety, motivation and small wins

Big ideas

  • Change is an external event; transition is the internal process of letting go, living with uncertainty and starting again. Plan for both.
  • Be skeptical of the claim that 70 percent of changes fail; it lacks a sound empirical basis.
  • Lewin's force field analysis shows that reducing restraining forces often works better than adding pressure. The tidy three-step model is useful shorthand but partly a later construction.
  • Kotter's eight steps offer a memorable sequence for large changes; they have limited direct testing and were later revised into concurrent accelerators.
  • Readiness rests on five beliefs: discrepancy, appropriateness, efficacy, principal support and personal valence. Leaders most often neglect efficacy and valence.
  • Resistance is information about the change, the process, real losses or trust. Ask before you persuade.
  • Psychological safety, autonomy-supportive motivation and visible small wins are the best-supported ingredients for making change stick.
  • Use step models as lenses and combine them with iterative test-and-learn cycles that involve the people doing the work.

  2. B. It has been tested in hundreds of controlled trials and outperforms all other models
  3. C. It is best used for continuous rather than episodic change
  4. D. Lewin wrote about it only briefly, and historians argue the tidy model was largely constructed by later writers
Show answer

D. Cummings, Bridgman and Brown (2016) showed that the neat three-step model owes much to later interpreters. It has not been tested in controlled trials, and the eight-stage model is Kotter's.

3. Staff at a wellness center agree the old booking system is failing and that the new one is well chosen, but they doubt they can learn it during peak season. Which readiness belief is weakest?

  1. A. Efficacy
  2. B. Discrepancy
  3. C. Appropriateness
  4. D. Principal support
Show answer

A. Efficacy is the belief that we can make the change work. Discrepancy (need for change) and appropriateness (right change) are already strong in this scenario, and nothing suggests doubts about leaders' commitment.

4. From the perspective of Ford, Ford and D'Amelio, a coach who repeatedly raises objections to a new curriculum is best seen as:

  1. A. An irrational resister who should be removed from the change team
  2. B. Evidence that the change should be abandoned
  3. C. A possible source of information about flaws in the change, the process, or trust
  4. D. Someone high in dispositional resistance who cannot be influenced
Show answer

C. These authors argue that resistance can be a resource that surfaces problems and keeps people engaged, and that change agents often contribute to it. Objections do not automatically mean the change should be abandoned, and disposition is only one factor.

5. In Edmondson, Bohmer and Pisano's study of cardiac surgery teams adopting a new technique, what distinguished successful teams?

  1. A. The surgeon's years of experience
  2. B. Leaders who framed the change as team learning, practiced early and made it safe for all members to speak up
  3. C. A larger budget for new equipment
  4. D. Strict adherence to Kotter's eight steps in order
Show answer

B. Teams with equally skilled surgeons varied widely. Success depended on leader framing, deliberate team preparation and psychological safety, not on experience or budget alone, and the study was not a test of Kotter's model.

6. Which is the strongest critique of step models such as Kotter's eight steps?

  1. A. They ignore the role of leaders entirely
  2. B. They have been proven to fail in most organizations
  3. C. They are too short to be useful
  4. D. They tend to be linear and top-down, and the full models have received limited rigorous empirical testing
Show answer

D. Reviews such as Appelbaum and colleagues (2012) found support for many individual steps but little testing of the model as a whole, and critics note linearity and top-down bias. Step models emphasize leaders heavily rather than ignoring them, and 'proven to fail' echoes the unsupported 70 percent claim.

Ethics, Purpose and Leading with Data

Good leaders do the right thing, do it fairly, and can show their working. This chapter gives you the frameworks, the fairness research and the simple numbers you need to make hard calls and explain them honestly.

You should be able to

  • Explain ethical leadership as both personal conduct and deliberate management of what others see rewarded and punished.
  • Apply consequence, duty, virtue, justice and care frameworks to a workplace dilemma and compare the answers they give.
  • Distinguish distributive, procedural and interactional justice and predict how each shapes reactions to a hard decision.
  • Calculate a break-even point and a simple staffing trade-off, then communicate the decision in a way that is honest about costs and uncertainty.

Chapter outline

  1. Why ethics is a leadership skill, not a personality trait
  2. Ethical leadership: being a moral person and a moral manager
  3. Frameworks for hard choices
  4. Organizational justice: why fairness drives reactions
  5. Purpose that means something
  6. Leading with data: break-even and staffing trade-offs made simple
  7. Explaining hard trade-offs

Big ideas

  • Ethical lapses usually come from situations, framing and blind spots rather than from bad character, so leaders who shape situations carry real responsibility.
  • Rest's four steps (awareness, judgment, intention, action) show where ethical behavior can break down; awareness often fails first because of ethical fading.
  • Ethical leadership (Brown, Treviño and Harrison, 2005) means being a moral person and a moral manager who makes ethics visible through communication, rewards and decisions.
  • Consequences, duties, virtue, justice and care are complementary lenses; where they disagree, you have found the real dilemma.
  • People judge fairness by outcomes, process and treatment; when outcomes are bad, fair process and honest, respectful explanations matter most.
  • Purpose is real only when it shapes choices and sometimes costs you something.
  • Break-even equals fixed costs divided by contribution margin; staffing choices trade fixed costs against per-unit margins, and the better option can reverse as volume changes.
  • Explain hard trade-offs by stating the decision, showing the reasoning and numbers, naming the costs, describing the process and voice, and treating people with dignity.

TermMeaning
Ethical fadingThe way the moral side of a decision drops out of view when it is framed only in business, numerical or procedural terms (Tenbrunsel and Messick, 2004).
Bounded ethicalityThe predictable blind spots that lead well-meaning people to act in ways they would not endorse on reflection, such as favoring people like themselves.

UtilitarianismA consequence-based ethical framework that judges an action right if it produces the greatest overall good and least harm for everyone affected.
DeontologyA duty-based ethical framework, associated with Kant, holding that some actions are right or wrong in themselves regardless of their consequences.
Virtue ethicsAn ethical approach, rooted in Aristotle, that asks what a person of good character would do and which habits a choice builds.

Practice questions

1. A studio manager is told to raise class fill rates and quietly starts overbooking classes beyond safe floor space, never thinking of it as an ethical choice. Which concept best explains this?

  1. A. Distributive injustice
  2. B. Ethical fading
  3. C. Moral intention failure caused by greed
  4. D. The fair process effect
Show answer

B. Ethical fading describes how the moral side of a decision disappears when it is framed as a numbers target. It is tempting to call it an intention failure, but the manager never noticed the ethical issue at all, which is an awareness failure driven by framing.

2. According to Treviño, Hartman and Brown, what does a leader who is personally honest but never talks about ethics or acts on lapses risk being seen as?

  1. A. A hypocritical leader
  2. B. A transformational leader
  3. C. An ethically neutral leader
  4. D. A moral manager
Show answer

C. Being a moral person alone can leave followers seeing the leader as ethically neutral. A moral manager actively makes ethics visible. Hypocrisy is the opposite pattern: talking about ethics while behaving otherwise.

3. A clinic has fixed costs of $30,000 a month, earns $80 per visit and has variable costs of $20 per visit. What is its break-even volume?

  1. A. 375 visits
  2. B. 300 visits
  3. C. 1,500 visits
  4. D. 500 visits
Show answer

D. Contribution margin is $80 minus $20, which is $60. Break-even is $30,000 divided by $60, which is 500 visits. Dividing by the $80 price (375) ignores variable costs, a common error.

4. Employees accept a pay freeze with little complaint at one site but react angrily at another, even though the freeze is identical. What most likely differs?

  1. A. Procedural and interactional justice in how the freeze was decided and explained
  2. B. Distributive justice, because the outcome was different
  3. C. The employees' Big Five personality traits
  4. D. The organization's legal obligations
Show answer

A. The outcome was identical, so distributive justice is the same. Research such as Greenberg's pay-cut study shows that the process and the quality of explanation strongly shape reactions to the same unfavorable outcome.

5. In the Riverbend example, why might hiring become the better financial choice even though overtime earns more today?

  1. A. Because overtime visits have higher variable costs than new-hire visits, so overtime always loses
  2. B. Because hiring adds a fixed cost but keeps a higher margin per visit, so above about 203 extra visits it yields more
  3. C. Because hiring lowers the practice's existing fixed costs
  4. D. Because break-even visits fall when overtime is used
Show answer

B. Hiring adds $8,540 of fixed cost but keeps the full $72 margin, while overtime earns only $30 per visit. The lines cross at 8,540 divided by 42, about 203 visits. Overtime does not always lose: below the crossover it earns more.

6. Which statement about Goodhart's law is most accurate?

  1. A. Organizations should stop using metrics because they are always gamed
  2. B. It proves that financial measures are more reliable than clinical ones
  3. C. Once a measure becomes a target, behavior shifts to hit it and it can stop reflecting the underlying goal
  4. D. It applies only to public sector organizations
Show answer

C. Goodhart's law warns that targets distort the measures they are based on. The practical response is to pair metrics, watch for gaming and remember what each number represents, not to abandon measurement.

Apply it at work

  1. Think of a recent decision at your workplace that was framed mainly in numbers. Who was affected who never appeared in the discussion, and how might naming them have changed the decision?
  2. Rate your own leadership on the "moral manager" side: in the last month, when did you talk openly about ethics, and when did you visibly reward or correct behavior on ethical grounds?
  3. Choose a real trade-off in your organization and run it through all five lenses. Where do the lenses disagree, and what is the real dilemma they reveal?
  4. Build a simple break-even calculation for one service, program or product you are responsible for. What is your margin of safety, and which single assumption, if it were 10 percent worse, would worry you most?

Integration: Leading the Whole System

Motivation, safety, teams, culture and change are not separate topics; they are parts of one living system, and you are part of it too. This chapter shows how to lead the whole thing, including yourself, and ends with a plan you can start on Monday.

You should be able to

  • Analyze an organizational problem as a system of connected parts, identifying reinforcing loops and points of leverage.
  • Integrate ideas on motivation, psychological safety, teams, culture and change into a single diagnosis of a workplace.
  • Design a one-page change plan with levers, pilots, measures and a communication approach.
  • Evaluate your own risk of burnout using the job demands-resources model and construct a personal leadership development plan.

Chapter outline

  1. Thinking in systems
  2. How the pieces connect
  3. Diagnose before you act
  4. Building a change plan
  5. Leader wellbeing and burnout
  6. Your personal leadership development plan
  7. Putting it all together

Big ideas

  • Organizational problems usually come from how parts are connected, so treating one symptom at a time rarely lasts.
  • The congruence model says performance depends on the fit among the work, the people, formal structures and the informal culture.
  • Reinforcing loops drive upward spirals (safety, voice, learning, trust) and downward ones (workload, exhaustion, turnover); leaders can act at any link.
  • Diagnose with several kinds of evidence, triangulate, involve the people closest to the work, and look for strengths as well as problems.
  • A good change plan fits on one page, pulls two or three reinforcing levers, starts with a pilot, tracks leading indicators, and stops something to make room.
  • Burnout (exhaustion, cynicism, reduced efficacy) is an occupational phenomenon driven by mismatches in workload, control, reward, community, fairness and values.
  • The JD-R model shows that reducing demands and adding resources both matter; organizational fixes tend to outperform individual coping alone.
  • A personal development plan works best when it combines strengths, feedback, stretch experiences, if-then plans and a protected recovery practice.

Practice questions

1. A clinic gives retention bonuses to stop resignations, but staff keep leaving because workloads remain heavy. What does a systems view suggest went wrong?

  1. A. The bonuses were too small to matter
  2. B. The fix treated one link in a reinforcing loop while leaving the drivers of the loop untouched
  3. C. Retention is a lagging indicator and cannot be changed
  4. D. Staff were not resilient enough
Show answer

B. The bonus addressed turnover but not the workload and exhaustion that kept the burnout spiral turning. Making the bonus larger is tempting, but it still leaves the loop's drivers in place.

2. According to Nadler and Tushman's congruence model, why might a new scheduling system cause frustration even if the software is good?

  1. A. Because software always reduces engagement
  2. B. Because culture cannot be changed
  3. C. Because the other components, such as roles, skills and norms, were not adjusted to fit the new work
  4. D. Because the strategy was wrong
Show answer

C. The model says performance depends on fit. Changing one component without adjusting the others creates misfit that shows up as workarounds and frustration. Nothing in the scenario suggests the strategy itself was wrong.

3. Which is the best example of a leading indicator for a plan to reduce staff burnout?

  1. A. Monthly unscheduled overtime hours
  2. B. Annual staff turnover
  3. C. Year-end profit
  4. D. Number of resignation letters last year
Show answer

A. Unscheduled overtime moves quickly and signals whether workload is easing. Turnover and resignations are lagging indicators: important, but they show results only after the damage is done.

4. In the job demands-resources model, what is the buffering effect?

  1. A. Demands automatically decrease when engagement rises
  2. B. Resources such as autonomy and support reduce the strain produced by the same level of demands
  3. C. Burnout protects people from further stress
  4. D. Resources only matter for highly engaged employees
Show answer

B. Resources buffer demands: the same workload produces less strain when people have control, support and feedback. The model does not say demands shrink on their own.

5. What did meta-analyses of burnout interventions for physicians suggest?

  1. A. Individual interventions work and organizational ones do not
  2. B. No intervention has any effect
  3. C. Burnout is best treated as a medical diagnosis
  4. D. Both types produce modest benefits, with some evidence that organizational changes have larger effects
Show answer

D. West and colleagues found modest benefits from both approaches, and Panagioti and colleagues found larger effects for organization-directed interventions. Claiming individual approaches alone work is the tempting but unsupported option.

6. Which statement is an implementation intention?

  1. A. I want to be a better listener this year
  2. B. I will try my best to be supportive
  3. C. If someone raises a concern in a meeting, then I will thank them before responding
  4. D. Listening is one of my top strengths
Show answer

C. An implementation intention links a specific situation to a specific action in if-then form. A general wish to listen better is a vague goal, which research shows is less likely to change behavior.

  3. Sketch a one-page change plan for that problem. What is one thing you would stop doing to make room for it?
  4. Rate your own current job demands and job resources. Which of the six areas of work life is most out of balance for you, and what one boundary or recovery practice will you protect?
