Activity 1

Hygiene or Motivator?

Herzberg's two-factor theory

Sort each workplace factor into hygiene factors (they prevent dissatisfaction when adequate, but do not by themselves motivate) or motivators (they come from the work itself and drive satisfaction). Drag a card onto a column, or select a card with a tap, click, Enter or Space and then press Place here on a column.

Context of the job. Absence causes dissatisfaction.

    Motivators

      Score: 0 / 12
      Instructor notes

      Objective: Learners distinguish extrinsic job-context factors from intrinsic job-content factors and explain why fixing hygiene issues removes dissatisfaction without necessarily producing motivation (Herzberg, Mausner & Snyderman, 1959).

      • Discussion use: Ask learners to name one hygiene factor and one motivator they could change in their own unit next month, and predict the effect of each.
      • Live session: Project the activity and have the group vote before each placement. Pause on salary: Herzberg classed it as hygiene, yet many learners argue it motivates. Use this to discuss critiques of the theory, including the critical incident method and later research on pay as recognition.
      • Extension: Link to job enrichment and the Job Characteristics Model (Hackman & Oldham).

      Name That Stage

      Tuckman (1965) and Tuckman & Jensen (1977)

      Read each team snapshot and choose the stage of group development it best illustrates. Feedback appears as soon as you choose. One attempt per scenario.

        Score: 0 / 10
        Instructor notes

        Objective: Learners recognize behavioral cues of forming, storming, norming, performing and adjourning, and select leader behaviors suited to each stage.

        • Discussion use: Have each learner post the stage their current team is in, the evidence, and one leader move that would help it progress.
        • Live session: Assign each breakout room a stage and ask it to act out a two-minute team meeting; the rest of the class names the stage.
        • Caveat to raise: The stages are a heuristic, not a fixed sequence. Teams can regress to storming when membership or goals change, and Gersick's punctuated equilibrium model offers an alternative for time-bound teams.

          Instructor notes

          Objective: Learners recall the sequence of Kotter's eight-stage process and apply it by diagnosing which stage a concrete leader action serves.

          • Discussion use: Ask learners to describe a change they lived through, identify the stage that was skipped or rushed, and connect it to Kotter's eight common errors (for example, declaring victory too soon).
          • Live session: Run Part A as a timed team race, then debrief why urgency comes before vision in Kotter's model. Contrast with Kotter's later "accelerate" framing (2014), which runs steps concurrently.
          • Critique to raise: The model is top-down and practitioner-derived; pair it with Lewin's unfreeze-change-refreeze and bottom-up change perspectives.

            Instructor notes

            Objective: Learners separate what a culture shows, what it says, and what it takes for granted, and explain why gaps between espoused values and assumptions predict change resistance (Schein, Organizational Culture and Leadership).

            • Discussion use: Ask learners to post one artifact from their workplace and infer the assumption beneath it, then have peers challenge the inference.
            • Live session: Focus on items 5 to 8 versus 9 to 12. The same topic (for example, work-life balance) can appear as a stated value and as a contradictory assumption. Ask: which one actually predicts behavior?
            • Teaching tip: Stories, rituals and jargon are artifacts even though they feel "cultural"; learners often misplace them as values.
            Activity 5

            Which Justice?

            Organizational justice dimensions (Colquitt, 2001)

            Decide whether each scenario is mainly about distributive justice (fairness of outcomes), procedural justice (fairness of the process used to reach outcomes), or interactional justice (fairness of treatment, meaning respect and dignity plus honest, timely explanations). Colquitt's model splits interactional justice into interpersonal and informational components; both count as interactional here.

              Score: 0 / 9
              Instructor notes

              Objective: Learners diagnose which justice dimension is at stake and choose a leader response that targets it, drawing on Adams (equity), Thibaut & Walker and Leventhal (process), Bies & Moag (interactional), and Colquitt's (2001) four-factor measure.

              • Discussion use: Give learners a real-looking but fictional layoff announcement and ask them to rewrite it to improve procedural and informational justice without changing the outcome.
              • Live session: Poll the room on scenario 4 and scenario 8. Both involve a decision, but one is about how the decision was made and the other about how it was communicated. The distinction drives the debrief.
              • Key point: Research on the fair process effect shows that fair procedures and respectful treatment soften reactions to unfavorable outcomes.
              Activity 6

              PERMA Workplace Audit

              Seligman's well-being model, Flourish (2011)

              Reflective, not graded

              Rate your own team from 1 (rarely true) to 5 (consistently true) on each PERMA element and add a note with evidence. The radar chart updates as you go, and the two lowest elements get evidence-based action ideas. Nothing is sent anywhere; use Download my audit to keep a copy.

              Instructor notes

              Objective: Learners apply Seligman's (2011) five pillars of well-being (Positive emotion, Engagement, Relationships, Meaning, Accomplishment) to a real team and plan targeted, evidence-informed interventions.

              • Discussion use: Learners attach their downloaded audit and write a short plan for one action, including how they would know within 30 days whether it worked.
              • Live session: Pair learners from different sectors (for example, a sports club and a clinic) to compare radar shapes and trade intervention ideas.
              • Caution: This is a structured reflection, not a validated measure. For research-grade assessment point learners to the PERMA-Profiler (Butler & Kern, 2016) or the Workplace PERMA Profiler.
