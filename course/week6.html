Week 6: Servant and Transformational Leadership

Two of the most influential ideas about leadership start from different places: one from serving people, the other from inspiring them. This week we compare them, and ask where each fits and where each struggles.

Start with two leaders

Consider two fictional leaders. Marisol runs Clearwater Pilates, a studio of eleven instructors. She is quiet, rarely gives speeches, and spends most of her time asking instructors what they need, clearing obstacles and coaching them toward their own goals, including two who now run satellite classes of their own. Theo is the athletic director at Northgate Rowing Club. He is energetic, paints a vivid picture of what the club could become, challenges his coaches to rethink training, and knows what matters to each of them. Both are admired. Both get results. They are leading in different ways.

1. Servant leadership

Robert Greenleaf introduced the idea of servant leadership in his 1970 essay "The Servant as Leader." His central claim was that great leaders are servants first: the desire to serve comes before the desire to lead. He proposed what he called the best test: do those served grow as persons? Do they, while being served, become healthier, wiser, freer, more autonomous and more likely themselves to become servants? He also asked what the effect is on the least privileged in society.

Later scholars described characteristics often associated with servant leaders, including listening, empathy, healing, awareness, persuasion rather than coercion, foresight, stewardship, commitment to the growth of people and building community. Marisol fits the pattern well. Her success is visible mainly in the growth of the people around her.

Servant leadership has clear strengths in settings where the work is care, such as clinics, wellness practices and coaching, because it models the behavior the organization wants its staff to show clients. It also has limits. Critics note that it can be hard to define and measure consistently, that some versions overlap heavily with other positive leadership styles, and that "serving" can slide into avoiding hard decisions if the leader loses sight of the mission.

2. Transformational leadership

James MacGregor Burns, a political scientist, contrasted transactional leadership, based on exchanges between leader and follower, with transforming leadership, in which leaders and followers raise one another to higher levels of motivation and morality. Bernard Bass developed the idea for organizations and measured it. In his model, transformational leadership has four components, often called the four I's.

Idealized influence means acting as a role model whom people trust and want to emulate. Inspirational motivation means articulating an appealing vision and communicating optimism about reaching it. Intellectual stimulation means challenging assumptions and encouraging people to think in new ways. Individualized consideration means attending to each person's needs and coaching their development. Theo shows all four.

Bass also argued that transformational leadership builds on transactional leadership rather than replacing it. Clear expectations and fair rewards, which he called contingent reward, are the foundation. A leader who inspires but never follows through on promises will not be trusted for long. Research has generally linked transformational leadership to follower satisfaction, motivation and performance, although critics such as Daan van Knippenberg and Sim Sitkin have argued that the concept mixes leader behaviors with their effects, which makes causal claims harder to test.

3. Authentic leadership

Authentic leadership, developed by Bruce Avolio, William Gardner, Fred Walumbwa and colleagues, focuses on the leader's inner compass. It is often described with four components: self-awareness, relational transparency (being open about who you are and what you think), balanced processing (weighing information fairly, including views that challenge your own) and an internalized moral perspective (acting on your values under pressure). It connects well with the strengths work from weeks 1 and 2, and with the trust model from week 4.

4. Where each fits

The approaches overlap, since both servant and transformational leaders pay individual attention to people, but their primary focus differs. Transformational leaders focus first on the organization's goals and inspire people to pursue them; servant leaders focus first on the people and trust that the organization's goals will follow. In a crisis or a turnaround, such as a sports club facing declining membership, transformational vision may be what people need. In a stable, care-centered practice with experienced staff, servant leadership may fit better. Many effective leaders draw on both, choosing deliberately rather than by habit.

Putting it together

Neither approach is a personality type. Each is a set of behaviors you can practice. As you write this week's memo, analyze your admired leader through both lenses, and notice which behaviors you already use and which you avoid. That observation will feed directly into your change plan, because the plan will need both vision and service to succeed.

Applied memo 4 and change plan proposal

Applied memo 4: choosing a leadership approach. Write a memo advising a leader (yourself or someone you support) on which leadership approach best fits a specific situation they face, such as a new team, a merger of two locations, a turnaround or a period of rapid growth.

Your memo should:

  1. Describe the situation, the team and what is at stake.
  2. Explain servant, transformational and authentic leadership accurately and briefly.
  3. Evaluate how well each approach fits this situation, including its likely strengths and risks.
  4. Recommend an approach (or a deliberate combination) and describe three specific leader behaviors for the next month.

Format. 600 to 900 words in memo format (To, From, Date, Subject), with at least two course readings cited in APA style. You may instead submit a recorded five-minute briefing with a one-page outline; the same rubric applies. Due Sunday at 11:59 pm. 100 points.

Leading Change Plan proposal. One page: the organization (yours or one you know well), the change you want to lead, why it matters now, who will be affected and what success would look like. The proposal is part of the final project grade and is checked for completeness; its main purpose is to get detailed feedback early. Due Sunday at 11:59 pm.
