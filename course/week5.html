Start with a new program team

Crestwood Athletic Club, a fictional community sports organization, launches a youth development program. The director assembles a strong group: a head coach, a strength coach, a registered dietitian and a part-time program coordinator. Three months in, the program is behind schedule. Meetings are friendly but inconclusive, the dietitian and strength coach have quietly stopped cooperating, and nobody is sure who decides what.

Every one of them is good at their job. The question this week is why good people so often make a mediocre team, and what a leader can do about it.

1. Tuckman: teams develop over time

In 1965 Bruce Tuckman reviewed studies of small groups and proposed that groups tend to move through four stages: forming, storming, norming and performing. In 1977 Tuckman and Mary Ann Jensen added a fifth stage, adjourning, for groups that complete their work and disband.

In forming, members are polite and uncertain, learning the task and each other. In storming, differences surface: disagreements about goals, roles and approach, and sometimes competition for influence. In norming, the group works out shared expectations and cohesion grows. In performing, energy goes mainly into the task. In adjourning, members wrap up and, ideally, recognize what they achieved.

Two cautions keep the model honest. First, it describes a common tendency, not a fixed timetable; some teams skip or repeat stages, and new members or a changed goal can send a team back to storming. Connie Gersick's research on project teams found a different pattern in groups with deadlines: long stretches of steady work punctuated by a burst of change near the midpoint. Second, storming is not failure. Teams that suppress it often stall in a polite, shallow version of norming. Crestwood's friendly but inconclusive meetings may be exactly that.

2. Hackman: conditions, not chemistry

J. Richard Hackman spent decades studying teams from airline cockpits to orchestras. His conclusion was that leaders cannot directly make a team perform well, but they can create conditions that make good performance much more likely. He described five:

  • A real teamClear membership, shared work and some stability over time.
  • Compelling directionA purpose that is clear, challenging and consequential.
  • Enabling structureWell-designed tasks, the right size and mix, and clear norms of conduct.
  • Supportive contextRewards, information, training and resources that fit the team's work.
  • Expert coachingHelp at the right moments: launch, midpoint and completion.

Hackman argued that most of a team's eventual performance is set by these conditions before or near launch. At Crestwood, direction is fuzzy ("develop young athletes" could mean anything), the structure has no decision rules, and rewards still recognize individual departments. The friction between dietitian and strength coach is a symptom. The cause is design.

3. Conflict: task versus relationship

Karen Jehn distinguished task conflict, disagreement about the content of the work, from relationship conflict, interpersonal friction and personal dislike. Early thinking suggested task conflict was healthy and relationship conflict harmful. The evidence turned out to be more complicated. A meta-analysis by Carsten De Dreu and Laurie Weingart found that, on average, both types were negatively related to team performance. A later meta-analysis by Frank de Wit, Lindred Greer and Jehn found that task conflict is not uniformly harmful and can be neutral or helpful in some conditions, especially when relationship conflict is low and the team feels safe discussing disagreement.

The practical lesson is to keep disagreement focused on the work and to prevent it from becoming personal. That is easier with the psychological safety you studied last week, with agreed ways of disagreeing, and with a leader who steps in early when debate turns into blame.

4. Virtual and hybrid teams

Many of you lead teams spread across clinic locations, shifts or remote roles. Distance amplifies the problems above. Missing hallway conversations make storming harder to spot, silence is easy to mistake for agreement, and trust takes longer to build. Research on virtual teams suggests that clear norms for communication (which channel for what, expected response times) and deliberate relationship-building early in a team's life help a great deal.

Putting it together: the team charter

A team charter puts Hackman's conditions and a conflict plan on paper before trouble starts. It states the team's purpose, each member's role, working norms, how decisions are made, how conflict will be raised and resolved, and when the team will check in on how it is working. If Crestwood's director had spent one hour building a charter with the group at launch, many of the next three months' problems would have surfaced early, while they were still small. Your project team will build one this week.

Team charter

Task. With your project team of three or four classmates, write a team charter that will guide your work together through week 7, when you complete a team process review with peer feedback.

Your charter should include:

  1. Purpose: what the team is for and what a successful outcome looks like.
  2. Roles: who is responsible for what, including any rotating roles such as facilitator or editor.
  3. Norms: how you will communicate (channels, response times), prepare and meet commitments.
  4. Decision rules: which decisions need consensus, which need a majority and which one person can make.
  5. Conflict plan: how disagreements will be raised early, kept focused on the task and escalated if needed.
  6. Check-in schedule: when and how the team will review how it is working, not just what it has produced.

Format. Two to three pages, submitted once per team, with a short paragraph citing at least two course readings to justify your design choices. All members confirm the charter in the team forum. Due Sunday at 11:59 pm. 100 points, part of the team charter project grade.
