Overview

Last week you compared leadership approaches and proposed a change for your final project. Culture is where that change will succeed or fail, so this week gives you the tools to diagnose it.

We will study Edgar Schein's three levels of culture, the mechanisms leaders use to embed culture, and Kim Cameron's positive leadership strategies, with attention to small and founder-led organizations. Your activity is a culture walk through your own workplace. You will also close the team charter project with a team process review and peer feedback.

Short lecture: reading the culture you actually have

Reading time about 8 minutes. A captioned video version and transcript accompany this text in the live course.

Start with a lobby

Walk into Evergreen Integrative Health, a fictional wellness practice. The lobby has a wall poster listing its values: "Patient first. Teamwork. Balance." The schedule on the front-desk screen shows practitioners booked back to back from 7 am to 7 pm with no lunch. In the staff room, a whiteboard ranks everyone by weekly visits. The founder, who still sees patients, is praised in team meetings for working through weekends.

What is the culture here? The poster gives one answer. The schedule, the whiteboard and the praise give another. Edgar Schein's model helps explain the gap and tells you which answer to believe.

1. Schein's three levels

Edgar Schein defined culture as a pattern of shared basic assumptions that a group learned as it solved its problems of adapting to the outside world and holding itself together, which worked well enough to be considered valid and is taught to new members as the correct way to perceive, think and feel. He described culture at three levels.

Artifacts are everything you can see, hear and feel: the physical space, dress, language, stories, rituals, schedules and published documents. They are easy to observe but hard to interpret. A ranking whiteboard might mean healthy competition or relentless pressure; you cannot tell from the board alone.

Espoused beliefs and values are what the organization says it believes: stated strategies, goals and philosophies, like Evergreen's poster. They may or may not match behavior.

Basic underlying assumptions are the taken-for-granted beliefs that people no longer question or even notice. They are the deepest level and the real source of behavior. At Evergreen, the artifacts suggest an assumption something like "a good practitioner is one who never stops," which directly contradicts the espoused value of balance. When espoused values and assumptions conflict, behavior follows the assumptions. That is why a new values poster rarely changes anything.

2. How leaders embed culture

Schein argued that culture and leadership are two sides of the same coin, especially in young organizations where founders' beliefs become the group's assumptions. He identified primary embedding mechanisms, the most powerful ways leaders transmit culture:

  • AttentionWhat leaders pay attention to, measure and control on a regular basis.
  • Critical incidentsHow leaders react to crises and mistakes.
  • ResourcesHow leaders allocate budgets, time and people.
  • Role modelingWhat leaders deliberately teach and coach, and how they behave.
  • Rewards and statusWhat leaders reward, promote and praise.
  • People decisionsWhom leaders recruit, select, promote and let go.

Schein also described secondary mechanisms, such as structure, systems and procedures, rituals, physical space, stories and formal statements. These reinforce culture only when they are consistent with the primary mechanisms. Evergreen's values poster is a formal statement, a secondary mechanism, and it is losing to what the founder rewards and models every week.

3. Culture in small and founder-led organizations

Most of you work in organizations where a founder or small leadership group is highly visible. That is both a risk and an opportunity. Culture forms quickly and closely mirrors the leader's own habits, so small changes in leader behavior can shift it faster than in a large company. But founders often cannot see their own assumptions, because to them those assumptions are simply how things are done. A culture diagnosis that includes staff perspectives is essential.

4. Positive climate and positive meaning

Kim Cameron's research on positive leadership offers a set of strategies that leaders can use to shift climate, the more changeable, day-to-day feel of an organization, which over time shapes culture. He describes four: positive climate (fostering compassion, forgiveness and gratitude), positive relationships (building energizing networks and using strengths), positive communication (supportive, affirming language and honest feedback) and positive meaning (connecting work to personal values and a lasting benefit for others).

At Evergreen, the founder could start with the primary mechanisms: stop praising weekend work, build protected lunch breaks into the schedule template, replace the visit ranking with a board of patient outcome stories, and talk openly about why balance matters for patient care. Those changes speak directly to assumptions. The poster can stay; it might finally be true.

Putting it together

To diagnose culture, start with artifacts, compare them with espoused values, and infer the assumptions that explain any gaps. To change culture, work mainly through what leaders pay attention to, reward, model and fund. This week's culture walk and memo give you practice at both, and they form the diagnosis section of your final Leading Change Plan.

Discussion: your culture walk

Prompt. Do a culture walk: spend 20 to 30 minutes observing your workplace (or one you know well) as if you were a visitor. Record at least five artifacts, such as the physical space, schedules, rituals, stories people tell, what gets celebrated and what gets measured. Post your list, then compare the artifacts with the organization's espoused values and propose two underlying assumptions that could explain what you saw. Remove identifying details.

Replies. Respond to at least two classmates. In one reply, offer an alternative assumption that could explain the same artifacts. In the other, suggest which of Schein's primary embedding mechanisms a leader could use to shift one of the assumptions they identified.

Participation expectations

Initial post of 250 to 350 words by Thursday at 11:59 pm, so classmates have time to respond. Two substantive replies of at least 100 words each by Sunday at 11:59 pm, posted on different days. Cite at least one course reading in your initial post.

What a substantive reply looks like

It adds something: a new angle, a question that moves thinking forward, evidence, or a respectful challenge. "Great post, I agree" is a kind thing to say but does not count toward participation.

Applied memo 5 and team process review

Applied memo 5: culture diagnosis. Write a memo to the leader of the organization you chose for your Leading Change Plan, diagnosing its culture as it relates to your proposed change.

Your memo should:

  1. Summarize the most telling artifacts from your culture walk.
  2. Compare them with the organization's espoused beliefs and values, noting any gaps.
  3. Infer two or three basic underlying assumptions and explain the evidence for each.
  4. Explain how these assumptions will help or hinder your proposed change.
  5. Recommend two leader actions using Schein's primary embedding mechanisms or Cameron's positive strategies.

Format. 600 to 900 words in memo format (To, From, Date, Subject), with at least two course readings cited in APA style. You may instead submit a recorded five-minute briefing with a one-page outline; the same rubric applies. Due Sunday at 11:59 pm. 100 points.

Team process review. With your project team, revisit the charter you wrote in week 5. Together, write a one-page review: what worked, what did not, which stage of development you reached and one change you would make. Each member also completes a short confidential peer feedback form using strengths-based language. This completes the team charter project. Due Sunday at 11:59 pm.
