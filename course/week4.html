Week 4: Psychological Safety, Trust and Voice

Teams learn only when people say what they see: the mistake, the risk, the better idea. This week is about why people speak up or stay quiet, and what leaders do to tip the balance.

Overview

Last week you studied what motivates people. This week asks what makes it safe for them to contribute fully. A motivated employee who is afraid to mention a problem is still a risk to the team, the patient or the client.

We will cover Amy Edmondson's research on psychological safety and team learning, the difference between voice and silence, the building blocks of trust, and Kim Cameron's work on positive communication and supportive feedback. We pay special attention to clinical and high-accountability settings, where speaking up can prevent harm. Your activity is a short team climate survey, and your memo commits you to three leader behaviors you will practice.

Short lecture: why people speak up, and why they stay quiet

Start with a near miss

At Lakeview Sports Medicine, a fictional outpatient clinic, a newly hired athletic trainer notices that a rehabilitation protocol on the whiteboard lists the wrong weight limit for a post-surgical patient. She hesitates. The protocol was written by the senior physical therapist, who is respected, busy and known for sharp replies. She tells herself he must know what he is doing. The patient is fine only because another clinician catches the error the next day.

Nothing about her training or motivation was lacking. What was missing was the belief that it was safe to speak. That belief has a name and a strong body of research behind it.

1. Psychological safety

Amy Edmondson defines psychological safety as a shared belief held by members of a team that the team is safe for interpersonal risk taking. Interpersonal risks include asking a question that might look ignorant, admitting a mistake, raising a concern or offering an unfinished idea. In a psychologically safe team, people expect that doing these things will not lead to embarrassment, rejection or punishment.

Two features of the definition matter. First, it is shared: it describes a team's climate, not one person's confidence. Second, it concerns risk taking, not comfort. Safety does not mean everyone agrees or that feedback is gentle. It means candor is expected and not punished.

Edmondson's research began with a surprise. Studying hospital nursing units, she found that units with better leadership and relationships appeared to have higher rates of reported medication errors. On closer look, those units were not making more mistakes. They were more willing to report and discuss them, which is exactly what allows a team to learn. Her later study of work teams found that psychological safety was associated with learning behavior, such as seeking feedback and discussing errors, and that learning behavior was in turn associated with team performance.

2. Safety is not the same as low standards

A common misunderstanding is that psychological safety means going easy on people. Edmondson addresses this directly by pairing safety with a second dimension: motivation and accountability for meeting demanding goals.

High standards without safety put people in the anxiety zone, where errors get hidden because admitting them is dangerous. Safety without standards creates a comfort zone: pleasant, but not much learning. Neither creates the learning zone, where people hold each other to high standards and feel free to speak up about what is getting in the way. In clinical work the learning zone is not a luxury. It is a patient safety requirement.

3. Voice, silence and trust

Researchers use the term voice for speaking up with ideas, questions or concerns intended to improve things, and silence for withholding them. Elizabeth Morrison and Frances Milliken described organizational silence as a collective pattern that can take hold when leaders fear negative feedback and employees learn that speaking up is futile or risky. Two beliefs drive silence: "it will not make a difference" and "it will cost me." A leader has to address both.

Trust sits underneath voice. A widely used model by Roger Mayer, James Davis and David Schoorman describes three qualities that make a leader trustworthy in others' eyes: ability (competence in the relevant area), benevolence (genuine care for the other person's interests) and integrity (adherence to principles the other person finds acceptable). A clinic owner who is brilliant at treatment but reacts to bad news with blame may score high on ability and low on benevolence, and her team will stay quiet accordingly.

4. What leaders can do

Edmondson summarizes the leader's role in three moves. Frame the work: make clear that the work is uncertain or interdependent, so everyone's input is needed. Invite participation: ask genuine questions, acknowledge your own fallibility and create structures for input. Respond productively: thank people for speaking up, respond to bad news without blame, and act on what you hear.

Kim Cameron's research on positive communication adds a practical ratio. In high-performing teams, supportive and appreciative statements outnumber critical ones, while problems are still raised directly. Cameron describes supportive communication as problem-oriented rather than person-oriented, specific rather than vague, and owned rather than hidden behind "they say."

Imagine the senior therapist at Lakeview practicing these moves. At the morning huddle he says, "These protocols change often and I make mistakes. If you see something that looks off, tell me. I would much rather hear it from you than find out from a patient." When someone does flag an error, he thanks them publicly and fixes it. That takes less than a minute a day, and it changes what the whole team believes is safe.

Putting it together

Psychological safety is a team-level belief, built or eroded by many small leader responses. It supports learning, not comfort, and it works best alongside clear, high standards. Voice depends on trust, and trust depends on ability, benevolence and integrity. This week you will measure your own team's climate and pick three behaviors to practice.

Discussion: reading your team's climate

Prompt. Share a short team climate survey adapted from Edmondson's published items with a team you lead or belong to (five to seven people is enough; keep it anonymous). Summarize the results without identifying anyone. Which items scored highest and lowest? Using this week's concepts, explain what you think is driving the pattern, and name which zone of the safety and accountability grid the team is closest to.

Replies. Respond to at least two classmates. In one reply, suggest a specific leader behavior (framing, inviting or responding) that fits their results. In the other, ask a question that would help them tell whether low scores reflect low safety, low trust or something else.

Applied memo 3: three behaviors for psychological safety

Scenario. Write a memo to yourself as a leader, or to a leader you support, setting out three behaviors you will practice over the next month to raise psychological safety on a specific team.

Your memo should:

  1. Describe the team and summarize its climate, drawing on your survey results.
  2. Explain the current state using psychological safety, voice and silence, and the trust model.
  3. Name three specific leader behaviors, each linked to framing the work, inviting participation or responding productively.
  4. Explain why the research predicts each behavior will help, and how it maintains high standards.
  5. Describe how you will know it is working, with at least one measure you can check in 30 to 60 days.

Format. 600 to 900 words in memo format (To, From, Date, Subject), with at least two course readings cited in APA style. You may instead submit a recorded five-minute briefing with a one-page outline; the same rubric applies. Due Sunday at 11:59 pm. 100 points.
