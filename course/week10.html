Overview

This is the final week. You have studied motivation, individual differences, psychological safety, teams, leadership approaches, culture, change, ethics and data. This week asks you to see them as one system and to apply that system to your own organization in your final Leading Change Plan.

We also turn to a topic that leaders often neglect: their own wellbeing. Leading change is demanding, and burned-out leaders cannot build flourishing teams. Early in the week you exchange draft plans with a peer reviewer. By Sunday you submit your final plan with a recorded briefing, and your strengths-based reflection. An optional live showcase lets you share your briefing with the class.

Short lecture: one system, one leader

Reading time about 8 minutes. A captioned video version and transcript accompany this text in the live course.

Start with a clinic director

Jordan directs Riverside Community Rehab, a fictional outpatient clinic with fourteen staff. Over ten weeks in a course much like this one, Jordan diagnosed a motivation problem among therapists, measured low psychological safety at the front desk, chartered a cross-functional team, traced a culture of overwork back to its founder's assumptions, and designed a change to patient scheduling. Jordan is also exhausted, answering messages at midnight and quietly wondering whether leadership is worth it.

Both halves of Jordan's story matter this week. The first is about integration. The second is about the leader.

1. The course as one system

It is tempting to treat each week's theory as a separate tool. In practice they are connected, and changes in one ripple through the others.

Motivation depends on whether people's needs for autonomy, competence and relatedness are met, and whether their strengths are seen. People who feel competent and connected are more willing to take interpersonal risks, which builds psychological safety. Safety allows teams to work through storming instead of around it, and to use task conflict productively. The patterns teams repeat, and that leaders reward, become culture. And culture decides whether a change sticks or quietly reverts. A well-led change, in turn, can restore motivation by giving people a real voice and visible wins, which is the loop Jordan's plan aims to start.

The same connections explain why single fixes so often fail. A new recognition program (motivation) will not survive a culture that punishes mistakes. A team charter (teams) will not help if nobody feels safe to invoke it. Your final plan should show that you see these links, which is why the rubric rewards integration, not just coverage.

2. The leader at the center

At the center of the system sits the leader, whose own motivation and wellbeing shape everything around them. Christina Maslach's research describes burnout as a response to chronic workplace stress with three dimensions: exhaustion, cynicism or detachment from the work, and a reduced sense of professional efficacy. The World Health Organization classifies burnout as an occupational phenomenon resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed, rather than as a medical condition.

The job demands-resources model, developed by Arnold Bakker and Evangelia Demerouti, offers a useful way to think about prevention. Every job has demands, such as workload, emotional labor and time pressure, and resources, such as autonomy, support, feedback and development opportunities. High demands without matching resources drive exhaustion. Resources buffer demands and fuel engagement. The model applies to leaders as much as to anyone else.

For Jordan, demands are high: a change effort on top of daily operations. But resources can be built. A small guiding coalition shares the load. A peer network of other clinic directors provides support. Protected time for the work Jordan finds most meaningful, such as mentoring new therapists, restores energy. Using signature strengths, as you learned in weeks 1 and 2, is itself a resource: work that draws on your strengths tends to energize rather than drain.

Cameron's positive leadership strategies apply inward too. Positive meaning, positive relationships and a positive climate are as important for the leader as for the team. Leaders who model sustainable habits also give their teams permission to do the same, which is an embedding mechanism in Schein's sense.

3. Your two final pieces

The Leading Change Plan brings the system view to your own organization: a culture diagnosis, a stakeholder map, a staged plan informed by Kotter and the course's positive leadership practices, measures of success, and a five-minute recorded briefing. The strengths-based reflection brings the leader view to you: your character strengths results from week 1, the reflected best self feedback from week 2, your weekly journal, and a development plan that builds on what is already best in you.

Treat the peer review exchange early in the week as a gift. A reviewer who has studied the same frameworks can see gaps you cannot, and giving feedback sharpens your own thinking.

Putting it together

Positive leadership is not a set of techniques applied to other people. It is a way of seeing organizations as systems where people can flourish, and a commitment to be a leader who can sustain that work over years, not weeks. Peer review exchange

Prompt. Post a draft of your Leading Change Plan (or a detailed outline) in your peer review group by Tuesday at 11:59 pm. Review the draft assigned to you and post your feedback by Thursday at 11:59 pm. Structure your feedback in three parts: two specific strengths of the plan and why they work; one place where the plan could better connect the course frameworks as a system; and one question the author should answer before submitting.

Replies. Reply to the feedback you receive, saying what you will change and why. You may also post your recorded briefing for the optional live showcase and comment on at least one classmate's briefing.

Participation expectations

Draft posted by Tuesday at 11:59 pm. Feedback of at least 250 words posted by Thursday at 11:59 pm. Your reply to your reviewer by Sunday at 11:59 pm. Use strengths-based language: specific, tied to outcomes, and honest about what needs work.

What a substantive reply looks like

It adds something: a new angle, a question that moves thinking forward, evidence, or a respectful challenge. "Great post, I agree" is a kind thing to say but does not count toward participation.

Final project: Leading Change Plan and strengths-based reflection

Leading Change Plan. Build on your week 6 proposal and week 7 culture diagnosis to present a complete plan for leading your chosen change.

Your plan should include:

  1. Culture diagnosis: artifacts, espoused values and underlying assumptions relevant to the change, revised from your memo 5 feedback.
  2. Stakeholder map: who is affected, their likely concerns and how resistance will be heard and used.
  3. Staged plan: the change organized around Kotter's steps, with specific positive leadership practices (such as strengths-based feedback, psychological safety behaviors and team charters) built into each stage.
  4. Measures: how you will know the change is working, including people measures and at least one operational or financial measure.
  5. Recorded briefing: a five-minute video or audio briefing presenting the plan to your organization's leaders.

Format. 2,500 to 3,500 words plus the stakeholder map and any tables, with at least six course readings cited in APA style, and the five-minute recorded briefing. Due Sunday at 11:59 pm. 100 points (30% of the course grade).

Strengths-based reflection. A reflective essay of 1,200 to 1,500 words drawing on your character strengths results from week 1, the reflected best self feedback from week 2 and your weekly journal. Describe how your signature strengths showed up in your leadership this term, one strength you overuse and its cost, and a development plan for the next six months with specific practices and a source of feedback. Due Sunday at 11:59 pm. 100 points (10% of the course grade).
