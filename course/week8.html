Overview

Everything in the course so far comes together in the work of leading change: motivation, psychological safety, teams, leadership approach and culture. This week we study the best-known model of change leadership, look critically at its limits, and treat resistance as useful information rather than an obstacle.

The centerpiece is the Summit Wellness leadership case simulation. You will lead a struggling multi-provider practice through a series of decisions, see the consequences, and then play again to compare paths. An optional live debrief session (recorded) walks through the most common decision patterns.

Short lecture: leading change without losing people

Reading time about 8 minutes. A captioned video version and transcript accompany this text in the live course.

Start with a software rollout

Bayside Movement Center, a fictional physical therapy and fitness practice, switches to a new scheduling and billing system. The owner announces the change by email on a Friday, the system goes live on Monday, and training is a single video. Within a month, two front-desk coordinators have resigned, clinicians are double-booked, and the owner is frustrated that staff "just resist anything new." The system itself is better than the old one. The rollout was the problem.

You will meet a very similar situation in this week's case simulation. The lecture gives you the frameworks to lead it well.

1. Kotter's eight steps

In a 1995 article on why transformation efforts fail, John Kotter drew on his observation of many organizations trying to change. He argued that successful change moves through a series of stages, and that skipping steps creates only an illusion of speed. He later expanded the argument in his book Leading Change.

The early steps build readiness. Urgency means helping people see honestly why the status quo is not acceptable. A guiding coalition is a group with enough credibility, expertise and influence to lead the effort; in a small practice it should include respected frontline staff, not just owners. A vision gives direction that can be explained in a few sentences.

The middle steps engage people: communicating the vision far more often than seems necessary, and through behavior as well as words; removing barriers such as systems, structures or managers that block the change; and creating short-term wins that are visible, unambiguous and clearly tied to the change. The final steps sustain it: using credibility from early wins to tackle bigger problems, and anchoring the new approaches in the culture, which connects directly to last week's work on Schein.

Bayside's owner jumped straight to implementation. There was no urgency people understood, no coalition, a vision stated only as "we are switching systems," and no attention to barriers such as training time. Kotter would not be surprised by the result.

2. Lewin in the background

Kurt Lewin, a founder of social psychology, is widely credited with a three-stage model: unfreeze the current state, change (or move), and refreeze the new state so it becomes stable. Lewin's broader work also gave us force field analysis, which weighs the forces driving change against those restraining it. Scholars have debated how much of the tidy three-step model Lewin actually wrote, as opposed to later textbook summaries, but the core insight holds: people need a reason to let go of the old way before they can adopt a new one, and the new way needs support to last.

3. Resistance as information

Leaders often treat resistance as a defect in employees. Jeffrey Ford, Laurie Ford and Angelo D'Amelio argued that this view is one-sided. Resistance can be a sign of real problems with the change, a response to how it was communicated, or a reaction to broken agreements from past changes. Resisters are often the people who care most about the work and who know where the plan will break.

This is where earlier weeks pay off. If psychological safety is low, concerns go underground and surface as quiet non-compliance. If the change threatens autonomy or competence, self-determination theory predicts pushback. A leader who listens to resistance, asks what it reveals and adjusts the plan often turns critics into the strongest members of the guiding coalition.

4. A critical view of step models

Kotter's model is popular because it is clear and practical. It also has critics. Step models can make change look more linear and controllable than it is, and researchers have noted that the model's empirical support comes mainly from case observation rather than systematic testing. Claims that most change efforts fail, often quoted as 70%, have also been challenged for lacking solid evidence. Use the steps as a checklist of what tends to matter, not as a guarantee, and expect to loop back.

Putting it together

At Bayside, a better rollout would have started with listening sessions, built a small coalition including a respected front-desk coordinator, explained the reason for the change in terms of patient experience, protected training time, and celebrated the first week without double-bookings. In the simulation, watch for the same choices. Notice which decisions raise short-term finances while eroding trust, and which build engagement that pays off later.

Discussion: comparing your two paths

Prompt. After playing the Summit Wellness simulation twice, describe your two decision paths and your final organizational health scores. Which decision had the biggest effect, and why? Use Kotter's steps and at least one idea from an earlier week (such as psychological safety, self-determination theory or Schein's levels) to explain the difference between the two outcomes.

Replies. Respond to at least two classmates. In one reply, compare their paths with yours and point out a decision that played out differently. In the other, suggest how the resistance they encountered in the simulation could have been used as information.

Participation expectations

What a substantive reply looks like

It adds something: a new angle, a question that moves thinking forward, evidence, or a respectful challenge. "Great post, I agree" is a kind thing to say but does not count toward participation.

Leadership case simulation debrief

Task. Play the Summit Wellness leadership case simulation at least twice, choosing a meaningfully different path the second time. Then write a structured debrief comparing your two decision paths.

Your debrief should:

  1. Summarize each path in a short table: decision, choice made and the effect on the four indicators.
  2. Report your final organizational health scores for both paths.
  3. Explain the outcomes using Kotter's steps and at least two other course theories.
  4. Identify the single most important decision and what it teaches about leading change.
  5. Describe how you will apply one lesson in your own Leading Change Plan.

Format. 800 to 1,100 words plus the path table, with at least two course readings cited in APA style. You may instead submit a recorded six-minute briefing with the path table; the same rubric applies. Due Sunday at 11:59 pm. 100 points.
