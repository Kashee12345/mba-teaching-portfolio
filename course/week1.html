Week 1: The Positive Lens on Organizational Behavior

Organizational behavior asks how people and groups actually behave at work. This week we add a second question that changes what leaders notice: what is going right, and how do we get more of it?

Overview

Welcome to the course. Most of you were promoted, hired or self-employed into leadership because you were excellent at the work itself: treating patients, coaching athletes, teaching classes or running operations. Leading people draws on a different body of knowledge, and this week we map it out.

We start with what organizational behavior (OB) studies and its three levels of analysis: the individual, the group and the organization. We then look at evidence-based management, a disciplined habit of asking what we actually know. Finally we meet positive organizational scholarship and the idea of positive deviance, which together set the tone for the whole course. Your first discussion is a "best self at work" story, and you will take a character strengths assessment that you will return to in weeks 2 and 10.

Start with a staff meeting

Imagine the monthly staff meeting at Ridgeline Family Chiropractic, a fictional six-person practice. The agenda has four items: two billing errors, a scheduling complaint, a late equipment delivery and a reminder about charting deadlines. Everyone leaves knowing exactly what went wrong last month. Nobody leaves knowing what went right, even though the practice had its best patient-retention month in two years.

That meeting is not unusual, and it is not foolish. Problems demand attention. But it shows a habit this course asks you to examine: leaders tend to study failure closely and let success go unexplained. This week gives you the vocabulary to do both.

1. What organizational behavior studies

Organizational behavior is the systematic study of how people think, feel and act in organizations, and how that behavior affects performance and wellbeing. It borrows from psychology, sociology, anthropology and economics. Its key move is to look at work at three levels of analysis.

The individual level covers personality, perception, attitudes, emotions and motivation. Why does one massage therapist thrive on a packed schedule while another burns out? The group level covers teams, communication, norms, conflict and leadership. Why does the morning crew at a fitness studio cover for each other while the evening crew does not? The organization level covers structure, culture, systems and change. Why does a two-clinic practice feel like two different companies?

Classifying a problem by level is useful because the fix usually lives at the same level. A leader who sends one struggling employee to a training course when the real issue is a broken handoff between teams has aimed at the wrong level. Many stubborn problems also cross levels. A culture that punishes mistakes (organization) shapes how safe a team feels (group), which shapes whether an individual speaks up about an error.

2. Evidence-based management

Medicine has long asked practitioners to base treatment on the best available evidence rather than habit or authority. Evidence-based management, promoted by scholars such as Denise Rousseau, Jeffrey Pfeffer and Robert Sutton, applies the same discipline to leadership. It asks a leader to combine four sources of evidence: findings from scientific research, data from the organization itself, the professional judgment of experienced practitioners, and the values and concerns of the people affected by the decision.

Consider a yoga studio owner who reads that open-plan break areas improve collaboration. Research may support the idea in general, but her own data show that instructors rarely overlap, her experienced staff say they value a quiet space to reset between classes, and they have told her so. Weighing all four sources leads to a better decision than any single one. Throughout this course, when you meet a theory, ask two questions: what is the evidence for it, and does it fit the situation in front of me?

3. Positive organizational scholarship

Around the turn of the century, Martin Seligman and Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi argued that psychology had become very good at understanding illness and damage, and far less attentive to what makes life worth living. They called for a positive psychology that would study strengths, wellbeing and flourishing with the same rigor. A few years later, Kim Cameron, Jane Dutton and Robert Quinn carried the idea into organizations under the name positive organizational scholarship. It studies the conditions that let people and organizations perform at their best: generosity, resilience, meaning, high-quality connections and excellence.

Positive organizational scholarship is sometimes mistaken for "think positive" slogans. It is not. It is a research tradition that measures its claims, and it takes difficulty seriously. Many of its best studies look at organizations that thrived through layoffs, disasters or grief. The aim is not to ignore problems but to stop treating the absence of problems as the finish line.

4. Positive deviance

In everyday speech, deviance sounds negative. In research it simply means departing from the norm. Cameron describes a continuum: on the left, negatively deviant performance such as errors, harm and ineffectiveness; in the middle, normal or expected performance; and on the right, positively deviant performance, meaning results or behavior far better than expected.

Most management tools aim at closing the gap between the left and the middle: fixing errors, resolving conflict, meeting targets. That work matters. But getting to normal is not the same as getting to excellent, and the practices that move a team from normal to extraordinary are often different ones. Gretchen Spreitzer and Scott Sonenshein describe positive deviance as intentional behavior that departs from norms in honorable ways, such as a front-desk coordinator who notices a nervous first-time patient and walks her to the treatment room personally.

Back at Ridgeline, a positive-deviance question would sound like this: "Retention was the best in two years. What did we do differently, and how do we make it normal?" The answer might be that one chiropractor started calling new patients the evening after their first visit. That practice was invisible until someone asked.

Putting it together

This course holds two questions side by side. The traditional OB question asks what is going wrong and why. The positive question asks what is going right and how to build on it. Good leaders ask both, at the right level of analysis, and they check their answers against evidence.

Your first task this week uses the second question on yourself. Think of a moment when you were at your best as a leader. What conditions made it possible? Who was there, what was at stake, and what strengths were you using? Those conditions are data, and by week 10 you will turn them into a development plan.

Discussion: your best self at work

Prompt. Introduce yourself to the class: your role, your organization type (no names needed) and what you hope to take from this course. Then tell a short "best self at work" story: one specific moment when you were at your best as a leader or colleague. Describe what happened and analyze the conditions that made it possible, using at least one idea from this week (a level of analysis, a source of evidence, or positive deviance).

Replies. Respond to at least two classmates. In one reply, name a strength you see in their story that they may not have named themselves. In the other, suggest a condition from their story that a leader could deliberately recreate for a team.

Participation expectations

What a substantive reply looks like

It adds something: a new angle, a question that moves thinking forward, evidence, or a respectful challenge. "Great post, I agree" is a kind thing to say but does not count toward participation.
