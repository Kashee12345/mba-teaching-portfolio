Overview

Last week you led change in a simulation. This week focuses on one of the hardest parts of that work: resource decisions that affect people's livelihoods and the mission. Staffing, pricing and investment choices need numbers, but numbers alone do not make a decision fair or wise.

We will study ethical leadership as defined by Michael Brown and Linda Treviño, the three forms of organizational justice, and purpose-driven organizations. Start with a spreadsheet

The owner of Pinecrest Recovery Studio, a fictional wellness business offering massage, stretching and recovery sessions, opens her monthly numbers. Evening massage slots run at 40% capacity while morning slots are full and turning people away. Labor is her largest cost. The spreadsheet points to an obvious move: cut evening hours and shift therapists to mornings. Two of her longest-serving therapists work only evenings because of childcare.

The data are clear. The decision is not. This week is about holding both at once.

1. Ethical leadership

Michael Brown, Linda Treviño and David Harrison defined ethical leadership as the demonstration of normatively appropriate conduct through personal actions and interpersonal relationships, and the promotion of such conduct to followers through two-way communication, reinforcement and decision-making. That definition has two halves, which Treviño and colleagues described as being a moral person and a moral manager.

The moral person is honest, fair and caring in their own behavior. The moral manager goes further: they talk about ethics openly, set clear standards, reward ethical conduct and discipline violations, and make decisions that visibly consider their effect on others. Research suggests that employees notice the second half most. A leader who is privately ethical but silent about it can be seen as ethically neutral, which leaves people unsure what is expected.

Purpose-driven leadership extends this by tying decisions to the organization's reason for existing. For Pinecrest, the purpose might be helping people recover and feel well in their bodies. A decision that saves money but reduces access for evening clients who cannot come in the morning should at least be weighed against that purpose, and some purpose-driven organizations track mission measures alongside financial ones for exactly this reason.

2. Organizational justice

Employees judge decisions not only by whether they like the result but by whether it was fair. Research on organizational justice identifies three forms.

Distributive justice concerns the fairness of outcomes: who gets what. It draws on J. Stacy Adams's equity theory, which holds that people compare their ratio of inputs to outcomes with that of others. Procedural justice concerns the fairness of the process. Gerald Leventhal proposed criteria for fair procedures: they are applied consistently, free of bias, based on accurate information, correctable, representative of the concerns of those affected, and consistent with ethical standards. Interactional justice, introduced by Robert Bies and Joseph Moag, concerns how people are treated as decisions are carried out: with dignity and respect, and with honest, timely explanations. Later researchers often split this into interpersonal and informational justice.

A consistent finding is that fair process and respectful treatment can make an unwelcome outcome much easier to accept. People who lose something in a decision are far more likely to stay committed if they were consulted, if the rules were applied consistently, and if the leader explained the reasons honestly and personally.

3. Leading with data without losing people

This week's lab gives you tools to see the economics of a decision: break-even volume, the effect of price changes on membership, and whether an investment pays back. Use them. A leader who decides on instinct alone risks both the business and the jobs it supports. But treat the numbers as one input among several, which is the evidence-based management approach from week 1.

At Pinecrest, the owner could share the capacity data with the team, ask for options before deciding, and look for solutions the spreadsheet did not show: a shorter evening block, a targeted evening promotion, a gradual transition with notice, or schedule swaps that protect the two therapists' childcare. Whatever she decides, she should explain it face to face, name the trade-off honestly, and describe how she will review it.

A useful test before any announcement is to imagine explaining the decision to the person most affected by it, in person, with the data on the table. If you would be uncomfortable doing that, either the decision or the process needs more work. Leaders who pass this test consistently build a reserve of trust that carries them through the next hard call, because people have learned that the process is fair even when the outcome hurts.

Putting it together

Ethical leadership asks you to be a moral person and a visible moral manager. Organizational justice tells you that process and explanation matter as much as the outcome. Data make the decision better informed, and purpose keeps it pointed at the reason the organization exists. Your memo this week puts all four to work on a real resource decision.

Applied memo 6: a data-informed resource decision

Scenario. Write a memo to your leadership team or owners recommending a staffing, pricing or investment decision, informed by your work in the Practice Economics Lab.

Your memo should:

  1. State the decision and summarize the relevant data, including your lab results.
  2. Present at least two options and the trade-offs of each, for the business, the staff and the people you serve.
  3. Analyze the ethical tensions using ethical leadership and the three forms of organizational justice.
  4. Recommend one option and explain why it best balances data, people and purpose.
  5. Set out your communication plan: who hears what, when, how and from whom, and how the decision will be reviewed.

Format. 600 to 900 words in memo format (To, From, Date, Subject), plus a short table or chart from the lab, with at least two course readings cited in APA style. You may instead submit a recorded five-minute briefing with a one-page outline; the same rubric applies. Due Sunday at 11:59 pm. 100 points.
