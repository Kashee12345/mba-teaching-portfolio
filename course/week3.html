Week 3: Motivation, Engagement and the Science of Flourishing

Why do capable people disengage, and what brings them back? This week we look at four ideas that answer that question from different angles, and we test each one against a real workplace.

Overview

Last week you looked at what people bring to work: personality, perception and character strengths. This week asks what the work and the leader bring to people. Motivation is not a fixed trait someone has or lacks. It rises and falls with the conditions around a person, and many of those conditions sit squarely inside a leader's control.

We will cover Herzberg's two-factor theory, self-determination theory, Seligman's PERMA model of wellbeing, and strengths-based feedback. Vroom's expectancy theory appears in the readings as a useful companion. By Sunday you will write a short memo diagnosing engagement on a real team and recommending what to do about it.

Start with a puzzle

Picture a physical therapy clinic that just had a good year. The owner gave everyone a raise, bought new treatment tables and repainted the break room. Six months later two of the best therapists have resigned, and the ones who stayed seem tired and flat. The owner is baffled. "I did everything they asked for," she says.

She probably did. The trouble is that what people ask for when they are unhappy is not always what makes them want to give their best. This week's four ideas explain why, and each one gives a leader something practical to do on Monday morning.

1. Herzberg: two different kinds of factors

In the late 1950s Frederick Herzberg and his colleagues interviewed engineers and accountants about times they had felt especially good and especially bad about their jobs. The stories fell into two groups that barely overlapped. When people described feeling bad, they talked about the context of the job: company policy, the quality of supervision, relationships with coworkers, working conditions, pay and security. When they described feeling good, they talked about the content of the job: achievement, recognition, the work itself, responsibility, and chances to advance and grow.

Herzberg called the first group hygiene factors and the second group motivators. His central claim was that these are two separate scales, not two ends of one scale. Improving hygiene factors can remove dissatisfaction, but it does not create satisfaction. The opposite of dissatisfaction is simply no dissatisfaction. To create genuine satisfaction and effort you have to work on the motivators.

Go back to the clinic. The raise, the tables and the paint were all hygiene factors. They were worth doing, because poor hygiene really does drive people out. But nothing changed about the work itself. The therapists still saw back-to-back patients on a fixed template, had no say in which cases they took, and heard from the owner mainly when a note was late. The motivators were thin, so the improvements produced relief rather than energy.

Herzberg's research has fair critics. People tend to credit themselves for good events and blame their surroundings for bad ones, which could create the two-group pattern on its own. Pay also resists neat sorting, since a raise can feel like recognition as well as compensation. Still, the practical lesson has held up well in management practice: do not expect to buy engagement with hygiene alone.

A practical way to use the model is to run two separate audits. First ask, "What here is actively annoying or unfair?" That list might include a broken scheduling app, unclear on-call rules or pay that lags the local market. Fix what you can and explain what you cannot, because neglected hygiene will drown out everything else. Then ask a different question: "Where in this job does a person get to achieve something, be recognized for it, and grow?" If the honest answer is "not many places," you have found your real engagement work.

2. Self-determination theory: three needs every person carries

Psychologists Edward Deci and Richard Ryan spent decades studying why people do things and how the quality of motivation affects performance and wellbeing. Their self-determination theory makes a helpful distinction. The question is not only how much motivation someone has but what kind. Controlled motivation comes from pressure, rewards or guilt. Autonomous motivation comes from genuine interest or from values a person has made their own. Autonomous motivation tends to produce more persistence, creativity and wellbeing.

According to the theory, autonomous motivation grows when three basic psychological needs are met:

Autonomy is the sense that you are the author of your actions and have a real voice in how you work. It is not the same as working alone. A group fitness instructor who helps design the class format has autonomy, even though she works inside the studio's brand standards.

Competence is the sense of being effective and growing in skill. A new front-desk coordinator at a wellness center who gets clear training, early wins and useful feedback feels competent. One who is thrown into a scheduling system with no support feels the opposite, however bright he is.

Relatedness is the sense of being connected to and cared about by others. A massage therapist who works mostly alone in a treatment room can feel isolated unless the leader builds in time for connection, such as a short team huddle or peer case reviews.

Self-determination theory also warns leaders about a common trap. When you attach tight controls and contingent rewards to work people already find meaningful, you can shift their motivation from autonomous to controlled. A personal trainer who loves helping clients reach goals may start to see sessions as sales quotas if every conversation with the manager is about package renewals. The pay incentive is not wrong in itself, but how it is framed and how much it dominates the conversation matter a great deal.

Notice how well this fits with Herzberg. Autonomy, competence and relatedness live mostly on the motivator side of his model. Self-determination theory explains why those motivators work: they satisfy needs that are basic to people everywhere.

3. PERMA: a fuller picture of flourishing

Martin Seligman, often called a founder of positive psychology, argued that wellbeing is not one thing and cannot be captured by asking only whether people feel happy. In his model, flourishing rests on five elements, each worth pursuing for its own sake and each measurable on its own.

  • P: Positive emotionJoy, gratitude, interest, hope and calm.
  • E: EngagementAbsorption in a task, sometimes called flow, often when strengths meet a real challenge.
  • R: RelationshipsSupportive, trusting connections with others.
  • M: MeaningBelonging to and serving something bigger than yourself.
  • A: AccomplishmentPursuing and reaching goals, and building mastery.

For leaders, PERMA works as a diagnostic lens. Take a corporate wellness team that runs health coaching for a large employer. Meaning is high, because they can see lives changing. Relationships are strong. But accomplishment is weak, because the only metric anyone sees is a participation count, and nobody hears whether coaching goals were reached. Engagement suffers too, because the coaches spend half the day on data entry. A leader who sees this can act precisely: share outcome stories and results, and protect blocks of time for the coaching that absorbs people.

You do not need a formal survey to start. In a one-on-one, ask a few plain questions that map onto the five elements: What part of your week gives you energy? When did you last lose track of time in your work? Who here do you rely on? What about this job feels worth doing? What have you finished recently that you are proud of? The answers tell you where to invest.

One caution. PERMA describes wellbeing; it is not a checklist for making people cheerful. Forced positivity, such as mandatory fun or ignoring real problems, tends to backfire. The aim is conditions in which people can flourish, not a performance of flourishing.

4. Strengths-based feedback

Most feedback in organizations focuses on gaps. That is understandable, because problems demand attention. But research from Gallup, the work on character strengths by Christopher Peterson and Martin Seligman, and writers such as Marcus Buckingham all point in a similar direction: people grow most in the areas where they already have some natural talent, and they tend to engage more when leaders notice and build on what they do well.

Buckingham and Ashley Goodall add a pointed argument. Our ratings of other people are unreliable, often telling us more about the rater than the person being rated, and feedback that dwells on faults tends to trigger defensiveness rather than learning. What helps more is catching people at their best and telling them specifically what you saw and why it mattered.

Compare two comments to a chiropractic assistant. The first: "Good job today." The second: "When the patient in room two got anxious about her x-rays, you slowed down, explained each step and she relaxed. That kind of calm explanation is a real strength of yours. It is exactly why our new-patient reviews mention feeling cared for." The second comment is specific, tied to an outcome, and tells the person what to do more of. It also supports competence and relatedness, and it is a motivator in Herzberg's sense, because it is recognition for achievement.

Strengths-based does not mean avoiding hard conversations. Clear expectations and honest attention to problems still matter, and a strength overused can become a weakness. The shift is one of proportion and starting point: begin with what is working, and use it as the path into what needs to change.

Putting it together

These four ideas form a useful sequence. Herzberg tells you to check the basics, then to look past them to the work itself. Self-determination theory tells you which features of the work matter most: autonomy, competence and relatedness. PERMA widens the view to the whole person and the whole team. Strengths-based feedback is a daily leadership habit that feeds all of the above.

Back at the physical therapy clinic, a better plan might look like this: keep the raise, then give therapists a voice in scheduling templates, create a monthly case conference where they teach each other, share patient outcome data so they can see their accomplishments, and have the owner give specific, strengths-based feedback every week. None of that is expensive. All of it is leadership.

As you move into the discussion and the memo, look at your own workplace through each lens in turn. Where is the hygiene weak? Which of the three needs is least supported? Which PERMA element is missing? And when did you last tell someone, specifically, what they did well?

Applied memo 2: engagement diagnosis

Scenario. Write a memo to a leader (real, or a realistic composite) about a team whose engagement is lower than it should be. This could be your own team, a department you work with, or a client organization.

Your memo should:

  1. Briefly describe the team and the signs of low engagement, such as turnover, absenteeism, complaints, quality slips or low energy.
  2. Diagnose the causes using Herzberg and self-determination theory. Separate hygiene problems from motivator problems, and name which of the three needs is least supported.
  3. Use PERMA to identify which elements of wellbeing are strong and which are weak.
  4. Recommend two interventions, at least one of which uses strengths-based feedback. Explain why the theory predicts each will work.
  5. Propose how you will measure whether the interventions succeeded within 60 to 90 days.

Format. 600 to 900 words in memo format (To, From, Date, Subject), with at least two course readings cited in APA style. You may instead submit a recorded five-minute briefing with a one-page outline; the same rubric applies. Due Sunday at 11:59 pm. 100 points.
