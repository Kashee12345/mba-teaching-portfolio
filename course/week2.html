Overview

Last week you looked at OB's levels of analysis and the positive lens. This week we zoom in on the individual level. People differ in stable ways, and the way leaders perceive those differences shapes hiring, feedback, promotion and trust.

We will cover the Big Five personality traits and what they do and do not predict, the attribution errors that distort performance conversations, emotional intelligence and the debate about its evidence, and character strengths as a shared language for what is best in people. Your activity is a short "reflected best self" request to two colleagues, and your first applied memo asks you to spot and correct an attribution error.

Start with a performance review

At Harbor Point Physical Therapy, a fictional three-clinic group, the regional manager is reviewing a young therapist named Dana whose patient notes are often late. "She is just disorganized," the manager writes. Two weeks later a colleague mentions that Dana carries the heaviest caseload in the group, covers the only clinic without a front-desk assistant, and has been doing her own scheduling. The notes are late for reasons that have little to do with Dana's character.

The manager is not a careless person. She made one of the most common errors in human judgment. This week explains why it happens, and it also gives you better tools for seeing what people actually bring to work.

1. Personality: the Big Five

After decades of research, most personality psychologists agree that five broad traits capture a great deal of how people differ. They are often remembered by the acronym OCEAN:

  • OpennessCuriosity, imagination and comfort with new ideas.
  • ConscientiousnessDependability, organization and persistence toward goals.
  • ExtraversionSociability, assertiveness and energy from others.
  • AgreeablenessCooperation, warmth and trust.
  • NeuroticismTendency toward anxiety and negative emotion; its opposite is emotional stability.

What do the traits predict? A widely cited meta-analysis by Murray Barrick and Michael Mount found that conscientiousness was the most consistent predictor of job performance across occupations. Other traits matter in particular jobs: extraversion helps in sales and management roles, and emotional stability helps under pressure. The relationships are real but modest. Personality is one influence on performance among many, and the situation often matters as much as the person.

That last point is the practical lesson. A highly extraverted trainer may shine in group classes and struggle in quiet one-on-one rehabilitation work. Fit between person and role is often a better question than whether someone has a "good" personality.

2. Perception and attribution

When we see someone's behavior, we automatically explain it. Attribution theory studies those explanations. Harold Kelley proposed that we judge whether a behavior reflects the person (an internal cause) or the situation (an external cause) by using three cues, whether we notice it or not.

Consensus asks whether other people behave the same way in the same situation. Distinctiveness asks whether this person behaves this way only in this situation or everywhere. Consistency asks whether the person behaves this way every time. If every therapist at the understaffed clinic has late notes (high consensus) and Dana's notes were on time at her previous clinic (high distinctiveness), the cues point to the situation, not to Dana.

The trouble is that we rarely check the cues. Lee Ross named the fundamental attribution error: when judging others, we tend to overestimate personal causes and underestimate situational ones. Its mirror image is the self-serving bias: we credit our own successes to our ability and blame our failures on circumstances. Put the two together in a performance conversation and you get a manager who blames the employee and an employee who blames the system, both certain they are right.

A simple habit helps. Before you decide why someone is struggling, ask yourself three questions that map onto Kelley's cues: Is anyone else having the same problem? Does this person have this problem elsewhere? Has it always been this way? The answers often redirect your attention to workload, tools or training.

3. Emotional intelligence and its limits

Psychologists Peter Salovey and John Mayer introduced emotional intelligence as the ability to perceive, understand, use and manage emotions. Daniel Goleman's popular books expanded the idea to include a broad set of competencies such as self-awareness, self-regulation, empathy and social skill, and made bold claims about its importance for leadership.

The evidence is more mixed than the popular claims. Ability-based measures of emotional intelligence do predict some outcomes, especially in jobs with heavy emotional demands such as patient care or client coaching. But critics point out that some broad models overlap heavily with personality traits and general mental ability, and that once those are accounted for, the added predictive power is smaller than often advertised. The fair conclusion is that the skills involved are genuinely useful and can be developed, while sweeping claims deserve skepticism. This is evidence-based management in action.

4. Character strengths: a language for what is best

Christopher Peterson and Martin Seligman led a project to classify the positive traits valued across cultures and traditions. The result was 24 character strengths grouped under six virtues: wisdom, courage, humanity, justice, temperance and transcendence. Examples include curiosity, perseverance, kindness, fairness, self-regulation and gratitude.

Unlike the Big Five, which describes where you fall on each trait, character strengths focus on what is best in you. Everyone has all 24 in some measure, and each person has a handful of "signature strengths" that feel most natural and energizing. Research suggests that using signature strengths in new ways is linked to greater wellbeing, and that people who use their strengths at work tend to report more engagement.

Your self-view is only one data source, however. The "reflected best self" exercise, developed by researchers including Laura Morgan Roberts and colleagues, asks people who know you to describe times you were at your best. Comparing their stories with your assessment results often reveals strengths you undervalue because they come so easily.

Putting it together

Back at Harbor Point, a better review would start by checking the cues, then look at fit between Dana's strengths and her role. Perhaps her signature strengths are kindness and perseverance, which explains why patients love her and why she keeps going under a caseload nobody else would accept. The fix is support and staffing, not a lecture on organization.

Discussion: how others see you at your best

Prompt. Before posting, send a short "reflected best self" request to two colleagues, friends or clients: ask each to describe one time they saw you at your best. Compare their answers with your character strengths results from week 1. Which of your signature strengths showed up in their stories? Did anything surprise you? Then describe one situation where you think a colleague of yours was misjudged at work, and use attribution theory to explain what happened.

Replies. Respond to at least two classmates. In one reply, point out a strength in their colleagues' stories that they did not mention. In the other, apply Kelley's three cues to the misjudged colleague they described and say whether the cues support their explanation.

Applied memo 1: correcting an attribution error

Scenario. Write a memo to a leader (real, or a realistic composite) about an attribution error you have seen in a performance conversation, evaluation or hiring decision. Remove names and identifying details.

Your memo should:

  1. Describe the situation and the judgment that was made about the person.
  2. Explain the error using attribution theory: name whether it was the fundamental attribution error, self-serving bias or both, and apply Kelley's consensus, distinctiveness and consistency cues.
  3. Consider what personality or character strengths the person may have been bringing that the judgment missed.
  4. Recommend a corrected approach to the conversation, including specific questions the leader should ask.
  5. Propose one change to a process (such as reviews, onboarding or scheduling) that would make this error less likely in future.

Format. 600 to 900 words in memo format (To, From, Date, Subject), with at least two course readings cited in APA style. You may instead submit a recorded five-minute briefing with a one-page outline; the same rubric applies. Due Sunday at 11:59 pm. 100 points.
